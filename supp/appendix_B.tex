\section{Separate Sexes and the Mating System Scalar ($\kappa$)}\label{app:matingapp}

Appendix A treats every individual as both a carrier of fitness genes and a parent whose own breeding value sets its family size. With separate sexes and pair bonds this fails in two ways: a parent's family size can depend on its mate's breeding value, and the two sexes must be combined into a single effective size. Neither requires new theory. We use the expected long-term genetic contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva (1999), which regress an ancestor's lasting gene flow on its selective advantages separately by sex, and the two-sex combination of Santiago and Caballero (1995). What follows applies that machinery to the mating systems in our data.

\paragraph{Setup and the meaning of $V_A$.} The population is diploid with discrete generations, constant size $N$ and an equal sex ratio. Each individual carries an additive effect $a$ on relative lifetime breeding success, and $\mathrm{Var}(a) = V_A$ in both sexes. This appendix works to first order, where $a = z - \bar{z}$, the deviation of the breeding value for log-fitness of Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}; the two symbols denote the same quantity at this order. This is the quantity Bonnet et al. (2022) estimate: one animal model per population, both sexes in the data, sex fitted as a fixed effect, and a single additive variance on the observed scale. Social partners pair at random with respect to $a$.

\paragraph{The cumulative advantage of a gene copy.} Follow a neutral gene copy carried by an individual $X$. Its expected number of transmitted copies is $1 + u_X$, where $u_X$ is the relative selective advantage of $X$. Its carriers in the next generation have expected breeding value $h_1$, and from then on that expectation halves each generation through Mendelian segregation with random mates (Appendix A). The cumulative expected advantage of the copy is
\begin{equation}
    L_X = u_X + \sum_{t \ge 1} \bar{m}\, h_t = u_X + 2\bar{m}\, h_1,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{m}$ is the sex-averaged coefficient with which a carrier's own breeding value enters its selective advantage. $1 + L_X$ is, to first order, the expected long-term contribution $R$ of $X$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:A_R}), so $L_X$ is its excess over the mean; it is the regression of that contribution on the selective advantages of $X$ (Woolliams et al. 1999, Equation 6b). Under the assumption $Z = 1$ of Appendix A, the variance contributed by selection to the drift of the copy's frequency is $\mathrm{Var}(L_X)\,p(1-p)/(2N)$. Santiago and Caballero (1995) combine the sexes through $1/N_e = 1/(4N_{e,m}) + 1/(4N_{e,f})$ (after Crow and Denniston 1988). With equal sex ratio this is a mean over the two sexes, not a sum:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{Var}(L_f) + \mathrm{Var}(L_m)\right]} \equiv \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa V_A}. \label{eq:kappa_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{No pair bond: $\kappa = 1$.} In polygynous and polygynandrous systems each parent's family size depends on its own breeding value alone, $u = a$ in both sexes. A carrier's offspring have expected breeding value $h_1 = a/2$, since mates are random, and $\bar{m} = 1$. So $L = a + 2 \cdot a/2 = 2a$ in both sexes, $\mathrm{Var}(L) = 4V_A$, and $\kappa = 1$. This is Appendix A unchanged: with both sexes expressing the same $V_A$, separating them changes nothing. It is also the $\beta_q = 2\alpha_q$ solution of Woolliams et al. (1999) for two categories with equal selection.

\paragraph{Social monogamy with extra-pair paternity.} In a socially monogamous system the unit of productivity is the nest, and both social partners contribute to it: a female's relative offspring number is $u_f = a_f + a_{m'}$, where $a_{m'}$ is her social mate's breeding value. Extra-pair paternity at rate $\alpha$ moves paternity but not productivity. A male sires a fraction $1-\alpha$ of his own nest's offspring and gains extra-pair offspring elsewhere,
\begin{equation}
    u_m = (1-\alpha)(a_m + a_{f'}) + \alpha\gamma\, a_m,
\end{equation}
where $a_{f'}$ is his social mate's breeding value and $\gamma$ is the dependence of extra-pair success on his own breeding value. Model A sets $\gamma = 0$ (extra-pair siring is a lottery). Model B sets $\gamma = 1$, so that extra-pair gains replace exactly the within-pair loss. Model B is the only version in which a male's breeding value enters his own breeding success with the same weight as a female's at every $\alpha$, as Bonnet et al.'s single-variance model assumes.

A carrier's own breeding value then enters its advantage with weight 1 in females and $m = 1 - \alpha + \alpha\gamma$ in males, so $\bar{m} = (1+m)/2$. Offspring of a female carrier are sired by her social mate with probability $1-\alpha$, and offspring of a male carrier come from his social mate with weight $1-\alpha$. To first order, therefore,
\begin{equation}
    h_1^{(f)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[a_f + (1-\alpha)a_{m'}\right], \qquad h_1^{(m)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[a_m + (1-\alpha)a_{f'}\right].
\end{equation}
Substituting,
\begin{align}
    L_f &= (1+\bar{m})\,a_f + \left[1 + \bar{m}(1-\alpha)\right] a_{m'}, \\
    L_m &= (m+\bar{m})\,a_m + (1+\bar{m})(1-\alpha)\,a_{f'},
\end{align}
and, with partners' breeding values independent ($r_{mf} = 0$), Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa_def} gives
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(\alpha) = \frac{(1+\bar{m})^2\left[1+(1-\alpha)^2\right] + \left[1+\bar{m}(1-\alpha)\right]^2 + (m+\bar{m})^2}{8}.
\end{equation}
For the two models:
\begin{align}
    \text{Model A } (\gamma = 0):&\quad \kappa_A(\alpha) = \frac{(4-\alpha)^2\left[1+(1-\alpha)^2\right] + (4-3\alpha+\alpha^2)^2 + (4-3\alpha)^2}{32}, \\
    \text{Model B } (\gamma = 1):&\quad \kappa_B(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Limits and what is not new.} Under strict monogamy ($\alpha = 0$) both models give $\kappa = 2$. This is the base model of Santiago and Caballero (1995) itself: $N/2$ pair-mated families sharing a family fitness whose variance is $C^2 = \mathrm{Var}(a_f + a_{m'}) = 2V_A$, so that $N_e/N = 1/(1 + Q^2 C^2) = 1/(1 + 8V_A)$. The factor of 2 arises because each nest pools two independent breeding values. It is not a second application of the same variance. At all orders the same limit is the $e^{-8V_A}$ that Weissman and Barton (2012) found for random pairs under multiplicative fitness (Equation~\eqref{eq:A_mono}), and without a pair bond the limit is their and Barton and Etheridge's (2011) $e^{-4V_A}$. What this appendix adds is the interpolation between those two published limits for partial extra-pair paternity, with separate sexes and nest sharing, at first order and, in the next paragraph, at all orders. As $\alpha$ rises, $\kappa_B$ falls monotonically to $9/8$ at $\alpha = 1$; it stays above 1 because the social male still contributes to nest productivity. $\kappa_A$ falls to $7/16$, because males then gain nothing from their own breeding value.

\paragraph{All orders under multiplicative fitness.} Under Model B with $w \propto e^{z}$, a copy's long-term contribution mixes two routes. Write $\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2)$ for the deviations from the mean of the breeding values for log-fitness of the carrier and of its social partner, independent with variance $V_A$. A copy in a female is in a nest of productivity $e^{z_f + z_{m'}}$. A fraction $1 - \alpha$ of her offspring are sired by $m'$ and a fraction $\alpha$ by extra-pair males. A copy in a male gains within-pair offspring from his own nest and extra-pair offspring elsewhere, in proportion to $e^{z_m}$. From the next generation on, a carrier with breeding value $y$ contributes in proportion to $e^{2y}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:A_R}), so
\begin{equation}
    R_f \propto (1-\alpha)\,e^{2u_1 + 2u_2} + \alpha c_f\,e^{2u_1 + u_2}, \qquad
    R_m \propto (1-\alpha)\,e^{2u_1 + 2u_2} + \alpha c_m\,e^{2u_1}.
\end{equation}
The constants $c_f$ and $c_m$ collect expectations over individuals outside the pair, the extra-pair sires and the nests in which a male sires extra-pair young, and equal one at $V_A = 0$. Each sex's contribution has the form $R = \sum_k c_k e^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\cdot\mathbf{u}}$, with exponent vectors $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (2,2), (2,1)$ in females and $(2,2), (2,0)$ in males, and $c_1 = 1 - \alpha$, $c_2 = \alpha c_s$. Gaussian moments, $\mathbb{E}[e^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{u}}] = e^{V_A|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^2/2}$, give
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}{\mathbb{E}[R]^2} = \sum_{k,l} w_k w_l\, e^{V_A\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}_l}, \qquad
    w_k = \frac{c_k\, e^{V_A|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k|^2/2}}{\sum_l c_l\, e^{V_A|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_l|^2/2}}, \label{eq:B_moments}
\end{equation}
where $w_k$ is the share of the copy's expected long-term contribution that passes through route $k$. $N/N_e$ is the mean of Equation~\eqref{eq:B_moments} over the sexes (Equation~\eqref{eq:A_ratio}). Every product $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}_l$ is at most 8, so the ratio is at most $e^{8V_A}$ in either sex, whatever the $c_k$. In the other direction, Jensen's inequality bounds the ratio below by $\exp(V_A|\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_s|^2)$, with $\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_s = \sum_k w_k\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k$, and the mean of two exponentials is at least the exponential of their mean. At $w = (1-\alpha, \alpha)$, $\tfrac12(|\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_f|^2 + |\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_m|^2) = 8 - 6\alpha + \tfrac52\alpha^2 = 4\kappa_B$, and $|\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_s|^2$ increases with the within-pair share. So whenever the extra-pair route carries no more than its share $\alpha$ of a copy's expected contribution,
\begin{equation}
    e^{-8V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa_B V_A}. \label{eq:B_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
The extra-pair share is exactly $\alpha$ when extra-pair siring success is proportional to $e^{z}$, which gives $c_f = e^{3V_A/2}$ and $c_m = e^{2V_A}$. It is smaller when the extra-pair sire's breeding value is held at the mean, $c_f = c_m = 1$, the case computed in the main text. Numerically, the bracket also holds for $c_f$ and $c_m$ up to $e^{2V_A}$. Both ends are equalities at $\alpha = 0$, where $\kappa_B = 2$ and Equation~\eqref{eq:B_mult_pair} closes on Weissman and Barton's $e^{-8V_A}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:A_mono}). The right-hand end is the first-order result $1/(1 + 4\kappa_B V_A)$ to first order. As $V_A$ grows the exact value moves from the right-hand end toward the left, because the within-pair route, with the largest exponent, comes to dominate the second moment.

\paragraph{General form: $Z$ and $r_{mf}$.} The results above set $Z = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$, the two assumptions of Appendix A, and both carry into the mating-system scalar. Let social partners' breeding values correlate $r_{mf}$, and let each generation of selection retain a fraction $Z$ of the association (the retention of Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}) between a gene copy and its background. A carrier with expected breeding value $h$ then has expected advantage $\bar{c}\,h$, with $\bar{c} = (1+m)/2 + r_{mf}(2-\alpha)/2$. Its carrier offspring have expected breeding value $\theta h$, with $\theta = (Z/2)\left[1 + (1-\alpha)r_{mf}\right]$. Summing the geometric series,
\begin{align}
    L_f &= (1+\Psi)\,a_f + \left[1 + \Psi(1-\alpha)\right] a_{m'}, \\
    L_m &= (m+\Psi)\,a_m + (1+\Psi)(1-\alpha)\,a_{f'}, \qquad \Psi = \frac{\bar{c}\,Z/2}{1-\theta},
\end{align}
and a weighted sum of two partners' breeding values, with weights $\xi_1$ and $\xi_2$, has variance $V_A(\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2 + 2r_{mf}\xi_1\xi_2)$. $\Psi = \bar{m}$ at $Z = 1$, $r_{mf} = 0$, which recovers the expressions above. Two limits reproduce Santiago and Caballero exactly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strict monogamy, $\alpha = 0$: $1 + \Psi = 2/[2 - Z(1+r_{mf})] = Q$ and $N_e/N = 1/(1 + Q^2C^2)$ with $C^2 = 2V_A(1+r_{mf})$, their pair-family model.
    \item No pair bond: $\kappa = Q^2/4$.
\end{itemize}
$Z$ is evaluated sex by sex and averaged, $Z = (Z_m + Z_f)/2$ (Santiago and Caballero 1995). Under the multiplicative model Equation~\eqref{eq:Zcgf} gives $Z = 1$ in both sexes and every mating system for a Gaussian, infinitesimal breeding value, and $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ per effect class for deleterious alleles at mutation--selection balance (Equation~\eqref{eq:Zclass}). Under an additive map Equation~\eqref{eq:Zarch} would apply, with $b$ the weight of a parent's own breeding value in its family size (1 in females and in non-monogamous males, $m$ in socially monogamous males), and would give $Z = 1 - V_A$ under Model B; that map implies negative fitness as $V_A$ grows and is not used (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}).

$\kappa$ and $r_{mf}$ are different quantities. $r_{mf}$ measures assortative pairing on breeding value and lengthens the persistence of an association. $\kappa$ arises from nest sharing between partners, which in Santiago and Caballero's framework enters through the family-level $C^2$. The mating system therefore enters through three measurable features: whether a pair bond shares productivity, the extra-pair rate $\alpha$, and assortment $r_{mf}$. We set $r_{mf} = 0$ for want of estimates; positive assortment would raise $\kappa$.

\paragraph{Own, partner and linked components.} The linked term of the main text needs $\kappa$ taken apart, because only some of the variance it counts can be linked to a focal gene copy. With $r_{mf} = 0$, $\mathrm{Var}(L_f) + \mathrm{Var}(L_m)$ separates into the carrier's own breeding value and its social partner's:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    \kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}, \\
    \kappa_\text{own} = \frac{(1+\Psi)^2 + (m+\Psi)^2}{8}, \qquad
    \kappa_\text{partner} = \frac{\left[1+\Psi(1-\alpha)\right]^2 + (1-\alpha)^2(1+\Psi)^2}{8}.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:kappa_split}
\end{equation}
The partner's genes are never on the focal copy's haplotype, so the partner component is wholly unlinked. Of the own component, the share on the focal haplotype ($v_h$) moves to the linked term. There a selected locus whose association survives a generation with probability $\rho = (1-r)Z$ contributes $m_X + \bar{m}\,\rho/(1-\rho)$ to the copy's cumulative advantage, where $m_X$ is the carrier's own weight (1 in females, $m$ in males) and later carriers average $\bar{m}$. Relative to the unweighted $1/(1-\rho)$, this gives
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_\text{cis}(\rho) = \frac{\left[1 + \bar{m}\chi\right]^2 + \left[m + \bar{m}\chi\right]^2}{2(1+\chi)^2}, \qquad \chi = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho},
\end{equation}
which falls from $(1+m^2)/2$ as $\rho \to 0$ to $\bar{m}^2$ under tight linkage. Without a pair bond, and under Model B, $m = 1$, so $\kappa_\text{own} = \kappa_\text{cis} = 1$ and $\kappa_\text{partner} = \kappa - 1$. The chromosome-level reduction of the main text (Equation~\eqref{total_red}) uses this split: $\kappa_\text{cis}$ on the linked term, and $Q^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)$ in the exponent of the unlinked background, with $Q = 2/(2-Z)$ at the same retention as the linked term. The same split carries over to the multiplicative model, where the mating system also enters the exponent of the unlinked reduction (Equation~\eqref{eq:B_mult_pair}).

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The derivation is first order in $a$ (a small selective advantage per lineage, $V_A \ll 1$), sets $Z = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$ in the primary results, assumes an equal sex ratio, and lets the social male's breeding value contribute to all of his nest's productivity whether or not he sires the offspring. Combining Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa_def} with Appendix A yields the mating-system-corrected unlinked expectation,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa V_A},
\end{equation}
which is Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} of the main text.
