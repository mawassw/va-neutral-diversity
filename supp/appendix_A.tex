\section{Appendix A: The Unlinked Reduction under One Model of Fitness}\label{app:unlinkedapp}
This appendix derives the reduction in effective size caused by heritable fitness variance at sites unlinked to any selected locus. Section A.1 gives the first-order result of Robertson (1961) and Santiago and Caballero (1995). Section A.2 connects it to the estimate of Bonnet et al. (2022), whose animal model is multiplicative. Section A.3 extends the reduction to all orders under that model, and Section A.4 treats $G$, the fraction of variance that survives selection.

\subsection*{A.1\quad First order}
In a neutral Wright-Fisher population with a constant diploid census size $N$, the variance in allele frequency change per generation due to random genetic drift for a neutral allele at frequency $p$ is the standard gametic sampling variance:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p^{\text{drift}}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}

When variance in reproductive success is heritable ($V_A > 0$), the neutral allele experiences selective interference from the genetic background on which it resides. In a given generation, a diploid population of $N$ individuals has relative fitnesses $w_i$, normalized so that $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i w_i = 1$, with variance $\text{Var}(w_i) = V_W$. Let $z_i \in \{0, \frac{1}{2}, 1\}$ be the frequency of the neutral allele within individual $i$, so that $p = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i z_i$. Weighting each individual's contribution by its relative fitness, the frequency in the next generation is
\begin{equation}
    p' \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i z_i = p + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w_i - 1)(z_i - p),
\end{equation}
where the second form uses $\sum_i (w_i - 1) = 0$. Because the neutral locus is unlinked to all selected sites, $w_i$ and $z_i$ are independent, so $\mathbb{E}[(w_i - 1)(z_i - p)] = 0$ and the expected change is zero. The variance in the allele frequency change is
\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p) = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{Var}\left[(w_i - 1)(z_i - p)\right] = \frac{V_W\,\text{Var}(z_i)}{N} = V_W\,\frac{p(1-p)}{2N}, \label{eq:A_dp_one}
\end{equation}
using $\text{Var}(z_i) = p(1-p)/2$ under Hardy--Weinberg proportions.

The neutral allele is not selected, yet its frequency changes. In each generation its copies are carried by individuals whose fitnesses are independent of the neutral genotype, and by chance the carriers' mean fitness differs slightly from the population mean which results in the allele's frequency moving in the direction of that difference. Equation~\eqref{eq:A_dp_one} gives the variance of this change: the variance in fitness, times how unevenly the allele is spread among individuals, divided by the $N$ individuals over which the chance difference is averaged. The factor $p(1-p)$ is the heterozygosity at the neutral locus. An allele that is fixed or absent is carried equally by every individual and cannot be moved by differences among them, so, as with genetic drift, the effect is largest at intermediate frequency and vanishes without variation at the neutral site.

However, what distinguishes this change from genetic drift is that part of it can persist across the generations. Write $V_W = V_A + V_R$, where $V_R$ collects the environmental and non-additive components of fitness variance. The $V_R$ component is not transmitted: it moves the allele once, as additional sampling variance in the generation it occurs, and is absorbed into the demographic background, here assumed to be Poisson. The additive component is transmitted, because the carriers' offspring inherit, on average, half of their parents' breeding values along with the neutral allele. The heritable part of the one-generation change is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p^{\text{sel}}) = V_A\,\frac{p(1-p)}{2N}. \label{eq:A_dp_sel}
\end{equation}
This formulation (Robertson 1961) relies on a large-$N$ approximation, assumes Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium ($\text{Var}(z_i) = p(1-p)/2$), and isolates the additive genetic component $V_A$ from the total fitness variance, as contributions from dominance and epistasis do not transmit stably across generations to drive multi-generational allele frequency changes.

Because the neutral allele and the selected background are unlinked (segregating on non-homologous chromosomes), they assort independently during meiosis (i.e., $r = 0.5$). Consequently, the statistical association, or linkage disequilibrium, between the neutral allele and its initial selected background halves each generation $t$. The directional change in the neutral allele frequency driven by this specific selective cohort decays geometrically:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_t^{\text{sel}} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t \Delta p^{\text{sel}}
\end{equation}

Integrating this effect over time yields the total cumulative change in the neutral allele frequency resulting from a single generation of heritable variance:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_{\text{total}}^{\text{sel}} = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t \Delta p^{\text{sel}} = 2 \Delta p^{\text{sel}}
\end{equation}

This series is a special case. Santiago and Caballero (1995; reviewed in Wang et al. 2016) give the general cumulative factor
\begin{equation}
    Q = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \left[\frac{G(1+r_{mf})}{2}\right]^i = \frac{2}{2 - G(1+r_{mf})},
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the fraction of additive variance remaining among selected parents and $r_{mf}$ is the correlation between the expected selective values of male and female parents. (We write $r_{mf}$ to distinguish it from the recombination fraction $r$.) The special case above sets $G = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$. $G = 1$ holds exactly under the multiplicative model of Section A.3. $r_{mf} = 0$ requires that mates pair at random with respect to their breeding values for fitness; under random mating Santiago and Caballero give $r_{mf} = -1/(N-1)$. Assortative mating for fitness ($r_{mf} > 0$) would raise $Q$. We do not interest ourselves in that specific process and keep $r_{mf} = 0$.

Assuming $V_A$ remains stationary under mutation-selection-drift equilibrium, a new influx of selective variance of magnitude $V_A p(1-p)/(2N)$ is generated each generation. The long-run footprint of each generation's selective variation resolves to $2\Delta p^{\text{sel}}$.

The total selective variance in allele frequency change is the variance of this cumulative sum. Because the selective association persists across multiple generations, the variance of the cumulative effect scales with the square of the cumulative sum:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p_{\text{total}}^{\text{sel}}) = \text{Var}(2 \Delta p^{\text{sel}}) = 4 V_A \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}
This establishes that the multi-generational persistence of associations across independently assorting chromosomes amplifies the effective variance four-fold.

Because the neutral allele is physically unlinked from the causal selected loci, the variance generated by selective interference and the variance generated by neutral demographic sampling arise from independent biological processes. Their contributions to the total variance in allele frequency are therefore additive. Equating the standard definition of effective gametic sampling variance to the sum of these independent variances yields:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e} = \text{Var}(\Delta p^{\text{drift}}) + \text{Var}(\Delta p_{\text{total}}^{\text{sel}})
\end{equation}

Substituting the expanded terms:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e} = \frac{p(1-p)}{2N} + 4 V_A \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}

Dividing by the common neutral variance term $p(1-p)$ and multiplying by 2 yields the unlinked expectation for the effective population size:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{N_e} = \frac{1}{N} (1 + 4V_A) \Rightarrow N_e = \frac{N}{1 + 4V_A}
\end{equation}
The derivation is first order: each step treats a lineage's selective advantage as small, so it holds for any map from breeding value to fitness while $V_A \ll 1$. The extension to separate sexes and structured mating systems is derived in Appendix B, where $V_A$ is scaled by the mating-system parameter $\kappa(\alpha)$, yielding $N_e/N = 1/(1+4\kappa V_A)$.

\subsection*{A.2\quad The latent scale and the measured variance}
Bonnet et al. (2022) model lifetime breeding success with two latent predictors: a logit-linked probability of breeding ($l_1$) and a log-linked expected number of offspring given breeding ($l_2$). Breeding values are additive and Gaussian on these latent scales, with genetic covariance matrix $\mathbf{G}$. They report the observed-scale additive variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{d}$ is the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to $l_1$ and $l_2$. For a log link the average derivative equals the mean, which is one for relative fitness, so for the log-linked component the observed-scale and latent variances coincide (de Villemereuil et al. 2016, Equations 15--16). To first order, then, $V_A(w)$ is the variance of a single breeding value $z$ for log-fitness, $w \propto e^{z}$, and that is the model used in Section A.3.

The logit component is different in kind. It acts on a probability, which cannot exceed one, so its contribution to fitness is bounded and compounds more slowly along a lineage than an exponential. Treating the whole of $V_A(w)$ as log-additive therefore overstates the reduction at all orders beyond the first; at first order the split between components does not matter. The posterior draws available to us report only the combined $V_A(w)$.

\subsection*{A.3\quad All orders under multiplicative fitness}
Let parents be chosen with probability proportional to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian with variance $V_A$ (Barton and Etheridge 2011). An ancestor with log value $z$ has expected offspring in proportion to $e^{z}$, and its descendants inherit, on average, half of its deviation each generation. Barton and Etheridge (2011, Appendix B) solve the resulting recursion for the mean reproductive value,
\begin{equation}
    M(z) = \exp\left[2(z - \bar{z}) - 2V_A\right],
\end{equation}
so that the cumulative factor $Q = 2$ sits in the exponent. They note that the variance of reproductive value across ancestors is $e^{4V_A}$, and that the probability that an ancestor is involved in a coalescence goes as the square of its reproductive value. The rate of inbreeding is proportional to the second moment of long-term genetic contributions (Wray and Thompson 1990), which gives, without a pair bond,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[M]^2}{\mathbb{E}[M^2]} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:A_mult}
\end{equation}
Its expansion, $1 - 4V_A + O(V_A^2)$, is the first-order result of Section A.1.

\paragraph{Social monogamy.} With the nest-sharing model of Appendix B (Model B), a carrier's family size is proportional to $e^{h}$ for its log breeding value $h$ in either sex, so later generations compound exactly as above. Only the first generation mixes two kinds of offspring. A copy in a female with social partner $s$ has nest productivity $e^{a_f + a_s}$; a fraction $1 - \alpha$ of those offspring are sired by $s$, and $\alpha$ by extra-pair males. A copy in a male with social partner $f'$ gains within-pair offspring from his own nest and extra-pair offspring elsewhere. The long-term contributions are, up to constants,
\begin{equation}
    r_f \propto (1-\alpha)\,e^{2(a_f + a_s)} + \alpha\,e^{2a_f + a_s}, \qquad
    r_m \propto (1-\alpha)\,e^{2(a_m + a_{f'})} + \alpha\,e^{2a_m},
\end{equation}
and $N/N_e$ is the mean over the sexes of $\mathbb{E}[r^2]/\mathbb{E}[r]^2$. With independent Gaussian breeding values these moments are sums of lognormal moments, $\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{a}}] = e^{V_A|\mathbf{u}|^2/2}$. The exponent vectors satisfy $\mathbf{u}_k\cdot\mathbf{u}_l \le \max_k|\mathbf{u}_k|^2 = 8$, from which $\mathbb{E}[r^2]/\mathbb{E}[r]^2 \le e^{8V_A}$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    e^{-8V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa_B V_A}. \label{eq:A_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
Equality holds at both ends under strict monogamy ($\alpha = 0$, where $\kappa_B = 2$). The right-hand end matches the first-order result $1/(1 + 4\kappa_B V_A)$, with slope $4\kappa_B$ at $V_A = 0$. We confirmed numerically that the exact value never falls outside the bracket, including when the weight of the extra-pair term is distorted by factors between $e^{-2V_A}$ and $e^{2V_A}$, which allows for the selection of extra-pair sires and the normalization of the extra-pair pool. At small $V_A$ the exact value lies close to the right-hand end, and as $V_A$ grows it moves toward the left, because the within-nest term compounds fastest.

\paragraph{Validity.} Equations~\eqref{eq:A_mult} and \eqref{eq:A_mult_pair} rest on the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness. The second moment $\mathbb{E}[M^2] \propto \mathbb{E}[e^{4z}]$ is carried by ancestors near $z = 4V_A$, or $4\sqrt{V_A}$ standard deviations above the mean. A 100-fold reduction without a pair bond ($V_A = \ln(100)/4$) rests on ancestors 4.3 standard deviations out, about one in $10^5$, and a $10^4$-fold reduction on ancestors 6.1 standard deviations out. The expectation is realized only in populations large enough to contain such individuals, and only if nothing caps their fitness. Territories, maximum clutch sizes and the bounded probability of breeding (Section A.2) all truncate the tail and reduce the effect. These forms are therefore upper bounds on the reduction that a given $V_A$ produces in a real population. The derivation also assumes Poisson sampling of offspring beyond the heritable component, random pairing with respect to breeding value, and loci unlinked to the focal site.

\subsection*{A.4\quad The fraction of variance that survives selection}
Let a parent's expected family size be $w = 1 + b\,a$, linear in its breeding value $a$ with mean zero. The additive variance among parents, weighted by their offspring, is then exactly $V_A - b^2V_A^2 + b\,\mu_3$, where $\mu_3$ is the third central moment of $a$, so
\begin{equation}
    G = 1 - b^2 V_A + b\,\mu_3/V_A. \label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
Under the multiplicative model of Section A.3 selection weights a Gaussian breeding value by $e^{z}$. Exponential tilting of a normal distribution shifts its mean by $V_A$ and leaves its variance unchanged, so $G = 1$ exactly on the log scale, at every value of $V_A$.

\paragraph{The additive map, as a remark.} If instead fitness were additive and Gaussian on the relative scale ($b = 1$, $\mu_3 = 0$), Equation~\eqref{eq:G} would give $G = 1 - V_A$, $Q = 2/(1+V_A)$ (the same form as $Q(s)$ in Appendix D), and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \left[1 + \frac{4V_A}{(1+V_A)^2}\right]^{-1}, \label{eq:Gcorrected}
\end{equation}
which never falls below 1/2. This saturation is an artifact of the map. With $w = 1 + a$ and $a$ Gaussian, a share $\Phi(-1/\sqrt{V_A})$ of individuals has negative expected fitness: about 8\% at $V_A = 0.5$ and 16\% at $V_A = 1$. The map is therefore internally inconsistent in exactly the range where it departs from the first-order result. It agrees with Section A.1 to first order and is not used beyond it.
