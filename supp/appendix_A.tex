\section{Effective size under heritable variance in log-fitness}\label{app:unlinkedapp}
This appendix derives the unlinked reduction in effective size $N_e$ used in the main text, under the multiplicative fitness model (Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult}) and its small-variance approximation (Equation~\eqref{eq:Q}), and the retention factor $Z$ of \autoref{sec:msdb}. The model is that of Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011}. Start with a population with $N$ breeding adults. A parent's expected number of offspring is proportional to $e^{z}$, where $z$ is its breeding value for log-fitness. The breeding value is assumed to be additive in its effect and follows a Gaussian distribution with variance $V_A$. The realized family sizes are assumed to be Poisson about that expectation. Only additive effects are included, because dominance and epistatic deviations are not transmitted stably across generations. Mating is taken to be random where each offspring's other parent is drawn independently and at random with respect to $z$. The neutral site is assumed to be unlinked to every selected locus. The issue of pair bonds and extra-pair paternity are treated in Appendix~B.

\subsection{Long-term contributions and effective size}\label{app:A_R}
Follow a neutral gene copy carried by an ancestor with breeding value $z$. We follow the gene copy rather than the ancestor so that the same bookkeeping applies to the pair bond (Appendix~\ref{app:matingapp}) and to linked sites (Appendix~\ref{app:chromosapp}); at an unlinked site both give the ancestor's long-term contribution, because the probability that a gene survives is proportional to the long-term contribution of the individual that carries it~\cite{barton2011}. Its carriers in each later generation inherit on average half of the previous carriers' deviation from the mean, so their deviations are $(z - \bar{z})/2$, $(z - \bar{z})/4$, and so on. Family sizes multiply along the lineage, so they sum on a logarithmic scale to $(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots)(z - \bar{z}) = 2(z - \bar{z})$. Barton and Etheridge~\cite[Appendix~B]{barton2011} give the exact solution to this recursion. The expected long-term contribution of the ancestor, normalized so that $\mathbb{E}[R] = 1$, is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) = \exp\left[Q(z - \bar{z}) - \tfrac12 Q^2V_A\right], \qquad Q = 2. \label{eq:A_R}
\end{equation}
Two neutral copies sampled in a distant generation coalesce in the ancestral generation when both descend from the same ancestral copy. Copy $j$, carried by an ancestor with breeding value $z_j$, is the source of a fraction $p_j = R(z_j)/\sum_k R(z_k) = R(z_j)/(2N\,\mathbb{E}[R])$ of later copies, and both sampled copies trace to it with probability $p_j^2$~\cite{barton2011}. Summing over the $2N$ ancestral copies gives the rate of inbreeding~\cite{wray1990},
\begin{equation}
    \Delta F = \sum_{j=1}^{2N} p_j^2 = \frac{1}{2N}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}. \label{eq:A_dF}
\end{equation}
Without heritable variance every copy has the same contribution with $R = \mathbb{E}[R]$ and $\Delta F = 1/(2N)$, the Poisson baseline. Heritable variance replaces $\mathbb{E}[R]^2$ by $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$. Because $\Delta F$ is the probability that two lineages coalesce in one generation, setting $\Delta F = 1/(2N_e)$ defines the inbreeding effective size, which here is also the coalescent effective size $N_{eC}$ that $\pi$ measures. It is not the variance effective size of a single generation, which sees only that generation's family-size variance and so misses the persistence of the carriers' advantage along the lineage that produces the factor $Q^2$. With $N$ and $V_A$ constant, the long-term $N_{eV}$ converges to the same value, and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}. \label{eq:A_ratio}
\end{equation}
Because $\log R$ is Gaussian, the ratio simplifies to $\exp[-\mathrm{Var}(\log R)]$. Under random mating $\mathrm{Var}(\log R) = Q^2V_A = 4V_A$, so
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:A_mult}
\end{equation}
This corresponds to the $e^{4V_A}$ increase in the rate of coalescence that Weissman and Barton~\cite{weissman2012} give for heritable variance in log-fitness at unlinked loci. Under strict monogamy, pairs form at random and a pair with breeding values $z$ and $z'$ has offspring in proportion to $e^{z + z'}$~\cite{weissman2012}. The partner's deviation then contributes to the copy's lineage in the same way as the carrier's, so $\log R = 2(z - \bar{z}) + 2(z' - \bar{z}) + \text{const}$. Its variance is $8V_A$, and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-8V_A}. \label{eq:A_mono}
\end{equation}
Weissman and Barton~\cite{weissman2012} find the same factor, $e^{-8V_A}$, for the reduction in the fixation probability of a new allele under monogamy, and the two results coincide because both depend on the same variance. A single new copy with a small advantage $s$ fixes with probability about $2s$ divided by the variance in its number of descendants, which is $2s$ under the Poisson baseline. Heritable variance raises that variance by the factor $\mathbb{E}[R^2]/\mathbb{E}[R]^2$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:A_ratio}, so the fixation probability becomes $2s\,N_e/N$, Kimura's~\cite{kimura1962} result for a population whose effective size differs from its census size, lower by $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating and by $e^{-8V_A}$ under monogamy.

\subsection{The small-variance approximation}\label{app:A_first}
For small values of $V_A$, $R \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$, so $\mathbb{E}[R^2] = 1 + \mathrm{Var}(R) = 1 + Q^2V_A$, and Equation~\eqref{eq:A_ratio} gives the expansion of Equation~\eqref{eq:A_mult}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2V_A}. \label{eq:A_first}
\end{equation}
Robertson~\cite{robertson1961} and Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} (reviewed in Wang et al.~\cite{wang2016}) obtained this form for any map from breeding value to fitness, valid while $V_A \ll 1$. Their transmission factor is $Q = 2/[2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})]$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Q}). Here $Z$ is the retention factor (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}), and $r_{mf}$ is the correlation between mates' breeding values, which raises $Q$ when positive. At $Z = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$, $Q = 2$, and Equation~\eqref{eq:A_first} becomes $1/(1 + 4V_A)$. Under strict monogamy the same expansion of Equation~\eqref{eq:A_mono} gives $1/(1 + 8V_A)$. With the mating system included (Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating}), setting $N_e/N = \delta$ and inverting gives the small-variance ceiling
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\max} = \frac{1 - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta}, \label{eq:vamax}
\end{equation}
the variance above which heritable fitness differences alone would remove more diversity than is observed. It holds only where $V_{A,\max} \le 1$, that is $\delta \gtrsim 1/(1 + 4\kappa)$, in a population that retains at least about a fifth of its neutral expectation under random mating ($\kappa = 1$, $\delta \gtrsim 0.20$), $0.12$--$0.16$ in the socially monogamous populations ($\kappa_B = 1.32$--$1.86$), and a ninth under strict monogamy ($\kappa = 2$, $\delta \gtrsim 0.11$); for smaller $\delta$ the least variance that could account for the deficit is $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}).

\subsection{The upper tail of log-fitness}\label{app:A_tail}
Under random mating, $\mathbb{E}[R^2] \propto \mathbb{E}[e^{4z}]$. Weighting the normal density of $z$ by $e^{4z}$ shifts its mean to $\bar{z} + 4V_A$, so the ancestors that carry the reduction lie $4\sqrt{V_A}$ standard deviations above the mean. A 100-fold reduction ($V_A = \ln(100)/4 = 1.15$) rests on ancestors 4.3 standard deviations out, about one in $10^5$. A $10^4$-fold reduction ($V_A = 2.30$) rests on ancestors 6.1 standard deviations out, about one in $10^9$. The expectation is realized only in a population large enough to contain such ancestors, and only if nothing caps their fitness. Any ceiling on fitness truncates the weighted density and lowers $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$: limits on territory or clutch size, or the probability of breeding in Bonnet et al.'s~\cite{bonnet2022} zero-inflation component. That probability cannot exceed one, so its contribution to log-fitness is bounded above by zero (\autoref{sec:onemodel}). Equations~\eqref{eq:A_mult}, \eqref{eq:A_mono} and their extension to extra-pair paternity (Equation~\eqref{eq:B_mult_pair}) are therefore upper bounds on the reduction that a given $V_A$ produces.

\subsection{The retention factor \texorpdfstring{$Z$}{Z}}\label{app:A_Z}
In the ancestor-centered perspective, the retention factor $Z$ is the additive variance among parents weighted by their offspring, relative to $V_A$, because each parent passes on its breeding value in proportion to the offspring it leaves, so the weighted variance is the part of $V_A$ that survives selection into the next generation. In the gene-centered perspective, $Z$ is the factor by which one generation of selection multiplies a gene copy's association with its selective background (the $G$ of Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995}). A retention per generation presupposes that allele frequencies at the selected loci are stable while the association or the linkage disequilibrium persists~\cite{barton1998,barton2000}, which a mutation--selection--drift balance provides. Under multiplicative fitness, weighting parents by $e^{z}$ is an exponential tilt of the distribution of $z$. Write $K(\theta) = \ln \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta z}]$ for the cumulant-generating function of $z$. The tilted distribution has variance $K''(1)$, so
\begin{equation}
    Z = \frac{K''(1)}{K''(0)}. \label{eq:Zcgf}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian $z$, $K(\theta) = \theta\bar{z} + \theta^2V_A/2$, and $Z = 1$ at every value of $V_A$: selection shifts the mean of the selected parents by $V_A$ and leaves their variance unchanged. This is the case used for the genome-wide reduction in the effective size. More generally, a Taylor expansion of $K''$ about zero gives $Z = 1 + [K'''(0) + \tfrac12K''''(0) + \dots]/V_A$, where $K'''(0)$ is the third cumulant of $z$, so a right-skewed distribution of breeding values, with the variance concentrated in a few lineages, raises the retention factor above one (\autoref{sec:approx}).

Suppose instead that $V_A$ is carried by rare deleterious alleles at mutation--selection balance. A heterozygous carrier of an allele in effect class $k$ has relative fitness $1 - s_k$, a deviation $\ln(1 - s_k)$ in $z$, and the allele has frequency $q_k \approx \mu/s_k$ (i.e. mutation-selection balance). Cumulants add over unlinked loci, and for a rare allele $K_k''(\theta) \approx 2q_k \ln^2(1 - s_k)\,(1 - s_k)^{\theta}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:Zcgf} then gives, to leading order in $s_k$,
\begin{equation}
    Z = \sum_k \nu_k Z_k, \qquad Z_k = 1 - s_k, \qquad \nu_k = \frac{\varphi_k s_k}{\bar{s}}, \label{eq:Zclass}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi_k$ the fraction of new mutations in class $k$. Each class is retained at its carriers' relative fitness, because the association is lost with the carriers that selection removes (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}). The weights $\nu_k$ are the classes' shares of $V_A$. The weighted mean is the aggregate $1 - \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s]$ of Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1998}, but $Q$ and $\overline{Q^2}_i$ are nonlinear in $Z$, so the main text deals with the classes separately. Faster purging lowers $Z$, and with it $Q$ and the reduction in $N_e$, below the Gaussian case. Rare beneficial alleles ($s_k < 0$) give $Z_k > 1$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:Zcgf} is specific to a multiplicative fitness model. If a parent's expected family size were instead linear in its breeding value, $1 + b\,a$ with $\mathbb{E}[a] = 0$, the offspring-weighted variance would be $\mathbb{E}[(1 + ba)a^2] - \mathbb{E}[(1 + ba)a]^2 = V_A + b\mu_3 - b^2V_A^2$, with $\mu_3$ the third central moment of $a$, so
\begin{equation}
    Z = 1 - b^2V_A + b\,\mu_3/V_A. \label{eq:Zarch}
\end{equation}
For additive, Gaussian fitness ($b = 1$, $\mu_3 = 0$), $Z = 1 - V_A$, $Q = 2/(1 + V_A)$ and $N_e/N = (1 + V_A)^2/(1 + 6V_A + V_A^2)$. This ratio is never below one half, reaching that value at $V_A = 1$ and rising back toward one at larger $V_A$, so under a linear map from breeding value to fitness heritable variance can at most halve the effective size, and beyond $V_A = 1$ more variance reduces it less. The limit comes from the linear map, not from selection: with $w = 1 + a$, a share $\Phi(-1/\sqrt{V_A})$ of individuals has negative expected fitness, about 8\% at $V_A = 0.5$ and 16\% at $V_A = 1$, which is the range in which the linear map departs from Equation~\eqref{eq:A_first}. Given this unrealistic assumption, we do not explore this mapping further.
