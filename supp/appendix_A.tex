\section{Effective size under heritable variance in log-fitness}\label{app:unlinkedapp}

\subsection{Long-term genetic contributions and effective size}\label{app:A_R}
Following Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011}, consider a diploid population of $N$ breeding adults in which a parent with additive breeding value $z$ for log-fitness produces a Poisson number of offspring with mean proportional to $e^{z}$, where $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{z}, V_A)$. Dominance and epistatic deviations are omitted because they do not transmit stably across generations. Assume initially that mating is random with respect to $z$, and consider a neutral locus unlinked to all selected loci (pair bonds and extra-pair paternity are treated in Appendix~\ref{app:matingapp}, and physical linkage in Appendix~\ref{app:chromosapp}).

Track a neutral gene copy carried by an ancestor with breeding value $z$. Tracking a gene copy rather than an individual allows the same bookkeeping to extend directly to pair bonds and linked sites; at an unlinked site the two perspectives are equivalent because a gene copy's survival probability is proportional to the long-term genetic contribution of its carrier~\cite{barton2011}. Under the infinitesimal model, offspring of carrier $z$ inherit a mean deviation $\tfrac12(z - \bar{z})$ plus a Mendelian segregation residual of variance $\tfrac12 V_A$. Because this segregation variance is homoscedastic (independent of $z$) and expected family sizes multiply along a lineage, deviations in each subsequent generation decay by a factor of $1/2$ and sum on the log scale to $(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots)(z - \bar{z}) = 2(z - \bar{z})$. Solving the exact reproductive-value recursion~\cite[Appendix~B]{barton2011}, the expected long-term contribution of the ancestral copy, normalized such that $\mathbb{E}[R] = 1$, is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) = \exp\left[Q(z - \bar{z}) - \tfrac12 Q^2V_A\right], \qquad Q = 2. \label{eq:A_R}
\end{equation}

Two neutral copies sampled in a distant future generation coalesce in the ancestral generation if both descend from the same ancestral copy. Ancestral copy $j$ ($j = 1, \dots, 2N$), carried by an individual with breeding value $z_j$, contributes an expected fraction $p_j = R(z_j)/\sum_{j'=1}^{2N} R(z_{j'}) = R(z_j)/(2N\,\mathbb{E}[R])$ of the descendant gene pool. Conditional on the carrier's breeding value, the two homologous copies within a diploid parent segregate independently into Poisson family sizes, so two sampled lineages trace to copy $j$ with probability $p_j^2$~\cite{barton2011}. Summing over all $2N$ ancestral copies yields the per-generation rate of inbreeding~\cite{wray1990},
\begin{equation}
    \Delta F = \sum_{j=1}^{2N} p_j^2 = \frac{1}{2N}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}. \label{eq:A_dF}
\end{equation}
In the absence of heritable variance, $R = \mathbb{E}[R]$ and $\Delta F = 1/(2N)$, the Wright--Fisher Poisson baseline. With heritable variance, setting the single-generation coalescence probability $\Delta F = 1/(2N_e)$ defines the inbreeding (and coalescent) effective size $N_e$ governing nucleotide diversity $\pi$. Unlike the single-generation variance effective size—which reflects only immediate family-size variance—this long-term $N_e$ incorporates the multi-generational persistence of fitness advantages encoded by $Q^2$. When $N$ and $V_A$ are stationary, the long-term variance and coalescent effective sizes coincide:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}, \label{eq:A_ratio}
\end{equation}
giving Equation~\eqref{eq:ratio} of the main text. Because $\log R$ is Gaussian, $\mathbb{E}[R]^2/\mathbb{E}[R^2] = \exp[-\mathrm{Var}(\log R)]$. Under random mating, $\mathrm{Var}(\log R) = Q^2V_A = 4V_A$, yielding Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:A_mult}
\end{equation}

Under strict lifetime monogamy, pairs form at random and a pair with breeding values $z$ and $z'$ produces offspring in proportion to $e^{z + z'}$~\cite{weissman2012}. Every offspring that inherits the focal neutral copy from parent $z$ simultaneously inherits half of its unlinked background from parent $z$ and half from the monogamous partner $z'$. Consequently, the partner's deviation $(z' - \bar{z})$ is co-transmitted along the focal gene's lineage through the same geometric series ($1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots = 2$) as the carrier's own deviation, yielding $\log R = 2(z - \bar{z}) + 2(z' - \bar{z}) - 4V_A$. Because $z$ and $z'$ are independent, $\mathrm{Var}(\log R) = 8V_A$ and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-8V_A}. \label{eq:A_mono}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:A_mult} and \eqref{eq:A_mono} match the $e^{-4V_A}$ and $e^{-8V_A}$ reductions in the fixation probability of a weakly beneficial allele derived by Weissman and Barton~\cite{weissman2012}, as both coalescence and fixation rates scale inversely with the variance of long-term genetic contributions $\mathbb{E}[R^2]/\mathbb{E}[R]^2$~\cite{kimura1962}.

\subsection{The small-variance approximation}\label{app:A_first}
When $V_A \ll 1$, linearizing $R(z) \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$ gives $\mathbb{E}[R^2] = 1 + \mathrm{Var}(R) \approx 1 + Q^2V_A$, reducing Equation~\eqref{eq:A_ratio} to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2V_A}. \label{eq:A_first}
\end{equation}
Robertson~\cite{robertson1961} and Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} (reviewed in Wang et al.~\cite{wang2016}) derived Equation~\eqref{eq:A_first} for any smooth mapping from breeding value to fitness, because all such mappings linearize to the same first-order form as $V_A \to 0$. In their general formulation, the cumulative transmission factor is $Q = 2/[2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})]$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Q}), where $Z$ is the retention factor (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}) and $r_{mf}$ is the correlation between mates' breeding values. At $Z = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$, $Q = 2$ and $N_e/N = 1/(1 + 4V_A)$; under strict monogamy, expanding Equation~\eqref{eq:A_mono} gives $1/(1 + 8V_A)$.

Incorporating the general mating-system factor $\kappa(\alpha)$ (Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating}), setting $N_e/N = \delta$, and inverting the small-variance form yields the linear variance ceiling
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\max} = \frac{1 - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta}, \label{eq:vamax}
\end{equation}
above which heritable fitness variance alone would remove more diversity than observed. Because the small-variance expansion holds only for $V_{A,\max} \le 1$, Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax} applies only when $\delta \gtrsim 1/(1 + 4\kappa)$: populations retaining at least $\delta \gtrsim 0.20$ of expected neutral diversity under random mating ($\kappa = 1$), $\delta \gtrsim 0.12$--$0.16$ under social monogamy with extra-pair paternity ($\kappa_B = 1.32$--$1.86$), and $\delta \gtrsim 0.11$ under strict monogamy ($\kappa = 2$). For deeper diversity deficits, the required variance must be evaluated from the exact multiplicative bound $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}).

\subsection{The upper tail of log-fitness and finite-population truncation}\label{app:A_tail}
Under random mating, $\mathbb{E}[R^2] \propto \mathbb{E}[e^{4z}]$. Exponentially tilting the Gaussian density of $z$ by $e^{4z}$ shifts its mean to $\bar{z} + 4V_A$ without altering its variance, meaning that the ancestors dominating $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$ lie $4\sqrt{V_A}$ standard deviations above the population mean. A $10^2$-fold reduction in $N_e$ ($V_A = \ln(100)/4 = 1.15$) is driven by ancestors $4.3$ standard deviations above the mean (roughly $1$ in $10^5$ individuals), whereas a $10^4$-fold reduction ($V_A = 2.30$) requires ancestors at $6.1$ standard deviations (roughly $1$ in $10^9$).

Realizing the analytical expectation $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$ therefore requires both an untruncated biological upper tail and a census size large enough to sample that tail. Ecological ceilings on reproductive output—such as territory saturation, clutch-size constraints, or the upper bound of unity on breeding probability in zero-inflated hurdle models~\cite{bonnet2022} (\autoref{sec:onemodel})—truncate the tilted density and lower $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$. Consequently, Equations~\eqref{eq:A_mult}, \eqref{eq:A_mono}, and \eqref{eq:B_mult_pair} represent upper bounds on the reduction in $N_e$ for a given $V_A$.

Finite population size imposes a second, structural ceiling. For a general mating structure with $\mathrm{Var}(\log R) = 4\kappa_{\max}V_A$, the lineages dominating $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$ originate from ancestors $k = 4\sqrt{\kappa_{\max}V_A}$ standard deviations above the mean. To leading exponential order in the Gaussian tail ($\propto e^{-k^2/2}$), such individuals comprise a fraction $\sim e^{-8\kappa_{\max}V_A}$ of the cohort. A population of $N$ breeding adults (or $N/2$ monogamous pairs) samples these ancestors in a typical generation only while
\begin{equation}
    8\kappa_{\max}V_A \lesssim \ln N, \label{eq:A_tail_limit}
\end{equation}
at which threshold $N_e/N = e^{-4\kappa_{\max}V_A} = N^{-1/2}$. Beyond this threshold, most generations lack the extreme tail individuals needed to achieve $\mathbb{E}[R^2]$; the analytical expectation is recovered only across vast averages dominated by rare generations containing an exceptional founder. For example, in single-generation Monte Carlo draws of $N = 10^6$ random-mating individuals (where the threshold is $V_A = \ln(10^6)/8 = 1.73$), the median realized pairwise coalescence rate at $V_A = 1.5$ is only $0.56$ of the analytical expectation (range $0.36$--$5.5\times$), dropping at $V_A = 1.73$ to $0.39$, where the single fittest individual accounts for $\sim 1\%$ of all long-term genetic contributions.

\subsection{The retention factor \texorpdfstring{$Z$}{Z}}\label{app:A_Z}
In an ancestor-centered framing, the retention factor $Z$ is the additive genetic variance among successfully reproducing parents (weighted by their offspring output) divided by the cohort variance $V_A$. In a gene-centered framing, $Z$ is the per-generation retention of a neutral allele's statistical association with its selective background (the factor $G$ of Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995}). Defining a stationary retention factor $Z$ requires that allele frequencies at selected loci change negligibly over the timescale on which the background association decays~\cite{barton1998,barton2000}, as holds at mutation--selection--drift balance.

Under multiplicative fitness, weighting parents by their expected offspring $e^{z}$ applies an exponential tilt with parameter $\psi = 1$ to the distribution of $z$. Letting $K(\psi) = \ln \mathbb{E}[e^{\psi z}]$ denote the cumulant-generating function of $z$, the variance of the offspring-weighted parent distribution is $K''(1)$, while $V_A = K''(0)$, giving
\begin{equation}
    Z = \frac{K''(1)}{K''(0)}. \label{eq:Zcgf}
\end{equation}
When $z$ is strictly Gaussian, $K(\psi) = \psi\bar{z} + \tfrac12\psi^2V_A$, so $K''(\psi) = V_A$ for all $\psi$ and $Z = 1$ identically: exponential selection shifts the parental mean by $V_A$ while leaving the variance invariant. Expanding $K''(1)$ around $\psi = 0$ for an arbitrary distribution gives
\begin{equation}
    Z = 1 + \frac{K'''(0) + \tfrac12 K''''(0) + \dots}{V_A},
\end{equation}
showing that positive skewness in breeding values ($K'''(0) > 0$) elevates $Z > 1$ (\autoref{sec:approx}).

Now suppose $V_A$ is maintained by rare deleterious alleles at mutation--selection balance. A heterozygous carrier of an allele in selective class $k$ has relative fitness $1 - s_k$, a log-fitness deviation $\ln(1 - s_k)$, and equilibrium frequency $q_k \approx \mu/s_k$. Because cumulants sum across unlinked loci, a rare locus in class $k$ contributes $K_k''(\psi) \approx 2q_k \ln^2(1 - s_k)\,(1 - s_k)^{\psi}$. Applying Equation~\eqref{eq:Zcgf} across classes to leading order in $s_k$ yields
\begin{equation}
    Z = \sum_k \nu_k Z_k, \qquad Z_k = 1 - s_k, \qquad \nu_k = \frac{\varphi_k s_k}{\bar{s}}, \label{eq:Zclass}
\end{equation}
where $\varphi_k$ is the proportion of new deleterious mutations falling in class $k$ and $\nu_k$ is that class's share of total $V_A$. Each mutational class is retained in proportion to its carriers' relative survival $1 - s_k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}). While the class-weighted average reproduces the aggregate retention factor $1 - \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s]$ of Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1998}, the main text evaluates $Q$ and $\overline{Q^2}_i$ separately by class because both quantities are nonlinear in $Z_k$. Stronger purifying selection ($s_k$ large) accelerates purging, lowering $Z_k$, $Q_k$, and the resulting reduction in $N_e$ below the Gaussian ($Z=1$) limit; conversely, rare beneficial alleles ($s_k < 0$) yield $Z_k > 1$.

Finally, Equation~\eqref{eq:Zcgf} relies on the exponential map $w \propto e^z$. Under an explicitly linear map $w = 1 + a$ with $\mathbb{E}[a] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(a) = V_A$, the offspring-weighted parental variance is $\mathbb{E}[(1 + a)a^2] - \mathbb{E}[(1 + a)a]^2 = V_A + \mathbb{E}[a^3] - V_A^2$, giving
\begin{equation}
    Z = 1 - V_A + \frac{\mathbb{E}[a^3]}{V_A}. \label{eq:Zarch}
\end{equation}
For a symmetric Gaussian breeding value ($\mathbb{E}[a^3] = 0$), $Z = 1 - V_A$, $Q = 2/(1 + V_A)$, and $N_e/N = (1 + V_A)^2/(1 + 6V_A + V_A^2)$. This ratio attains a minimum of $1/2$ at $V_A = 1$ before increasing back toward $1$ at larger $V_A$. That pathological rebound is an artifact of the linear map assigning negative expected offspring ($1 + a < 0$) to a fraction $\Phi(-1/\sqrt{V_A})$ of the cohort ($8\%$ of individuals at $V_A = 0.5$ and $16\%$ at $V_A = 1$); we therefore do not consider the linear fitness map further.
