\section{Appendix A: The Unlinked Reduction under One Model of Fitness}\label{app:unlinkedapp}
This appendix sets out the reduction in effective size caused by heritable fitness variance at sites unlinked to any selected locus. Section A.1 states the first-order result of Robertson (1961) and Santiago and Caballero (1995). Section A.2 connects it to the estimate of Bonnet et al. (2022), whose animal model is multiplicative. Section A.3 gives the all-orders results under that model, published for random mating and for strict monogamy (Barton and Etheridge 2011; Weissman and Barton 2012), and extends them to extra-pair paternity, which is the part derived here. Section A.4 treats the retention $Z$ and how it depends on the architecture of $V_A$.

\subsection*{A.1\quad First order}
The first-order result is that of Robertson (1961), generalized by Santiago and Caballero (1995; reviewed in Wang et al. 2016); we state it and the assumptions it carries rather than rederive it. Consider $N$ breeding adults whose family sizes would be Poisson in the absence of selection, and a neutral site unlinked to every selected locus. By chance, the carriers of a neutral allele differ from the population mean in breeding value for fitness, and the allele's frequency moves with that difference. Non-heritable variance in fitness moves the allele only once and is absorbed into the demographic background. Heritable variance moves it again in each later generation, because the carriers' descendants inherit part of the difference. The persistence is the cumulative transmission factor
\begin{equation}
    Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left[\frac{Z(1+r_{mf})}{2}\right]^t = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1+r_{mf})},
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the retention, the fraction of a gene copy's association with its selective background that survives one generation of selection (their $G$; Section A.4), and $r_{mf}$ is the correlation between mates' breeding values for fitness (written $r_{mf}$ to distinguish it from the recombination fraction $r$). Heritable fitness differences then add $Q^2V_A$ to the variance of long-term contributions among ancestors beyond its Poisson expectation, and that variance sets the rate of drift (Wray and Thompson 1990), so that
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2V_A}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4V_A} \quad \text{at } Z = 1,\ r_{mf} = 0. \label{eq:A_first}
\end{equation}
$Q = 2$ is the first-order form of the expected long-term contribution of Section~A.3: an ancestor with breeding value $z$ has $R(z) \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$, and without a pair bond this is $1 + L_X$, with $L_X$ the cumulative advantage of Appendix~B.

$Z = 1$ holds exactly for a Gaussian, infinitesimal breeding value under the multiplicative model of Section A.3, and is an upper bound when $V_A$ is carried by deleterious alleles (Section A.4). $r_{mf} = 0$ requires that mates pair at random with respect to their breeding values; under random mating Santiago and Caballero give $r_{mf} = -1/(N-1)$, and assortative mating for fitness ($r_{mf} > 0$) would raise $Q$. Only the additive component of fitness variance enters, because dominance and epistatic effects are not transmitted stably across generations. The result is first order: it treats a lineage's selective advantage as small, so it holds for any map from breeding value to fitness while $V_A \ll 1$. The extension to separate sexes and mating systems is in Appendix B, where it becomes $N_e/N = 1/(1+4\kappa V_A)$.

\subsection*{A.2\quad The latent scale and the measured variance}
Bonnet et al. (2022) model lifetime breeding success with two latent predictors: a logit-linked probability of breeding ($l_1$) and a log-linked expected number of offspring given breeding ($l_2$). Breeding values are additive and Gaussian on these latent scales, with genetic covariance matrix $\mathbf{G}$. They report the observed-scale additive variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{d}$ is the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to $l_1$ and $l_2$. For a log link the average derivative equals the mean, which is one for relative fitness, so for the log-linked component the observed-scale and latent variances coincide (de Villemereuil et al. 2016, Equations 15--16). To first order, then, $V_A(w)$ is the variance of a single breeding value $z$ for log-fitness, $w \propto e^{z}$, and that is the model used in Section A.3.

The logit component is different in kind. It acts on a probability, which cannot exceed one, so its contribution to fitness is bounded and compounds more slowly along a lineage than an exponential. Treating the whole of $V_A(w)$ as log-additive therefore overstates the reduction at all orders beyond the first; at first order the split between components does not matter. The posterior draws available to us report only the combined $V_A(w)$.

\subsection*{A.3\quad All orders under multiplicative fitness}
Let parents be chosen with probability proportional to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian with variance $V_A$ (Barton and Etheridge 2011). An ancestor with log value $z$ has expected offspring in proportion to $e^{z}$, and its descendants inherit, on average, half of its deviation each generation. Barton and Etheridge (2011, Appendix B) solve the resulting recursion for the expected long-term contribution of the ancestor (its mean reproductive value),
\begin{equation}
    R(z) = \exp\left[2(z - \bar{z}) - 2V_A\right],
\end{equation}
so that the cumulative factor $Q = 2$ sits in the exponent. They note that the variance of $R$ across ancestors is $e^{4V_A}$, and that the probability that an ancestor is involved in a coalescence goes as the square of $R$. The rate of inbreeding is proportional to the second moment of long-term genetic contributions (Wray and Thompson 1990), which gives, without a pair bond, the factor $e^{4V_A}$ by which Weissman and Barton (2012) find that heritable variance in log-fitness at unlinked loci increases the rate of coalescence,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}{\mathbb{E}[R^2]} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:A_mult}
\end{equation}
Its expansion, $1 - 4V_A + O(V_A^2)$, is the first-order result of Section A.1.

\paragraph{Strict monogamy.} Weissman and Barton (2012) treat the case in which pairs form at random and each pair, with breeding values $z$ and $z'$, produces a Poisson number of offspring with expectation proportional to $e^{z + z'}$. They show that the fixation probability of a new mutation is proportional to the square of the fitness of the individual in which it arises in this model as in the random-mating one, and that with monogamy heritable variance in log-fitness reduces it by $e^{-8V_A}$ rather than $e^{-4V_A}$ (their Results, "Unlinked loci", and Text S2). Because coalescence goes as the square of the long-term contribution in the same way, the reduction in effective size under strict monogamy is $N_e/N = e^{-8V_A}$. This is the all-orders counterpart of the pair-mated family variance $C^2 = 2V_A$ of Santiago and Caballero (1995), whose first-order form is $1/(1 + 8V_A)$. Their model is haploid and has no separate sexes or extra-pair paternity.

\paragraph{Extra-pair paternity.} What follows extends the strict-monogamy result to extra-pair paternity at rate $\alpha$. With the nest-sharing model of Appendix B (Model B), a carrier's family size is proportional to $e^{z}$ for its log breeding value $z$ in either sex, so later generations compound exactly as above. Only the first generation mixes two kinds of offspring. A copy in a female with social partner $m'$ has nest productivity $e^{z_f + z_{m'}}$; a fraction $1 - \alpha$ of those offspring are sired by $m'$, and $\alpha$ by extra-pair males. A copy in a male with social partner $f'$ gains within-pair offspring from his own nest and extra-pair offspring elsewhere. The expected long-term contributions are, up to constants,
\begin{equation}
    R_f \propto (1-\alpha)\,e^{2(z_f + z_{m'})} + \alpha\,e^{2z_f + z_{m'}}, \qquad
    R_m \propto (1-\alpha)\,e^{2(z_m + z_{f'})} + \alpha\,e^{2z_m},
\end{equation}
and $N/N_e$ is the mean over the sexes of $\mathbb{E}[R^2]/\mathbb{E}[R]^2$. With independent Gaussian breeding values these moments are sums of lognormal moments, $\mathbb{E}[e^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{z}}] = e^{V_A|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^2/2}$. The exponent vectors satisfy $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}_l \le \max_k|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_k|^2 = 8$, from which $\mathbb{E}[R^2]/\mathbb{E}[R]^2 \le e^{8V_A}$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    e^{-8V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa_B V_A}. \label{eq:A_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
Equality holds at both ends under strict monogamy ($\alpha = 0$, where $\kappa_B = 2$), which recovers Weissman and Barton's $e^{-8V_A}$. The right-hand end matches the first-order result $1/(1 + 4\kappa_B V_A)$, with slope $4\kappa_B$ at $V_A = 0$. We confirmed numerically that the exact value never falls outside the bracket, including when the weight of the extra-pair term is distorted by factors between $e^{-2V_A}$ and $e^{2V_A}$, which allows for the selection of extra-pair sires and the normalization of the extra-pair pool. At small $V_A$ the exact value lies close to the right-hand end, and as $V_A$ grows it moves toward the left, because the within-nest term compounds fastest.

\paragraph{Validity.} Equations~\eqref{eq:A_mult} and \eqref{eq:A_mult_pair} rest on the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness. The second moment $\mathbb{E}[R^2] \propto \mathbb{E}[e^{4z}]$ is carried by ancestors near $z = 4V_A$, or $4\sqrt{V_A}$ standard deviations above the mean. A 100-fold reduction without a pair bond ($V_A = \ln(100)/4$) rests on ancestors 4.3 standard deviations out, about one in $10^5$, and a $10^4$-fold reduction on ancestors 6.1 standard deviations out. The expectation is realized only in populations large enough to contain such individuals, and only if nothing caps their fitness. Territories, maximum clutch sizes and the bounded probability of breeding (Section A.2) all truncate the tail and reduce the effect. These forms are therefore upper bounds on the reduction that a given $V_A$ produces in a real population. The derivation also assumes Poisson sampling of offspring beyond the heritable component, random pairing with respect to breeding value, and loci unlinked to the focal site.

\subsection*{A.4\quad The retention $Z$ and the architecture of $V_A$}
The retention $Z$ is the fraction of a gene copy's association with its selective background that survives one generation of selection. Santiago and Caballero (1995) write it $G$ and define it through the variance among selected parents; Santiago and Caballero (1998) and Buffalo and Kern (2024) write it $Z$, and Buffalo and Kern's $1 - Z$ is their $\kappa$, which is unrelated to the mating-system scalar of Appendix B. A retention per generation presupposes a selected background that is constant over time, which mutation--selection--drift balance provides (Barton 1998, 2000). The two definitions coincide, and the variance definition shows how $Z$ depends on the architecture of $V_A$. Let a parent's expected family size be $w = 1 + b\,a$, linear in its breeding value $a$ with mean zero. The additive variance among parents, weighted by their offspring, is then exactly $V_A - b^2V_A^2 + b\,\mu_3$, where $\mu_3$ is the third central moment of $a$, so
\begin{equation}
    Z = 1 - b^2 V_A + b\,\mu_3/V_A. \label{eq:Zarch}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Gaussian, infinitesimal breeding values.} Under the multiplicative model of Section A.3 selection weights a Gaussian breeding value by $e^{z}$. Exponential tilting of a normal distribution shifts its mean by $V_A$ and leaves its variance unchanged, so $Z = 1$ at every value of $V_A$. This is the case used for the genome-wide reduction and for the all-orders forms of Section A.3.

\paragraph{Deleterious alleles at mutation--selection balance.} If $V_A$ is carried by rare alleles of effect $-s_k$, the distribution of $a$ is strongly left-skewed. Summed over loci with frequencies $q_k \approx \mu/s_k$, $\mu_3/V_A = -\mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s]$, so Equation~\eqref{eq:Zarch} gives $Z \approx 1 - \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s]$ for the variance as a whole (the term in $V_A$ vanishes on the multiplicative scale), the aggregate rate of Santiago and Caballero (1998), and $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ for each class: the association is lost with the carriers that selection removes. The aggregate is the variance-weighted mean of the $Z_k$, but $Q$ and the linked interference are nonlinear in $Z$, so the main text carries the classes separately. A direct calculation for a single rare allele confirms this ($Z = 0.990$, $0.901$ and $0.702$ at $s = 0.01$, $0.1$ and $0.3$). Under multiplicative fitness the retention is the carriers' relative fitness, $e^{-s_k} \approx 1 - s_k$. Fast purging therefore lowers $Z$, and with it $Q$ and the reduction, below the infinitesimal case. Rare beneficial alleles skew $a$ the other way and give $Z > 1$.

\paragraph{The additive map, as a remark.} If instead fitness were additive and Gaussian on the relative scale ($b = 1$, $\mu_3 = 0$), Equation~\eqref{eq:Zarch} would give $Z = 1 - V_A$, $Q = 2/(1+V_A)$ (the same form as $Q = 2/(1+s)$ in Appendix D), and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \left[1 + \frac{4V_A}{(1+V_A)^2}\right]^{-1}, \label{eq:Gcorrected}
\end{equation}
which never falls below 1/2. This saturation is an artifact of the map. With $w = 1 + a$ and $a$ Gaussian, a share $\Phi(-1/\sqrt{V_A})$ of individuals has negative expected fitness: about 8\% at $V_A = 0.5$ and 16\% at $V_A = 1$. The map is therefore internally inconsistent in exactly the range where it departs from the first-order result. It agrees with Section A.1 to first order and is not used beyond it.
