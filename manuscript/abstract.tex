\section*{Abstract}
Wild populations exhibit substantial additive genetic variance in lifetime fitness according to pedigree-based quantitative genetic estimates. Classical population genetics theory predicts two equilibrium consequences of such variance: it should reduce neutral diversity, and it should be maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation at mutation--selection--drift balance. We test pedigree estimates of the additive genetic variance in lifetime breeding success from 19 populations against both models where the data allow, under the assumption that the effects of loci on fitness multiply. We extend the predicted reduction in effective population size to social monogamy with extra-pair paternity and to linked sites along empirical linkage maps, and compute the variance that measured mutation rates and empirical distributions of fitness effects can maintain in these populations. Results demonstrate that the measured variance is too small to explain the deficit in neutral diversity measured in these populations and too large to be maintained by mutation--selection balance. The measured heritable variance roughly halves the effective size of the median population, whereas the observed neutral diversity deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold would require a substantially larger long-term variance. Maintaining such a variance would require the average new mutation to lower the fitness of a heterozygous carrier by about 16\%, a median 21 times the mean effects estimated in related species. Taken as the long-term variance, no single scaling of the measured variance allows it to pass both tests, because the multiples the two tests require differ about 390-fold on average and over 100-fold in each of the tested populations. The conflict between the two equilibria does not depend on the estimates themselves, because in those populations a variance maintained by mutation would remove at most 5\% of neutral diversity at unlinked sites. Our results suggest that if the measured variance persists, most of it is therefore maintained by processes other than recurrent deleterious mutation, and most of the missing diversity has causes other than heritable variance in fitness.
