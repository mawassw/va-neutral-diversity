\section*{Abstract}
Pedigree-based estimates show that wild populations carry substantial additive genetic variance in lifetime fitness. In classical theory, such variance reduces neutral diversity and is maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation. This variance is not expected to explain the missing genetic diversity of abundant species, but neither its share of that deficit nor the share maintained by mutation has been measured in individual natural populations. We measure both in 19 pedigreed populations of birds and mammals (18 for maintenance, 9 for diversity), allowing for extra-pair paternity and chromosome-scale linkage. If loci act multiplicatively on fitness, the measured variance roughly halves the median population's effective size. On a logarithmic scale, it accounts for a median 7\% (1--40\%) of the observed 230- to 38,000-fold deficits; explaining them would require a long-term variance above one, larger than any estimate. At measured mutation rates and mean deleterious effects estimated in related species, mutation--selection balance maintains about a twentieth of the variance in the median population and less than all of it in 16 of 18 populations. This excess persists in 12 populations when a larger mutational target, undetected large-effect mutations, and an estimate inflated twofold are combined, and in 6 under stronger departures. Because the variance that mutation--selection balance can maintain removes at most 5\% of neutral diversity at unlinked sites in populations with diversity data, no rescaled estimate fits both equilibria. With current estimates, most of the measured variance is maintained by other processes, and most of the missing diversity has other causes.
