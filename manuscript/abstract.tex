\section*{Abstract}
Pedigree-based estimates show that wild populations carry substantial additive genetic variance in lifetime fitness. Classical population genetics theory predicts that such variance should reduce neutral diversity and be maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation at mutation--selection--drift balance. We test both equilibrium models using estimates from 19 pedigreed populations (18 for maintenance, 9 for diversity). We extend the models of the reduction in effective population size to extra-pair paternity and to linked sites at a chromosomal level, and compute the variance that measured mutation rates and distributions of fitness effects can maintain. The measured variance is too small to explain the missing neutral diversity and too large to be maintained by mutation--selection balance. It roughly halves the effective size of the median population, whereas observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold would require a long-term variance above one, larger than any estimate. Maintaining the measured variance would require the average new mutation to lower the fitness of a heterozygous carrier by about 16\%, a median 21 times the mean effects estimated in related species. No single rescaling of the estimates makes them congruent with the equilibrium models, because in every population with diversity data a variance that mutation--selection balance can maintain would remove at most 5\% of neutral diversity at unlinked sites, and the rescalings the two models require differ by more than 100-fold. If the measured variance persists, most of it is maintained by processes other than mutation--selection balance, and most of the missing diversity has causes other than heritable variance in fitness.
