\section*{Abstract}
Pedigree-based animal models now estimate the additive genetic variance in lifetime fitness, $V_A$, in wild populations, where it is often substantial. Classical theory attaches two equilibrium expectations to such a variance: it should reduce neutral diversity, and it should be maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation at mutation--selection--drift balance. We put pedigree estimates from 19 populations to both tests where the data allow, 18 to the maintenance test and 9 to the diversity test, under the multiplicative fitness model on which the estimates rest. We extend the reduction in effective size to social monogamy with extra-pair paternity and to linked sites along empirical linkage maps, and compute the variance that measured mutation rates and empirical distributions of fitness effects can maintain. The measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing and too large to be maintained by mutation. It roughly halves effective size in the median population, whereas the observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold would require a long-term $V_A$ above one. Maintaining it would require the average new mutation to lower heterozygous fitness by about 16\%, a median 21 times the mean effects estimated in related species. No single rescaling of the estimates to a long-term value passes both tests, because the required values differ about 390-fold on average and over 100-fold in each of the nine populations with diversity data. The conflict does not depend on the estimates, because in those populations a variance maintained by mutation would remove at most 5\% of neutral diversity. If the measured variance persists, most of it is therefore maintained by processes other than recurrent deleterious mutation, and most of the missing diversity has causes other than heritable variance in fitness.
