\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical population genetics theory attributes to such variance. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves the effective size of the median population if, as the animal model behind the estimates assumes, the effects of loci on fitness multiply and breeding values for log-fitness are normally distributed. Compared against the diversity each population has lost, however, it explains only a small part of each deficit, even if nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring. In the median population the same variance is more than an order of magnitude larger than mutation--selection--drift balance can maintain at the effects of deleterious mutations measured in related species. Most of the missing diversity therefore has causes other than heritable variance in fitness, and most of the measured variance must be maintained by forces other than recurrent mutation, so both equilibria leave most of what they are meant to explain to other processes. The question these estimates raise is not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test shows that heritable variance in fitness of the measured magnitude explains only a small part of neutral diversity deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. The median reduction at unlinked sites lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates, using an approximation that holds while the variance is small, and across species Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} found that background selection and recurrent hitchhiking, at \textit{Drosophila} parameters or tenfold stronger ones, also explain only part of the deficit. Most of the rest is likely to come from processes that act over the many generations that neutral diversity reflects, which neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Diversity reflects the harmonic mean of population size over its genealogy, which bottlenecks and range contractions hold close to the smallest sizes, as Charlesworth and Jensen emphasize, so a contemporary census overstates the population size from which we computed the diversity expected without selection. Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by the same mechanism through which heritable fitness differences increase drift, acting on demes rather than individuals, because a deme's success, like an individual's heritable advantage, persists across generations~\cite{whitlock1997}. Relatives share their deme, so a pedigree within a single deme cannot measure this source of drift, which, like a bottleneck, raises the variance that the diversity test requires without implying that the estimate is too low. Demography and deme turnover are therefore the likeliest causes of the missing diversity, because they directly reduce the effective size that diversity reflects.

Heritable variance in fitness reduces diversity at a neutral site in two ways, through the individual that carries the site, an effect shared by every site, and through the chromosome segment that carries it, an effect that varies along the genome. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024} because they estimate the second effect relative to a genome-wide baseline that already includes the first~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Such maps cannot measure the genome-wide effect, which accounts of the deficit have presumed small~\cite{matheson2024}, whereas a pedigree, which measures variance in fitness among individuals, provides the variance on which it depends. The same maps attribute all selected variance to recurrent deleterious mutation, and to match the depth of the diversity reductions they fit deleterious mutation rates and selected targets higher than independent estimates support~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Our maintenance test shows that recurrent mutation maintains about a twentieth of the variance measured in wild pedigrees, so the pattern of diversity along a chromosome, which depends on where selected sites lie and how fast selection removes them, can follow mutation--selection--drift balance while the depth of the reduction requires variance from other sources, whose genome-wide effect the maps count as part of their baseline. Along chromosomes, by contrast, the reduction depends on what maintains the variance, because an association between a neutral site and linked selected alleles lasts only as long as those alleles do~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}, so it is large only when the variance is carried by alleles that selection removes slowly, the alleles least able to maintain a large variance. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test this prediction, since diversity on each chromosome should decrease with the amount of selected sequence per unit of map length, along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test shows that lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates, like the fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} that Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} studied, carries more additive variance than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, so the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} applies to fitness itself. For mutation to maintain the measured variance, the average new mutation in the functional genome would have to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 16\%, a median 21 times the mean effect estimated for related species, and large-effect mutations hidden from site-frequency spectra, such as transposable-element insertions, could raise the mean that far only if about 30\% of new mutations had a heterozygous effect of 0.5. Molecular estimates imply a high deleterious mutation rate in humans~\cite{kondrashov1993}, recently put at 2 to 10 per generation~\cite{matheson2023} and above 3.8 once slightly deleterious mutations that fix are counted~\cite{hernandez2026}, with about 7--9\% of the non-coding genome under purifying selection~\cite{di2025}. Counting up to 15\% of the unannotated sequence of our assemblies as deleterious lowers the median excess from 21-fold to 4-fold but resolves it only in the meerkats, and in most bird populations the deleterious sequence required would be larger than the genome. Drift cannot maintain the excess either, because selection on these mutations is strong relative to drift, so the variance that mutation maintains does not depend on effective size. A higher deleterious mutation rate also removes more diversity through background selection, yet even with the largest target we considered, the variance that mutation maintains removes at most about half of neutral diversity, so a mutational account narrows the maintenance gap without explaining the missing diversity.

What else maintains the variance depends on whether selection is described on phenotypes or on alleles, because the same variance describes both (\autoref{sec:quantity}). On phenotypes, additive variance in fitness is directional selection, predicting change in every trait that covaries genetically with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}, and at mutation--selection--drift balance it is purifying selection on alleles, so our maintenance test shows that about a twentieth of the directional selection implied by the pedigrees can be purifying in the median population. A moving or fluctuating optimum, consistent with the year-to-year variation in selection on breeding date across 39 populations of birds and mammals~\cite{devillemereuil2020}, can maintain the rest by turning standing variation in a trait into heritable variance in fitness through the lag of the population mean behind the optimum~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Drift around a balanced equilibrium can also maintain it, because a single overdominant or negatively frequency-dependent locus displaced from equilibrium contributes as much additive variance in fitness as a hundred to ten thousand loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations~\cite{connallon2026}. A moving optimum is one form of the deteriorating environment of Fisher's fundamental theorem, through which Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, who interpreted their estimates as the rate of contemporary adaptation, and earlier reviews~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018} explained why a large heritable variance in fitness need not show in population growth. Such an environment can offset the gain in mean fitness that selection produces but not the drift that its allele-frequency changes add each generation, so neutral diversity records the variance accumulated over its genealogy, whereas the theorem describes a single generation. A pedigree cannot tell these sources of variance apart, but they predict different patterns, large-effect variants at intermediate frequency under balancing selection and, under a changing environment, breeding values that change while phenotypes may not, one explanation offered for evolutionary stasis in the wild~\cite{merila2001}.

Shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that the animal model does not include can inflate pedigree estimates~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}, and an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection would understate the variance. Both tests concern the same long-term variance, which removes more diversity the larger it is but is harder to maintain, so an inflated estimate brings the maintenance test closer to passing and widens the diversity gap by the same factor. Within every population with diversity data, the long-term variance that the diversity test requires is at least 108 times the variance that the maintenance test allows, and at least 81 times with linkage, so no error that scales the estimate up or down lets it pass both. We chose the approximations so that each test is biased toward passing, which makes a failed test conservative (\autoref{sec:approx}). If only one adult in ten breeds, the variance that the deficits of bighorn sheep and song sparrows require with linkage comes within a few percent of the largest their estimates allow, and below it at the lower end of the mutation-rate interval, and only in snow voles, red squirrels and meerkats does the measured variance exceed what mutation maintains by less than threefold. To reconcile the two tests, an assumption of unknown direction, such as discrete generations in iteroparous populations~\cite{engen2005}, a single deme per population, or distributions of fitness effects~\cite{lin2026} and diversity taken from related species, would have to shift a result by about two orders of magnitude. Because the usual criticisms of pedigree estimates describe errors that scale the estimate, the gap reflects more the processes the equilibrium models leave out than error in the estimate.

Predicting the loss of diversity from the variance alone also requires that allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while a neutral site remains associated with them~\cite{barton2000}, a condition met at mutation--selection--drift balance and by a polygenic response to a changing environment (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but their substitutions are bounded by the slow rate of molecular evolution. Balancing selection meets the condition least well, because its variance exists only away from equilibrium and its effects change with allele frequency, and slight fluctuations of balanced polymorphisms reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, possibly more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness. Balancing selection that fluctuates with the seasons reduces genome-wide effective size by about 30\% for 100 loci in one model and by about 50\%, up to 76\%, in another, largely independently of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}, far less than the deficits observed here, but its full effect at linked sites is not yet known. Our conclusion that the measured variance is too small to explain the missing diversity therefore holds for a variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance, and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained, and whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is the open question most likely to change it.

The condition for a single long-term variance to pass both tests (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) involves only the deleterious mutation rate, the mean effect of new mutations, the mating system and the loss of diversity, so the conflict lies between what mutation can maintain and the diversity that is missing, and the condition applies unchanged to any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness. Temporal change in allele frequencies provides one such estimate, because its covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. Replicate populations founded from a common base population come closer to the long-term variance that both tests concern, because the change in allele frequency due to selection, inferred across replicates over several generations, gives the additive genetic covariance in fitness across replicates and generations rather than a variance in the environment of a single generation~\cite{geetaarun2026}. Natural populations rarely provide such replicates, but long-term evolve-and-resequence experiments do, and repeating the estimate over successively longer intervals would show how far a short-term variance in fitness differs from its long-term value. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction in effective size directly, because expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give the variance of long-term contributions from which every reduction at unlinked sites here is derived~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}, so they measure the reduction from all sources of variance in reproductive success, of which heritable variance in fitness is one.
