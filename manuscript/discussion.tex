\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical population genetics theory attributes to such variance. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves the effective size of the median population if, as the animal model behind the estimates assumes, the effects of loci on fitness multiply and breeding values for log-fitness are normally distributed. \claude{A variance this small has that much effect because a pedigree measures only an ancestor's own advantage, to which inheritance by its descendants adds as much again, and partners who share their offspring share each other's advantage.} \claude{No account of the missing diversity relies on heritable variance in fitness alone, and against the diversity each population has lost this halving is a small part, a median of about a fifteenth of each deficit on a logarithmic scale,} even if nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring. \claude{In the median population, mutation--selection--drift balance maintains about a twentieth of the same variance, at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species and with the annotated functional sequence as the deleterious target.} Most of the missing diversity therefore has causes other than heritable variance in fitness, and\claude{, at mean effects taken from related species and the annotated target,} most of the measured variance \claude{would be} maintained by forces other than recurrent mutation, so both equilibria leave most of what they are meant to explain to other processes. The question these estimates raise is not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test shows that the median reduction at unlinked sites lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates, using an approximation that holds while the variance is small. \claude{Because each further tenfold loss of diversity requires the same further increment of variance, the observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold would require long-term variances above one, larger than any of the estimates.} Similarly, across species, Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} found that background selection and recurrent hitchhiking, at \textit{Drosophila} parameters or tenfold stronger ones, also explain only part of the deficit. Most of the rest is likely to come from processes that act over the many generations that neutral diversity reflects, which neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Because diversity reflects the harmonic mean of population size over its genealogy, which bottlenecks and range contractions hold close to the smallest sizes, as Charlesworth and Jensen emphasize, a contemporary census overstates the population size from which we computed the diversity expected without selection. \claude{Trajectories of effective size inferred from genome sequences could show whether these populations passed through such bottlenecks, although they include every source of drift and so cannot by themselves separate demography from heritable variance in fitness.} Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by the same mechanism through which heritable fitness differences increase drift, acting on demes rather than individuals, because a deme's success, like an individual's heritable advantage, persists across generations~\cite{whitlock1997}. \claude{Subdivision into demes that persist and exchange migrants has the opposite effect, raising diversity above the expectation for the total number of breeders}~\cite{whitlock1997}\claude{, so population structure lowers species-wide diversity only through deme turnover or unequal contributions of demes.} Because relatives share their deme, a pedigree within a single deme cannot measure this source of drift, which, like a bottleneck, raises the variance that the diversity test requires without implying that the estimate is too low. \claude{Similarly, Lewin and Eyre-Walker found contemporary effective sizes, which like a pedigree are estimated in a single locality over a few generations, to be unrelated to long-term effective sizes across species, and suggested that gene flow, which links local populations only over longer times, explains the mismatch}~\cite{lewin2026}\claude{.} \claude{Demography and deme turnover are the expected causes of the missing diversity, because they directly reduce the effective size that diversity reflects, so heritable variance in fitness adds a share to theirs rather than competing with them as an explanation.}

Heritable variance in fitness reduces diversity at a neutral site in two ways, through the individual that carries the site, an effect shared by every site, and through the chromosome segment that carries it, an effect that varies along the genome. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024} because they estimate the second effect relative to a genome-wide baseline that already includes the first~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Such maps cannot measure the genome-wide effect, which accounts of the deficit have presumed small~\cite{matheson2024}. A pedigree, by contrast, measures variance in fitness among individuals and so provides the variance on which the genome-wide effect depends. The same maps attribute all selected variance to recurrent deleterious mutation, and to match the depth of the diversity reductions they fit deleterious mutation rates and selected targets higher than independent estimates support~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Our maintenance test shows that recurrent mutation maintains about a twentieth of the variance measured in wild pedigrees \claude{when the annotated functional sequence is the deleterious target and about a quarter when 15\% of the remaining sequence is counted as well}, so the pattern of diversity along a chromosome, which depends on where selected sites lie and how fast selection removes them, can follow mutation--selection--drift balance while the depth of the reduction requires variance from other sources. Along chromosomes, the reduction depends on what maintains the variance~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}\claude{. Our derivation shows that linkage adds to the loss of diversity only through the variance on the chromosome copy that carries a neutral site, and only while that variance stays with the site beyond the generation or two that an unlinked association lasts. The alleles that stay longest are those purged most slowly, which are also the mutations that contribute least to the maintained variance, so the conditions for a large effect of linkage and for a large maintained variance exclude each other. With the measured variance, the linkage maps and the assigned mutational effects, linkage lowers the effective size of every population with a map yet leaves each deficit unexplained, by at least 53-fold at the estimated variance. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test how linkage acts}, since diversity on each chromosome should decrease with the amount of selected sequence per unit of map length, along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test shows that\claude{, at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species,} lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates, like the fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} that Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} studied, carries more additive variance than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, so the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} applies to fitness itself. \claude{Because a new mutation adds variance in proportion to the square of its effect for a time inversely proportional to that effect, it contributes in proportion to its effect whatever its size, so only the deleterious mutation rate and the mean effect set the variance that mutation maintains, and drift can only lower it.} Large-effect mutations hidden from site-frequency spectra, such as transposable-element insertions, could raise the mean effect to the required 16\% only if they made up \claude{a large share} of new mutations. Molecular estimates imply a high deleterious mutation rate in humans~\cite{kondrashov1993}, recently put at 2 to 10 per generation~\cite{matheson2023} and above 3.8 once slightly deleterious mutations that fix are counted~\cite{hernandez2026}, with about 7--9\% of the non-coding genome under purifying selection~\cite{di2025}. \claude{However, counting even 15\% of the unannotated sequence of our assemblies as deleterious leaves the median population with four times the variance that mutation maintains, and in most bird populations the deleterious sequence required would be larger than the genome. Because selection on these mutations is strong relative to drift, drift cannot maintain the excess either.} \claude{The mean effect on which this result depends is the feature of a distribution of fitness effects that site-frequency spectra constrain least, because rare mutations of large effect set it, and we took it from related species. When the target, the mean effect and the estimate vary together, the excess persists in 12 of 18 populations if 8\% of the unannotated sequence is deleterious, 1\% of new mutations have large effects and the estimate is twice the long-term variance, and in 6 under larger departures. The excess is therefore firm in a few populations, those with the largest estimates and birds with small mean effects, and provisional for the median population.} A higher deleterious mutation rate also removes more diversity through background selection\claude{, yet} even with the largest target we considered, the variance that mutation maintains removes at most about half of neutral diversity, so a mutational account narrows the maintenance gap without explaining the missing diversity.

Resolving what maintains the remaining variance requires distinguishing the phenotypic manifestion of selection from its allelic causes (\autoref{sec:quantity}). At the phenotypic level, additive variance in relative fitness is directional selection, governing the evolutionary change of every trait genetically correlated with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}. Under mutation--selection--drift balance, that directional selection reflects nothing more than the continual purging of deleterious alleles; our maintenance test therefore shows that purifying selection accounts for only a small fraction of the directional selection acting in wild pedigrees. A fluctuating or moving phenotypic optimum—consistent with year-to-year shifts in selection on breeding date across 39 bird and mammal populations~\cite{devillemereuil2020}—can supply the rest without requiring a larger mutational target. As a population lags behind a shifting optimum, standing variation in selected traits is continuously converted into additive variance in fitness~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Crucially, recent theory shows that even slow, continuous fluctuations in a trait optimum strongly amplify the underlying quantitative genetic variance itself: optimum displacements transiently reverse the sign of selection on rare alleles, lifting them to higher frequencies and rendering standing variance largely insensitive to the mutational target size precisely when selection is strong relative to drift~\cite{bertram2026}. Two additional mechanisms can elevate fitness variance without expanding the mutational target. First, drift around a balanced equilibrium carries disproportionate weight: a single overdominant or negatively frequency-dependent locus displaced from equilibrium contributes as much additive fitness variance as $10^2$ to $10^4$ loci at mutation--selection balance, particularly in smaller populations~\cite{connallon2026}. Second, migration--selection balance across heterogeneous habitats injects variance whenever immigrant alleles adapted to adjacent environments pull a local population away from its optimum~\cite{lenormand2002}—a dynamic directly documented in three of our Mediterranean blue tit populations, where laying date and other heritable traits diverge across deciduous and evergreen oak mosaics over just a few kilometers despite persistent gene flow~\cite{charmantier2016,perrier2020}. In Fisher's fundamental theorem, a shifting optimum, local maladaptation from gene flow, and environmental change all enter as a deteriorating environment that offsets the selective gain in mean fitness, explaining why high $V_A$ need not produce runaway population growth~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018,bonnet2022}. Yet while environmental deterioration cancels the change in mean fitness within a single generation, it cannot cancel the cumulative genetic drift generated by the underlying allele-frequency changes over coalescent time. Although a pedigree cannot separate balancing selection from a moving optimum, the two mechanisms leave distinct signatures: intermediate-frequency polymorphisms of large effect under balancing selection, versus shifting breeding values masked by phenotypic stasis under a moving optimum~\cite{merila2001}.

Shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that the animal model does not include can inflate pedigree estimates~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}. Conversely, an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection would understate the variance. Because both tests rely on the same long-term variance, which removes more diversity the larger it is but is harder to maintain, correcting an inflated estimate would narrow the maintenance gap and widen the diversity gap by the same factor. Within every population with diversity data, the long-term variance that the diversity test requires is at least 108 times the variance that the maintenance test allows, and at least 81 times with linkage, so no error that scales the estimate up or down lets it pass both. \claude{A large enough error of scale would nonetheless reduce the two results to one already known. If the estimates overstated the long-term variance about twentyfold, the factor that the median population requires to pass the maintenance test, recurrent mutation would maintain that variance, and our diversity test would show only that the variance mutation maintains removes a few percent of neutral diversity at sites unlinked to selected loci.} \claude{Even the diversity test alone is passed only by bighorn sheep and song sparrows, with linkage, if only one adult in ten breeds and the mutation rate lies at the lower end of its interval.} To reconcile the two tests, an assumption of unknown direction, such as discrete generations in iteroparous populations~\cite{engen2005}, a single deme per population, or distributions of fitness effects~\cite{lin2026} and diversity taken from related species, would have to shift \claude{one result relative to the other} by about two orders of magnitude. \claude{The maintenance test alone is less secure, because it comes within a factor of about two of passing in the median population when a larger deleterious target, undetected large-effect mutations and a twofold inflated estimate are combined.} Because the usual criticisms of pedigree estimates describe errors that scale the estimate, the gap reflects more the processes the equilibrium models leave out than error in the estimate.

Predicting diversity loss from fitness variance alone also requires that allele frequencies at selected loci change little while a neutral lineage remains associated with them~\cite{barton2000}—a quasi-steady-state condition satisfied at mutation--selection--drift balance and during polygenic tracking of a moving optimum (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Directional sweeps violate this condition and generate excess drift per unit variance, though their cumulative impact is bounded by the slow rate of molecular substitution. More fundamentally, the multiplicative log-normal projection assumes that the finite population actually contains, in every generation, the extreme upper-tail individuals responsible for the predicted drift. The variance required to explain the observed diversity deficits reaches 70 to 95\% of the threshold at which those required ancestors fall below a single individual per generation. Near that finite-size boundary, coalescence shifts from a steady Kingman process to episodic multiple mergers driven by single exceptional lineages~\cite{eldon2006,neher2013,diamantidis2024}, violating the assumptions of standard variance-to-$N_e$ mappings. Balancing selection violates the quasi-steady-state condition even more directly, because an equilibrium polymorphism carries zero additive variance; its contribution arises entirely from transient displacements across allele frequencies. Slight oscillations of balanced polymorphisms can depress linked diversity across broad chromosomal regions: seasonally fluctuating balancing selection across 100 loci reduces genome-wide $N_e$ by 30 to 76\%, largely independent of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}. Thus, while our conclusion that heritable fitness variance cannot explain the diversity deficit holds under purifying and polygenic directional regimes, whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can bridge the linked diversity gap remains the decisive open question.

The condition for a single long-term variance to pass both tests (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) involves only the deleterious mutation rate, the mean effect of new mutations, the mating system and the loss of diversity. The conflict therefore lies between what mutation can maintain and the diversity that is missing, and the condition applies unchanged to any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness. Temporal change in allele frequencies provides one such estimate, because its covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. Replicate populations founded from a common base population come closer to the long-term variance that both tests concern, because the change in allele frequency due to selection, inferred across replicates over several generations, gives the additive genetic covariance in fitness across replicates and generations rather than a variance in the environment of a single generation~\cite{geetaarun2026}. Long-term evolve-and-resequence experiments provide such replicates, which natural populations rarely do. Repeating the estimate over longer intervals would show how far a short-term variance in fitness differs from its long-term value. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction in effective size directly, because expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree \claude{show how unequal ancestors' shares of later generations are, the inequality} from which every reduction at unlinked sites here is derived~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}. Such pedigrees therefore measure the reduction from all sources of variance in reproductive success, of which heritable variance in fitness is one.
