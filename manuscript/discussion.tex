\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical population genetics theory attributes to such variance. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves the effective size of the median population if, as the animal model behind the estimates assumes, the effects of loci on fitness multiply and breeding values for log-fitness are normally distributed. \claude{No account of the missing diversity relies on heritable variance in fitness alone, and against the diversity each population has lost this halving is a small part, a median of about a fifteenth of each deficit on a logarithmic scale,} even if nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring. \claude{In the median population, mutation--selection--drift balance maintains about a twentieth of the same variance, at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species and with the annotated functional sequence as the deleterious target.} Most of the missing diversity therefore has causes other than heritable variance in fitness, and\claude{, at mean effects taken from related species and the annotated target,} most of the measured variance \claude{would be} maintained by forces other than recurrent mutation, so both equilibria leave most of what they are meant to explain to other processes. The question these estimates raise is not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test shows that the median reduction at unlinked sites lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates, using an approximation that holds while the variance is small. Similarly, across species, Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} found that background selection and recurrent hitchhiking, at \textit{Drosophila} parameters or tenfold stronger ones, also explain only part of the deficit. Most of the rest is likely to come from processes that act over the many generations that neutral diversity reflects, which neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Because diversity reflects the harmonic mean of population size over its genealogy, which bottlenecks and range contractions hold close to the smallest sizes, as Charlesworth and Jensen emphasize, a contemporary census overstates the population size from which we computed the diversity expected without selection. \claude{Trajectories of effective size inferred from genome sequences could show whether these populations passed through such bottlenecks, although they include every source of drift and so cannot by themselves separate demography from heritable variance in fitness.} Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by the same mechanism through which heritable fitness differences increase drift, acting on demes rather than individuals, because a deme's success, like an individual's heritable advantage, persists across generations~\cite{whitlock1997}. \claude{Subdivision into demes that persist and exchange migrants has the opposite effect, raising diversity above the expectation for the total number of breeders}~\cite{whitlock1997}\claude{, so population structure lowers species-wide diversity only through deme turnover or unequal contributions of demes.} Because relatives share their deme, a pedigree within a single deme cannot measure this source of drift, which, like a bottleneck, raises the variance that the diversity test requires without implying that the estimate is too low. \claude{Similarly, Lewin and Eyre-Walker found contemporary effective sizes, which like a pedigree are estimated in a single locality over a few generations, to be unrelated to long-term effective sizes across species, and suggested that gene flow, which links local populations only over longer times, explains the mismatch}~\cite{lewin2026}\claude{.} \claude{Demography and deme turnover are the expected causes of the missing diversity, because they directly reduce the effective size that diversity reflects, so heritable variance in fitness adds a share to theirs rather than competing with them as an explanation.}

Heritable variance in fitness reduces diversity at a neutral site in two ways, through the individual that carries the site, an effect shared by every site, and through the chromosome segment that carries it, an effect that varies along the genome. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024} because they estimate the second effect relative to a genome-wide baseline that already includes the first~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Such maps cannot measure the genome-wide effect, which accounts of the deficit have presumed small~\cite{matheson2024}. A pedigree, by contrast, measures variance in fitness among individuals and so provides the variance on which the genome-wide effect depends. The same maps attribute all selected variance to recurrent deleterious mutation, and to match the depth of the diversity reductions they fit deleterious mutation rates and selected targets higher than independent estimates support~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Our maintenance test shows that recurrent mutation maintains about a twentieth of the variance measured in wild pedigrees \claude{when the annotated functional sequence is the deleterious target and about a quarter when 15\% of the remaining sequence is counted as well}, so the pattern of diversity along a chromosome, which depends on where selected sites lie and how fast selection removes them, can follow mutation--selection--drift balance while the depth of the reduction requires variance from other sources. Along chromosomes, the reduction depends on what maintains the variance~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. It is large only when slowly purged alleles carry the variance, and these are the alleles least able to maintain much of it. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test this prediction, since diversity on each chromosome should decrease with the amount of selected sequence per unit of map length, along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test shows that\claude{, at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species,} lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates, like the fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} that Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} studied, carries more additive variance than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, so the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} applies to fitness itself. Large-effect mutations hidden from site-frequency spectra, such as transposable-element insertions, could raise the mean effect to the required 16\% only if they made up \claude{a large share} of new mutations. Molecular estimates imply a high deleterious mutation rate in humans~\cite{kondrashov1993}, recently put at 2 to 10 per generation~\cite{matheson2023} and above 3.8 once slightly deleterious mutations that fix are counted~\cite{hernandez2026}, with about 7--9\% of the non-coding genome under purifying selection~\cite{di2025}. \claude{However, counting even 15\% of the unannotated sequence of our assemblies as deleterious leaves the median population with four times the variance that mutation maintains, and in most bird populations the deleterious sequence required would be larger than the genome. Because selection on these mutations is strong relative to drift, drift cannot maintain the excess either.} \claude{The mean effect on which this result depends is the feature of a distribution of fitness effects that site-frequency spectra constrain least, because rare mutations of large effect set it, and we took it from related species. When the target, the mean effect and the estimate vary together, the excess persists in 12 of 18 populations if 8\% of the unannotated sequence is deleterious, 1\% of new mutations have large effects and the estimate is twice the long-term variance, and in 6 under larger departures. The excess is therefore firm in a few populations, those with the largest estimates and birds with small mean effects, and provisional for the median population.} A higher deleterious mutation rate also removes more diversity through background selection\claude{, yet} even with the largest target we considered, the variance that mutation maintains removes at most about half of neutral diversity, so a mutational account narrows the maintenance gap without explaining the missing diversity.

What else maintains the variance depends on whether selection is described on phenotypes or on alleles, because the same variance describes both (\autoref{sec:quantity}). On phenotypes, additive variance in fitness is directional selection, predicting change in every trait that covaries genetically with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}. At mutation--selection--drift balance, the same variance is purifying selection on alleles, so \claude{the share of the directional selection implied by the pedigrees that can be purifying is the share that recurrent mutation maintains.} A moving or fluctuating optimum, consistent with the year-to-year variation in selection on breeding date across 39 populations of birds and mammals~\cite{devillemereuil2020}, can maintain the rest by turning standing variation in a trait into heritable variance in fitness through the lag of the population mean behind the optimum~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Drift around a balanced equilibrium can also maintain it, because a single overdominant or negatively frequency-dependent locus displaced from equilibrium contributes as much additive variance in fitness as a hundred to ten thousand loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations~\cite{connallon2026}. \claude{Gene flow between habitats that favor different phenotypes can maintain it as well, because immigrant alleles adapted elsewhere keep a population away from its local optimum}~\cite{lenormand2002}\claude{. Three of our populations are Mediterranean blue tits, which breed in a mosaic of deciduous and evergreen oak forest between which laying date and other heritable traits have diverged over a few kilometers despite strong and long-lasting gene flow}~\cite{charmantier2016,perrier2020}\claude{, the setting in which immigrants would add such variance.} A moving optimum is one form of the deteriorating environment of Fisher's fundamental theorem, through which Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} and earlier reviews~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018} explained why a large heritable variance in fitness need not show in population growth. Such an environment can offset the gain in mean fitness that selection produces but not the drift that its allele-frequency changes add each generation, so neutral diversity records the variance accumulated over its genealogy, whereas the theorem describes a single generation. A pedigree cannot tell these sources of variance apart. They nonetheless predict different patterns: large-effect variants at intermediate frequency under balancing selection, and, under a changing environment, breeding values that change while phenotypes may not, one explanation offered for evolutionary stasis in the wild~\cite{merila2001}.

Shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that the animal model does not include can inflate pedigree estimates~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}. Conversely, an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection would understate the variance. Because both tests rely on the same long-term variance, which removes more diversity the larger it is but is harder to maintain, correcting an inflated estimate would narrow the maintenance gap and widen the diversity gap by the same factor. Within every population with diversity data, the long-term variance that the diversity test requires is at least 108 times the variance that the maintenance test allows, and at least 81 times with linkage, so no error that scales the estimate up or down lets it pass both. \claude{A large enough error of scale would nonetheless reduce the two results to one already known. If the estimates overstated the long-term variance about twentyfold, the factor that the median population requires to pass the maintenance test, recurrent mutation would maintain that variance, and our diversity test would show only that the variance mutation maintains removes a few percent of neutral diversity at sites unlinked to selected loci.} \claude{Even the diversity test alone is passed only by bighorn sheep and song sparrows, with linkage, if only one adult in ten breeds and the mutation rate lies at the lower end of its interval.} To reconcile the two tests, an assumption of unknown direction, such as discrete generations in iteroparous populations~\cite{engen2005}, a single deme per population, or distributions of fitness effects~\cite{lin2026} and diversity taken from related species, would have to shift \claude{one result relative to the other} by about two orders of magnitude. \claude{The maintenance test alone is less secure, because it comes within a factor of about two of passing in the median population when a larger deleterious target, undetected large-effect mutations and a twofold inflated estimate are combined.} Because the usual criticisms of pedigree estimates describe errors that scale the estimate, the gap reflects more the processes the equilibrium models leave out than error in the estimate.

Predicting the loss of diversity from the variance alone also requires that allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while a neutral site remains associated with them~\cite{barton2000}, a condition met at mutation--selection--drift balance and by a polygenic response to a changing environment (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but their substitutions are bounded by the slow rate of molecular evolution. \claude{The same prediction requires that a population contain, in every generation, the ancestors whose fitness produces the reduction. If loci act multiplicatively, these ancestors lie several standard deviations above the mean, and the variance that each observed deficit requires is 70 to 95\% of the variance at which they become rarer than one per generation. Near that limit, coalescence comes in bursts from single exceptional lineages rather than at a steady rate, as in species with highly skewed numbers of offspring}~\cite{eldon2006,diamantidis2024} \claude{and in rapidly adapting populations}~\cite{neher2013}\claude{, whose genealogies have multiple mergers and an excess of rare variants. A variance large enough to explain the missing diversity would therefore also change the shape of the genealogy, which the frequency spectra of these species can test with methods that distinguish multiple mergers from population growth and selection}~\cite{eldon2015,freund2023,korfmann2024}\claude{.} Balancing selection meets the condition least well, because its variance exists only away from equilibrium and its effects change with allele frequency. Slight fluctuations of balanced polymorphisms reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, possibly more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness. Balancing selection that fluctuates with the seasons reduces genome-wide effective size by about 30\% for 100 loci in one model and by about 50\%, up to 76\%, in another, largely independently of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}. These reductions are far less than the deficits observed here, but the full effect of such selection at linked sites is not yet known. Our conclusion that the measured variance is too small to explain the missing diversity therefore holds for a variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance, and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained. Whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is the open question most likely to change this conclusion.

The condition for a single long-term variance to pass both tests (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) involves only the deleterious mutation rate, the mean effect of new mutations, the mating system and the loss of diversity. The conflict therefore lies between what mutation can maintain and the diversity that is missing, and the condition applies unchanged to any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness. Temporal change in allele frequencies provides one such estimate, because its covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. Replicate populations founded from a common base population come closer to the long-term variance that both tests concern, because the change in allele frequency due to selection, inferred across replicates over several generations, gives the additive genetic covariance in fitness across replicates and generations rather than a variance in the environment of a single generation~\cite{geetaarun2026}. Long-term evolve-and-resequence experiments provide such replicates, which natural populations rarely do. Repeating the estimate over longer intervals would show how far a short-term variance in fitness differs from its long-term value. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction in effective size directly, because expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give the variance of long-term contributions from which every reduction at unlinked sites here is derived~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}. Such pedigrees therefore measure the reduction from all sources of variance in reproductive success, of which heritable variance in fitness is one.
