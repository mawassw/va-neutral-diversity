\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations (Bonnet et al. 2022) against the two equilibria that classical theory attaches to such a variance, and they pass neither. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves effective size in the median population and leaves most of the observed deficits unexplained, even under the multiplicative bound. Yet it is more than an order of magnitude larger than mutation--selection--drift balance can maintain at the effects of deleterious mutations measured in related species. The condition that would reconcile the two equilibria, Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, contains no pedigree estimate, so the conflict lies between the mutational parameters and the observed diversity themselves, and the pedigree estimates only locate each population between its two ends. The question these estimates raise is therefore not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Heritable variance in fitness at the magnitude that pedigrees measure explains little of the diversity these populations are missing. The reduction it causes is of the same order as the demographic one: a median $N_e/N$ relative to breeding adults of 0.5--0.6, against demographic ratios $N_{eV}/N$ of 0.1--0.5 (Frankham 1995). Even if the two multiply, they account for a deficit of about 20-fold at most in the median population, against observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. The rest must come from processes that act over the coalescent window and that neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Linked selection does not account for it across species (Buffalo 2021), and our results remove heritable variance in fitness at the measured magnitude from the remaining candidates. Because no input to our predictions comes from diversity data (Methods), that conclusion was not calibrated against the deficit it is tested against.

Separating the global reduction from the local one reconciles two observations about linked selection. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes (Murphy et al. 2023; Buffalo and Kern 2024), yet across species linked selection does not account for the deficit (Buffalo 2021). Such maps capture the local reduction, which acts through the chromosome segment carrying a neutral site and varies with the density of functional sequence per Morgan. Because the global reduction acts through the individual instead, every neutral site shares it, and it is absorbed into the reference level against which the maps measure local diversity (McVicker et al. 2009). Methods that quantify linked selection cannot see it, which is one reason it has been left out of accounts of the deficit (Matheson and Masel 2024), whereas a pedigree, which measures variance among individuals, supplies exactly what it needs. In birds the measured variance deepens the local reduction substantially, but only if weakly selected alleles carry it. Carried instead by alleles of the effect that would maintain it, or reduced to the variance that MSDB maintains, it changes predicted diversity by only a few percent. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test the local prediction, since diversity should decrease with the selected target per Morgan along one curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test extends to lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates the conclusion that Charlesworth (2015) reached for fitness components in \textit{Drosophila}: there is more additive variance in fitness than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain. The measured variance requires the average new mutation in the functional genome to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 15\%, a median 18 times the mean effect in the DFE assigned to each population. A larger mutational target cannot close that difference, because our $U_d$ already treats every mutation in the exome and in an equal regulatory target as deleterious (Methods). Nor can a tail of strong effects hidden from site-frequency spectra, which would need a heterozygous effect of 0.5 in about 30\% of new mutations to raise the mean that far. If the measured variance persists, most of it is therefore maintained by something other than recurrent deleterious mutation.

What else maintains it depends on whether selection is described on phenotypes or on alleles, because the same variance describes both (\autoref{sec:quantity}). On phenotypes, a non-zero $V_A$ is directional selection, predicting change in every trait that covaries genetically with fitness (Robertson 1966; Price 1970; Morrissey et al. 2010), which is how the estimates have been interpreted as contemporary adaptation. At MSDB it is purifying selection on alleles, and $U_d\bar{s}$ is at once the variance, the gain in mean breeding value for fitness that selection makes, and the loss that new mutations cause. Our maintenance test therefore asks how much of the directional selection implied by the pedigrees can be purifying selection, and in the median population the answer is about a twentieth. If the variance persists, a changing environment can cancel the rest by favoring alleles once climate, coevolving competitors and parasites, or the population's own density change; this is positive or fluctuating selection, and adaptation in the sense of Bonnet et al. (2022). So can drift around a balanced equilibrium, which displaces overdominant and negatively frequency-dependent polymorphisms far enough that one balanced locus contributes as much $V_A(w)$ as hundreds to thousands of loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations (Connallon and Czuppon 2026). Migration among the demes of a metapopulation would instead put immigration in the place of mutation. A pedigree cannot tell these apart, but they leave different traces: large-effect variants at intermediate frequency under balancing selection, and under a changing environment breeding values that change while phenotypes may not, one explanation offered for evolutionary stasis in the wild (Merilä et al. 2001).

Because both tests concern the same long-term variance and respond to its rescaling $c$ in opposite directions, no correction to the pedigree estimate passes both. Pedigree estimates absorb shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that are not modelled (Kruuk and Hadfield 2007; Stopher et al. 2012; Baud et al. 2022; Mawass and Milot 2025), and they are conditional by construction on the environments sampled (\autoref{sec:quantity}). An estimate inflated by these sources implies $c < 1$, easing maintenance but widening the diversity gap by the same factor, whereas one that misses episodes of strong selection implies $c > 1$ and does the reverse. Within every population the two required values of $c$ lie more than a hundredfold apart, so the result holds against the standard criticism of pedigree $V_A$ whatever the size of the bias. What the gap measures is not error in the estimate but what the two equilibrium models leave out.

According to Table~\ref{tab:c}, the rescaling becomes a diagnostic: every quadrant but the upper left names a departure from equilibrium, and three processes documented in wild populations each correspond to one. A moving or fluctuating optimum, common for breeding date across 39 long-term studies of birds and mammals (de Villemereuil et al. 2020), supplies variance in fitness that mutation does not and places a lagging population in the right-hand column. A steady lag keeps that variance stationary and, if polygenic, meets the conditions of \autoref{sec:unlinked}, whereas a fluctuating optimum makes it vary and diversity responds to its mean. Selection that reverses direction from one generation to the next also cuts an ancestor's advantage short, lowering $Q$ below two ($2/3$ under strict alternation). Models of seasonally fluctuating selection reduce genome-wide effective size by only 30--50\%, largely independently of census size (Wittmann et al. 2023; Johnson et al. 2026). Bottlenecks and range contractions instead pull the harmonic mean of $N$ over the coalescent window toward the smallest sizes (Charlesworth and Jensen 2022; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), so a contemporary census overstates the $N$ against which $\delta$ is measured. That places a population in the lower row whatever its variance, and if heritable variance in fitness peaks during crashes, its $1/N$-weighted mean also exceeds its time average (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity (Pannell and Charlesworth 2000) by Robertson's mechanism at the scale of the deme, because a deme's fortune persists across generations and so amplifies drift (Whitlock and Barton 1997; Barton 2000). Relatives share their deme, so a pedigree cannot measure this term, which like a bottleneck raises $c_\text{div}$ without implying that the estimate is low. Of the three, demography and turnover alone can remove diversity on the scale observed, and only the moving optimum bears on the maintenance test.

Neutral diversity records the variance in a way that mean fitness does not. Fisher's fundamental theorem equates the partial change in mean fitness due to selection with $V_A$ each generation, but that change can be offset. New mutation offsets it at MSDB, and an environment that deteriorates at the same rate, Fisher's own deterioration term, offsets it otherwise, so a large $V_A$ need leave no trace in population growth. Neither can offset its effect on diversity, because the allele-frequency changes that selection makes happen whatever the environment does, and each generation of them adds drift. Diversity therefore integrates the variance over the coalescent window whatever happens to mean fitness, and in these populations it shows how little of the missing diversity the measured variance explains, although it cannot exclude that variance (\autoref{sec:res_ceiling}).

The variance needed to explain a loss of diversity, $V_{A,\delta}$, depends only on $\delta$ and the mating system, so any population with a diversity estimate can be placed against it without our estimates of $V_A$. Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, which contains only the mutational parameters and the diversity ratio, sets the same standard for any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness, from pedigrees or from sequence data: it must pass both tests at once. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction without passing through $V_A$. Expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give the variance of long-term contributions, and so the reduction in effective size, directly (Wray and Thompson 1990; Chen et al. 2019).

Finally, these results should be read in light of the assumptions that remain (\autoref{sec:approx}). Generations are discrete, whereas the populations are iteroparous and age structure changes the mapping from variance in family size to effective size (Engen et al. 2005), and each study population is treated as a single deme. Excluding the Z chromosome, hemizygous in female birds and recombining only in males, understates the linked reduction, as would clustering of selected loci in regions of low recombination rather than the uniform placement we assume (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}). Each population's DFE is assigned from related species, because few wild vertebrates have their own (Lin et al. 2026). The diversity ratio rests on census sizes estimated from density and range (Buffalo 2021) and, in four populations, on diversity measured in another population of the species. The estimates count fitness over each individual's whole life, whereas the reduction is computed for breeding adults (\autoref{sec:quantity}), so heritable survival before breeding is expressed in a breeder's offspring and its advantage persists one generation less. Treating it as part of a single variance therefore overstates its contribution to the reduction, unless survival and fecundity are negatively correlated genetically, as under a life-history trade-off. None of these approaches the gap between the two tests, which is at least 108-fold within any population without linkage and 81-fold with it.

The Santiago--Caballero prediction (Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}) requires that allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while an association persists (Barton 2000), and MSDB meets that condition at unlinked sites while also holding $V_A$ constant over the coalescent window (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). A polygenic response to a changing environment moves each allele little, as the infinitesimal model assumes, and Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic} and the multiplicative bound cover the changing amount of variance. Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but substitutions are bounded by the slow rate of molecular evolution (Barton 2000), and models of recurrent hitchhiking do not account for the deficit across species (Buffalo 2021). The balancing selection that Connallon and Czuppon (2026) describe meets the condition least well, because its variance exists only away from equilibrium and its effects change with allele frequency. Slight fluctuations of balanced polymorphisms also reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, possibly more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness, by an amount not yet known (Barton 2000). The diversity verdict therefore holds under its own assumptions for the variance that MSDB maintains, the regime in which we computed the local reduction as background selection, and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained. Whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is the open question most likely to change it.
