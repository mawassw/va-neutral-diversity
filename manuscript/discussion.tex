\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations (Bonnet et al. 2022) against the two equilibria that classical theory attaches to such a variance, and they pass neither. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves effective size in the median population and leaves the observed deficits unexplained, even under the multiplicative bound, yet it is more than an order of magnitude larger than mutation--selection--drift balance can maintain at the effects of deleterious mutations measured in related species. Because the condition that would reconcile the two equilibria, Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, contains no pedigree estimate, the conflict lies between the mutational parameters and the observed diversity themselves, and the pedigree estimates only locate each population between its two ends. The question these estimates raise is therefore not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test removes heritable variance in fitness at the measured magnitude from the candidate explanations for the deficit, which across species already exclude linked selection, whether background selection or recurrent hitchhiking (Buffalo 2021), so the rest must come from processes that act over the coalescent window and that neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Separating the global reduction from the local reduction reconciles Buffalo's result with maps of background selection, which explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes (Murphy et al. 2023; Buffalo and Kern 2024). Such maps capture the local reduction, which acts through the chromosome segment carrying a neutral site, whereas the global reduction acts through the individual, so every neutral site shares it and it is absorbed into the reference level against which the maps measure local diversity (McVicker et al. 2009). Methods that quantify linked selection therefore cannot detect it, one reason it has been left out of accounts of the deficit (Matheson and Masel 2024), but a pedigree, which measures variance among individuals, supplies exactly what it requires. The local reduction is where the process that maintains the variance matters, because a linked association lasts only as long as the selected alleles do (Li and Berg 2026), and it is large only when weakly selected alleles carry the variance, the alleles least able to maintain it. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test the local prediction, since diversity should decrease with the selected target per Morgan along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test extends to lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates the conclusion that Charlesworth (2015) reached for fitness components in \textit{Drosophila}, that there is more additive variance in fitness than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, and so brings the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained (Johnson and Barton 2005) to fitness itself. The measured variance would require the average new mutation in the functional genome to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 15\%, a median 18 times the mean effect in the DFE assigned to each population. Because our deleterious mutation rate already counts every mutation in the exome and in an equal regulatory target, a larger mutational target cannot resolve that difference, and neither can a tail of large effects hidden from site-frequency spectra. If the measured variance persists, most of it is therefore maintained by something other than recurrent deleterious mutation.

What else maintains the variance depends on whether selection is described on phenotypes or on alleles, because the same variance describes both (\autoref{sec:quantity}). On phenotypes, a non-zero $V_A$ is directional selection, predicting change in every trait that covaries genetically with fitness (Robertson 1966; Price 1970; Morrissey et al. 2010). At MSDB it is purifying selection on alleles, so our maintenance test asks how much of the directional selection implied by the pedigrees can be purifying, and in the median population the answer is about a twentieth. A changing environment can supply the rest. A moving or fluctuating optimum, common for breeding date across 39 long-term studies of birds and mammals (de Villemereuil et al. 2020), turns standing variation in a trait into heritable variance in fitness through the lag of the population mean behind the optimum (B\"urger and Lynch 1995; Lande and Shannon 1996). Its effect on diversity is modest, because models of seasonally fluctuating selection reduce genome-wide effective size by only 30--50\%, largely independently of census size (Wittmann et al. 2023; Johnson et al. 2026). Drift around a balanced equilibrium can also maintain the variance, displacing overdominant and negatively frequency-dependent polymorphisms far enough that a single balanced locus contributes as much $V_A(w)$ as hundreds to thousands of loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations (Connallon and Czuppon 2026). A pedigree cannot tell these apart, but they leave different traces, large-effect variants at intermediate frequency under balancing selection and, under a changing environment, breeding values that change while phenotypes may not, one explanation offered for evolutionary stasis in the wild (Merilä et al. 2001).

Bonnet et al. (2022) interpreted their estimates through Fisher's fundamental theorem, as the rate at which selection raises mean fitness and so as rapid contemporary adaptation, an interpretation shared by reviews of the evolution of fitness in the wild (Burt 1995; Hendry et al. 2018). The theorem equates the partial change in mean fitness due to selection with $V_A$ each generation, but that change can be offset, by new mutation at MSDB and otherwise by an environment that deteriorates at the same rate, Fisher's own deterioration term, so a large $V_A$ need leave no trace in population growth. Neither can offset its effect on neutral diversity, because the allele-frequency changes that selection makes happen whatever the environment does, and each generation of them adds drift. Diversity therefore integrates the variance over the coalescent window independently of mean fitness, a record that the theorem, a statement about a single generation, does not provide. In these populations that record shows how little of the missing diversity the measured variance explains, although it cannot exclude a variance of that size.

Because both tests concern the same long-term variance and respond to its rescaling $c$ in opposite directions, no correction to the pedigree estimate passes both. Pedigree estimates absorb shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that are not modeled (Kruuk and Hadfield 2007; Stopher et al. 2012; Baud et al. 2022; Mawass and Milot 2025). An estimate inflated by these sources implies $c < 1$, easing maintenance but widening the diversity gap by the same factor, whereas an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection implies $c > 1$ and does the reverse. Within every population the two required values of $c$ lie more than a hundredfold apart, so the result holds against the standard criticism of pedigree $V_A$ whatever the size of the bias, and the gap measures not error in the estimate but what the two equilibrium models leave out.

Demographic departures from equilibrium fail the diversity test whatever the variance, because they lower diversity without changing the estimate. Bottlenecks and range contractions pull the harmonic mean of $N$ over the coalescent window toward the smallest sizes (Charlesworth and Jensen 2022; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), so a contemporary census overstates the $N$ against which the diversity ratio is measured. Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity (Pannell and Charlesworth 2000) by Robertson's mechanism at the scale of the deme, because a deme's fortune persists across generations and so increases drift (Whitlock and Barton 1997). Relatives share their deme, so a pedigree cannot measure this term, which like a bottleneck raises $c_\text{div}$ without implying that the estimate is low. Of the processes considered here, only demography and deme turnover can remove diversity on the scale observed, and only a changing environment bears on the maintenance test.

The variance needed to explain a loss of diversity, $V_{A,\delta}$, depends only on the diversity ratio and the mating system, so any population with a diversity estimate can be placed against it without our estimates of $V_A$. Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, which contains only the mutational parameters and the diversity ratio, sets the same standard for any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness, which must pass both tests at once, whether it comes from pedigrees or from temporal allele-frequency change, whose covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci (Buffalo and Coop 2019, 2020). Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction without passing through $V_A$, because expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give the variance of long-term contributions, and so the reduction in effective size, directly (Wray and Thompson 1990; Chen et al. 2019).

These results also rest on assumptions that remain (\autoref{sec:approx}). Generations are discrete, whereas the populations are iteroparous and age structure changes the mapping from variance in family size to effective size (Engen et al. 2005), and each study population is treated as a single deme. Excluding the Z chromosome, hemizygous in female birds and recombining only in males, understates the linked reduction, as would clustering of selected loci in regions of low recombination rather than the uniform placement we assume (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}). Each population's DFE is assigned from related species, because few wild vertebrates have their own (Lin et al. 2026), and the diversity ratio rests on census sizes estimated from density and range and, in four populations, on diversity measured in another population of the species (Methods). The estimates count fitness over each individual's whole life, whereas the reduction is computed for breeding adults, so heritable survival before breeding is expressed in a breeder's offspring and its advantage persists one generation less. Treating it as part of a single variance therefore overstates its contribution to the reduction, unless survival and fecundity are negatively correlated genetically, as under a life-history trade-off. Each of these is small against the gap between the two tests, which is at least 108-fold within any population without linkage and 81-fold with it.

The Santiago--Caballero prediction (Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}) also requires that allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while an association persists (Barton 2000), a condition that MSDB and a polygenic response to a changing environment both meet (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but their substitutions are bounded by the slow rate of molecular evolution. Balancing selection meets the condition least well, because its variance exists only away from equilibrium and its effects change with allele frequency, and slight fluctuations of balanced polymorphisms reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, possibly more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness, by an amount not yet known. The diversity verdict therefore holds under its own assumptions for the variance that MSDB maintains, the regime in which we computed the local reduction as background selection, and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained. Whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is the open question most likely to change it.
