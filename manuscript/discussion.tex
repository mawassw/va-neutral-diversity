\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical theory attaches to such a variance, and they pass neither. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves effective size in the median population under the multiplicative model and leaves the observed deficits unexplained, even under the multiplicative bound, yet in the median population it is more than an order of magnitude larger than mutation--selection--drift balance can maintain at the effects of deleterious mutations measured in related species. Because the condition that would reconcile the two equilibria, Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, contains no pedigree estimate, the conflict lies between the mutational parameters and the observed diversity themselves, and the pedigree estimates only locate each population between its two ends. The question these estimates raise is therefore not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test leaves heritable variance in fitness at the measured magnitude able to explain only a small part of the deficit. Across species, background selection and recurrent hitchhiking at \textit{Drosophila} parameters, or tenfold stronger, also leave much of it unexplained~\cite{buffalo2021}, so most of the rest is likely to come from processes that act over the coalescent window and that neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Separating the global reduction from the local reduction can reconcile Buffalo's result with maps of background selection, which explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Such maps capture the local reduction, which acts through the chromosome segment carrying a neutral site, whereas the global reduction acts through the individual, so every neutral site shares it and it is absorbed into the reference level against which the maps measure local diversity~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Methods that quantify linked selection therefore cannot separate it from that reference level, which may be one reason it has been omitted from accounts of the deficit, where it has been presumed small~\cite{matheson2024}, but a pedigree, which measures variance among individuals, supplies what it requires. The local reduction is where the process that maintains the variance matters, because a linked association lasts only as long as the selected alleles do~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}, and it is large only when slowly purged alleles carry the variance, the alleles least able to maintain it. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test the local prediction, since diversity should decrease with the selected target per Morgan along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test extends to lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates the conclusion that Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} reached for fitness components in \textit{Drosophila}, that there is more additive variance in fitness than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, and so brings the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} to fitness itself. The measured variance would require the average new mutation in the functional genome to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 16\%, a median 21 times the mean effect in the DFE assigned to each population. Our deleterious mutation rate counts every point mutation in the exome and in an equal regulatory target as deleterious, so mutation alone could resolve that difference only with a deleterious target about 20 times larger, or with a class of large-effect mutations that site-frequency spectra do not detect, such as the transposable-element insertions that contribute to the variance Charlesworth found in \textit{Drosophila}, which would have to make up about 30\% of new mutations at a heterozygous effect of 0.5. If the measured variance persists, most of it is therefore maintained by something other than recurrent deleterious mutation.

What else maintains the variance depends on whether selection is described on phenotypes or on alleles, because the same variance describes both (\autoref{sec:quantity}). On phenotypes, a non-zero $V_A$ is directional selection, predicting change in every trait that covaries genetically with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}. At MSDB it is purifying selection on alleles, so our maintenance test asks how much of the directional selection implied by the pedigrees can be purifying, and in the median population the answer is about a twentieth. A changing environment can supply the rest. A moving or fluctuating optimum, consistent with the year-to-year variation in selection on breeding date across 39 populations of birds and mammals~\cite{devillemereuil2020}, turns standing variation in a trait into heritable variance in fitness through the lag of the population mean behind the optimum~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Drift around a balanced equilibrium can also maintain the variance, displacing overdominant and negatively frequency-dependent polymorphisms far enough that a single balanced locus contributes as much $V_A(w)$ as hundreds to thousands of loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations~\cite{connallon2026}. Balancing selection that fluctuates with the seasons reduces genome-wide effective size substantially, by about 30\% for 100 loci in one model and about 50\%, up to 75\%, in another, largely independently of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}, but by far less than the deficits observed here. A pedigree cannot tell these apart, but they leave different traces, large-effect variants at intermediate frequency under balancing selection and, under a changing environment, breeding values that change while phenotypes may not, one explanation offered for evolutionary stasis in the wild~\cite{merila2001}.

Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} interpreted their estimates through Fisher's fundamental theorem, as the rate at which selection raises mean fitness and so as rapid contemporary adaptation. The theorem equates that partial change with $V_A$ each generation, and Bonnet et al., like earlier reviews of the evolution of fitness in the wild~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018}, noted that it can be offset by mutation, gene flow or an environment that deteriorates at the same rate, Fisher's own deterioration term, so a large $V_A$ need leave no trace in population growth. None of these offsets its effect on neutral diversity, because the allele-frequency changes that selection makes happen whatever the environment does, and each generation of them adds drift. Diversity therefore integrates the variance over the coalescent window independently of mean fitness, a record that the theorem, a statement about a single generation, does not provide. In these populations that record shows how little of the missing diversity the measured variance explains, although it cannot exclude a variance of that size.

Because both tests concern the same long-term variance and respond to its rescaling $c$ in opposite directions, no single rescaling of the pedigree estimate passes both in any population we tested. Pedigree estimates absorb shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that are not modeled~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}. An estimate inflated by these sources implies $c < 1$, easing maintenance but widening the diversity gap by the same factor, whereas an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection implies $c > 1$ and does the reverse. Within every population with diversity data the two required values of $c$ lie more than a hundredfold apart, so the result holds against any bias that rescales the estimate, whatever its size, which is the form the standard criticism of pedigree $V_A$ takes, and the gap reflects what the two equilibrium models leave out more than error in the estimate.

Demographic departures from equilibrium can fail the diversity test whatever the variance, because they lower diversity without changing the estimate. Bottlenecks and range contractions pull the harmonic mean of $N$ over the coalescent window toward the smallest sizes~\cite{charlesworth2022}, so a contemporary census overstates the $N$ against which the diversity ratio is measured. Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by Robertson's mechanism at the scale of the deme, because a deme's fortune persists across generations and so increases drift~\cite{whitlock1997}. Relatives share their deme, so a pedigree within a single deme does not capture this term, which like a bottleneck raises $c_\text{div}$ without implying that the estimate is low. Of the processes considered here, demography and deme turnover are the likeliest to remove diversity on the scale observed, because they act on the coalescent effective size directly, whereas a changing environment bears mainly on the maintenance test.

The variance needed to explain a loss of diversity, $V_{A,\delta}$, depends only on the diversity ratio and the mating system, so any population with a diversity estimate can be placed against it without our estimates of $V_A$. Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, which contains only the mutational parameters and the diversity ratio, sets the same standard for any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness, which must pass both tests at once, whether it comes from pedigrees or from temporal allele-frequency change, whose covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction without passing through $V_A$, because expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give the variance of long-term contributions directly~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}, and so the reduction in effective size from all sources of variance in reproductive success, of which heritable variance in fitness is one.

We chose the approximations so that each test is biased toward passing, which makes a failed test conservative (\autoref{sec:approx}). The diversity prediction uses the multiplicative bound, $\kappa_\text{max}$, $Q = 2$ despite purging, all non-Poisson variance in family size, Haldane's map, a male-only macaque map, and the measured variance on the linked segments, each of which overstates the reduction; the maintenance test uses the strong-selection limit and a deleterious target that counts the whole exome and an equal regulatory target, which overstate what MSDB maintains. Assigning all of $V_A$ to the autosomes also places on them the share that the Z or X chromosome carries, which slightly overstates the autosomal linked reduction. A few choices go the other way. Census sizes that count all individuals rather than breeding adults lower $\delta$ by a factor of 2 to 10, and the two literature values of $\pi$ from MAF-filtered SNP sets are probably biased low, although by much less; a tenfold increase in $\delta$ leaves every population short of its deficit at unlinked sites, and with linkage it brings bighorn sheep and song sparrows within a few percent of theirs. The bird census sizes err in the opposite direction, by about 1.2 orders of magnitude, so in birds the two census biases partly offset. On the maintenance side, counting the autosomes only lowers $U_d$ by 4 to 6\% relative to a target that includes the Z or X chromosome, and the direct mutation rates of the primary analysis are 7--60\% below the phylogenetic rates, which raises $s^*$ (median 0.16, against 0.11) while raising $\delta$ and so favoring the diversity test. Biases of this size could change the maintenance verdict only in snow voles, red squirrels and meerkats, where $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds $U_d\bar{s}$ by less than threefold; the next closest population exceeds it about ninefold. Two departures that are not measured in these populations would raise the reduction instead, a right-skewed distribution of breeding values, which raises the retention above one (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}), and selected loci clustered in regions of low recombination rather than placed independently of it (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}).

Other assumptions have no known direction. Generations are discrete, whereas the populations are iteroparous and age structure changes the mapping from variance in family size to effective size~\cite{engen2005}, and each study population is treated as a single deme. Each population's DFE is assigned from related species, because few wild vertebrates have their own~\cite{lin2026}, and in four populations the diversity ratio rests on diversity measured in another population of the species (Methods). The estimates count fitness over each individual's whole life, whereas the reduction is computed for breeding adults, so heritable survival before breeding is expressed in a breeder's offspring and its advantage persists one generation less. Treating it as part of a single variance therefore overstates its contribution to the reduction, unless survival and fecundity are negatively correlated genetically, as under a life-history trade-off. Within every population with diversity data the two tests are at least 108-fold apart without linkage and 81-fold with it, so reconciling them would require an assumption to shift a result by about two orders of magnitude.

The Santiago--Caballero prediction (Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}) also requires that allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while an association persists~\cite{barton2000}, a condition that MSDB and a polygenic response to a changing environment both meet (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but their substitutions are bounded by the slow rate of molecular evolution. Balancing selection meets the condition least well, because its variance exists only away from equilibrium and its effects change with allele frequency, and slight fluctuations of balanced polymorphisms reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, possibly more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness; models of seasonally fluctuating balancing selection put the genome-wide effect at tens of percent (above), but its full extent at linked sites is not yet known. The diversity verdict therefore holds under its own assumptions for the variance that MSDB maintains, the regime in which we computed the local reduction as background selection, and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained. Whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is the open question most likely to change it.
