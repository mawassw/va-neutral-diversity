\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical population genetics theory attributes to such variance. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves the effective size of the median population if, as the animal model behind the estimates assumes, the effects of loci on fitness multiply and breeding values for log-fitness are normally distributed. Compared against the diversity each population has lost, however, it explains only a small part of each deficit, even if nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring. In the median population, the same variance is more than an order of magnitude larger than mutation--selection--drift balance can maintain at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species. Most of the missing diversity therefore has causes other than heritable variance in fitness, and most of the measured variance must be maintained by forces other than recurrent mutation, so both equilibria leave most of what they are meant to explain to other processes. The question these estimates raise is not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test shows that the median reduction at unlinked sites lies within the 16 to 55\% range that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates under a small-variance approximation. Similarly, across species, Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} found that background selection and recurrent hitchhiking—even at tenfold Drosophila intensities—explain only part of the deficit. Most of the remainder likely stems from processes acting over the deep genealogical timescales that neutral diversity integrates, which neither a multi-decade pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Because diversity reflects the harmonic mean of effective size over coalescent time, historical bottlenecks and range contractions anchor diversity near minimum population sizes, causing contemporary censuses to overstate the selection-free baseline~\cite{charlesworth2022}. At the metapopulation scale, recurrent deme extinction and recolonization depress species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by the exact mechanism through which heritable fitness differences accelerate drift: differential reproductive success that persists across generations, acting among demes rather than individuals~\cite{whitlock1997}. Because relatives within a study site share a deme, a local pedigree cannot capture metapopulation turnover. Consistent with this timescale disconnect, Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026} found that contemporary effective sizes estimated within single localities are uncorrelated with long-term coalescent effective sizes across species, attributing the mismatch to gene flow connecting subpopulations over longer evolutionary windows. Historical demography and deme turnover thus remain the primary drivers of the diversity deficit.

Heritable variance in fitness reduces neutral diversity at two scales: a genome-wide reduction driven by unlinked loci across individuals, and a local reduction driven by physical linkage along chromosomes. Maps of background selection capture much of the chromosomal variation in human diversity~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024} because they estimate local linked reductions relative to a genome-wide baseline that already absorbs the unlinked effect~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Because classical background selection models focus on strong purifying selection—where unlinked effects are negligible—accounts of Lewontin's paradox have treated the unlinked baseline reduction as minor~\cite{matheson2024}. A pedigree, by contrast, directly measures individual fitness variance and shows that the unlinked reduction alone roughly halves $N_e$. Furthermore, to match the depth of local diversity troughs solely through recurrent deleterious mutation, background selection maps must fit deleterious mutation rates and target sizes higher than independent molecular estimates support~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Our maintenance test shows that recurrent mutation sustains only about a twentieth of the additive fitness variance measured in wild pedigrees. Consequently, while the spatial profile of diversity along chromosomes tracks the density and purging rate of deleterious mutations, the absolute depth of the reduction draws on fitness variance maintained by other forces~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test this directly: chromosomal diversity should decline with selected sequence per map unit along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test shows that lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates, like the fitness components of Drosophila analyzed by Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015}, harbors far more additive variance than recurrent deleterious mutation can sustain—extending the classical problem of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} to fitness itself. Unobserved large-effect mutations, such as transposable-element insertions hidden from site-frequency spectra, could raise the mean deleterious effect to the required 16\% only if they constituted the bulk of new mutations. Alternatively, bridging the gap through mutational influx alone would require an implausibly large selective target. Even in humans, where molecular estimates suggest high deleterious mutation rates ($U \approx 2$--$10$ per generation) and purifying selection across 7--9\% of the non-coding genome~\cite{kondrashov1993,matheson2023,di2025,hernandez2026}, the implied mutational input falls short. In our study species, assigning deleterious effects to even 15\% of all unannotated assembly sequence still leaves the median population with four times the variance that mutation can maintain, and in most bird populations the required functional target would exceed the entire genome. Because purifying selection on these alleles is strong relative to drift ($2N_e s \gg 1$), genetic drift cannot sustain the excess variance either. Moreover, while inflating the deleterious mutation rate increases diversity loss via background selection, even the largest mutational target we considered removes at most half of neutral diversity—narrowing the maintenance gap without resolving the diversity deficit.

Resolving what maintains the remaining variance requires distinguishing the phenotypic manifestion of selection from its allelic causes (\autoref{sec:quantity}). At the phenotypic level, additive variance in relative fitness is directional selection, governing the evolutionary change of every trait genetically correlated with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}. Under mutation--selection--drift balance, that directional selection reflects nothing more than the continual purging of deleterious alleles; our maintenance test therefore shows that purifying selection accounts for only a small fraction of the directional selection acting in wild pedigrees. A fluctuating or moving phenotypic optimum—consistent with year-to-year shifts in selection on breeding date across 39 bird and mammal populations~\cite{devillemereuil2020}—can supply the rest without requiring a larger mutational target. As a population lags behind a shifting optimum, standing variation in selected traits is continuously converted into additive variance in fitness~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Crucially, recent theory shows that even slow, continuous fluctuations in a trait optimum strongly amplify the underlying quantitative genetic variance itself: optimum displacements transiently reverse the sign of selection on rare alleles, lifting them to higher frequencies and rendering standing variance largely insensitive to the mutational target size precisely when selection is strong relative to drift~\cite{bertram2026}. Two additional mechanisms can elevate fitness variance without expanding the mutational target. First, drift around a balanced equilibrium carries disproportionate weight: a single overdominant or negatively frequency-dependent locus displaced from equilibrium contributes as much additive fitness variance as $10^2$ to $10^4$ loci at mutation--selection balance, particularly in smaller populations~\cite{connallon2026}. Second, migration--selection balance across heterogeneous habitats injects variance whenever immigrant alleles adapted to adjacent environments pull a local population away from its optimum~\cite{lenormand2002}—a dynamic directly documented in three of our Mediterranean blue tit populations, where laying date and other heritable traits diverge across deciduous and evergreen oak mosaics over just a few kilometers despite persistent gene flow~\cite{charmantier2016,perrier2020}. In Fisher's fundamental theorem, a shifting optimum, local maladaptation from gene flow, and environmental change all enter as a deteriorating environment that offsets the selective gain in mean fitness, explaining why high $V_A$ need not produce runaway population growth~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018,bonnet2022}. Yet while environmental deterioration cancels the change in mean fitness within a single generation, it cannot cancel the cumulative genetic drift generated by the underlying allele-frequency changes over coalescent time. Although a pedigree cannot separate balancing selection from a moving optimum, the two mechanisms leave distinct signatures: intermediate-frequency polymorphisms of large effect under balancing selection, versus shifting breeding values masked by phenotypic stasis under a moving optimum~\cite{merila2001}.

Shared environments, maternal effects, and indirect genetic effects omitted from the animal model can inflate pedigree estimates~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}, whereas unrecorded episodes of intense selection can understate them. Yet because a larger long-term variance removes more diversity while becoming harder to maintain, rescaling an estimate narrows one gap only by widening the other by the exact same factor. Across every population with diversity data, the long-term variance required by the diversity test exceeds the variance permitted by the maintenance test by at least 108-fold at unlinked sites and 81-fold with linkage. Consequently, the compatibility condition for any single long-term variance to satisfy both equilibria (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) depends only on the deleterious mutation rate, the mean mutational effect, the mating system, and the observed diversity deficit—rendering the conflict independent of the pedigree estimate itself. Even when evaluated in isolation with linkage, the diversity deficit is explained in only two species (bighorn sheep and song sparrows), and only if effective breeders represent a tenth of the adult census at the lowest plausible mutation rate. Reconciling the two tests through unmodeled life-history or structural factors—such as overlapping generations in iteroparous species~\cite{engen2005} or borrowed DFE parameters~\cite{lin2026}—would require a two-order-of-magnitude shift. Because standard critiques of pedigree $V_A$ describe multiplicative biases, the mismatch reflects the ecological and evolutionary processes omitted from the two equilibrium models rather than estimation error.

Predicting diversity loss from fitness variance alone also requires that allele frequencies at selected loci change little while a neutral lineage remains associated with them~\cite{barton2000}—a quasi-steady-state condition satisfied at mutation--selection--drift balance and during polygenic tracking of a moving optimum (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Directional sweeps violate this condition and generate excess drift per unit variance, though their cumulative impact is bounded by the slow rate of molecular substitution. More fundamentally, the multiplicative log-normal projection assumes that the finite population actually contains, in every generation, the extreme upper-tail individuals responsible for the predicted drift. The variance required to explain the observed diversity deficits reaches 70 to 95\% of the threshold at which those required ancestors fall below a single individual per generation. Near that finite-size boundary, coalescence shifts from a steady Kingman process to episodic multiple mergers driven by single exceptional lineages~\cite{eldon2006,neher2013,diamantidis2024}, violating the assumptions of standard variance-to-$N_e$ mappings. Balancing selection violates the quasi-steady-state condition even more directly, because an equilibrium polymorphism carries zero additive variance; its contribution arises entirely from transient displacements across allele frequencies. Slight oscillations of balanced polymorphisms can depress linked diversity across broad chromosomal regions: seasonally fluctuating balancing selection across 100 loci reduces genome-wide $N_e$ by 30 to 76\%, largely independent of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}. Thus, while our conclusion that heritable fitness variance cannot explain the diversity deficit holds under purifying and polygenic directional regimes, whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can bridge the linked diversity gap remains the decisive open question.

Because the conflict between maintainable variance and missing diversity (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) applies to any measure of heritable fitness variance, resolving how short-term estimates relate to long-term coalescent drift requires tracking fitness covariance across intermediate timescales. Temporal autocovariance in allele frequencies offers a genomic bridge, as it measures the additive fitness variance actively transmitted across generations via linked neutral markers~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. Replicate populations founded from a shared base—typical of evolve-and-resequence experiments, though rare in the wild—isolate the persistent genetic covariance in fitness across environments from single-generation environmental noise~\cite{geetaarun2026}. Finally, in wild populations with both deep pedigrees and genome sequences, expected long-term genetic contributions calculated directly on the pedigree quantify the total realized reduction in $N_e$ across all sources of reproductive skew~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}, allowing the heritable component to be partitioned directly against demographic noise.
