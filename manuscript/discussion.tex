\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical population genetics theory attributes to such variance. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves the effective size of the median population if, as the animal model behind the estimates assumes, the effects of loci on fitness multiply and breeding values for log-fitness are normally distributed. A variance this small has that much effect because inheritance adds as much again to an ancestor's own advantage through its descendants, and partners who share their offspring share each other's advantage. As expected, since no account of the missing diversity relies on heritable variance in fitness alone, this halving is a small part of what each population has lost, a median of about a fifteenth of each deficit on a logarithmic scale, even if nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring. In the median population, mutation--selection--drift balance maintains about a twentieth of the same variance, at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species and with the annotated functional sequence as the deleterious target. Both equilibria therefore leave most of what they are meant to explain to other processes. The question these estimates raise is not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test shows that the median reduction at unlinked sites lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates, using an approximation that holds while the variance is small \cite{santiago1995, santiago1998}. Because each tenfold loss of diversity requires the same increment of variance, the observed 230- to 38,000-fold deficits would require long-term variances above one, larger than any estimate. Most of each deficit is therefore likely to come from processes that act over the many generations that neutral diversity reflects, which neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Because diversity reflects the harmonic mean of population size over its genealogy, which bottlenecks and range contractions hold close to the smallest sizes, as Charlesworth and Jensen emphasize, a contemporary census overstates the population size from which we computed the diversity expected without selection. Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by the same mechanism through which heritable fitness differences increase drift, acting on demes rather than individuals, because a deme's success, like an individual's heritable advantage, persists across generations~\cite{whitlock1997}. Because relatives share their deme, a pedigree within a single deme cannot measure this source of drift, which, like a bottleneck, raises the variance that the diversity test requires without implying that the estimate is too low. Demography and deme turnover therefore remain the expected causes of the missing diversity, and heritable variance in fitness adds a share to theirs rather than competing with them as an explanation. This non-exclusivity argument between these causes also suggests that their interactions are relevant with respect to shaping neutral diversity patterns.

Heritable variance in fitness reduces diversity at a neutral site in two ways, through the individual (or equivalently the diploid genome) that carries the site, an effect shared by every site, and through the chromosome segment that carries it, an effect that varies along the genome. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024} because they estimate the second effect relative to a genome-wide baseline that already includes the first~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Such maps therefore cannot measure the genome-wide effect, which accounts of the diversity deficit have presumed small~\cite{matheson2024}, whereas a pedigree measures the variance in fitness among individuals on which that global effect depends. The same maps attribute all selected variance to recurrent deleterious mutation, and to match the depth of the diversity reductions they fit deleterious mutation rates and selected targets higher than independent estimates support~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Our maintenance test shows that recurrent mutation maintains only a small share of the variance measured in wild pedigrees, so the pattern of diversity along a chromosome, which depends on where selected sites lie and how fast selection removes them, can follow mutation--selection--drift balance while the depth of the reduction requires variance from other sources. Along chromosomes, the reduction depends on what maintains the variance~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. Our derivation shows that linkage adds to the loss of diversity only while the variance on the chromosome copy that carries a neutral site stays with it beyond the generation or two that an unlinked association lasts. The alleles that stay longest are those purged most slowly, which contribute the smallest share of the maintained variance, so the conditions for a large effect of linkage and for a large maintained variance exclude each other. At the measured variance, linkage reduces the effective size of every population with a map yet leaves each diversity deficit unexplained by at least 53-fold. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test how linkage acts, since diversity on each chromosome should decrease with the amount of selected sequence per unit of map length, along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test shows that, at the mean deleterious effects estimated in related species, lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates, like the fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} that Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} studied, carries more additive variance than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, so the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} applies to fitness itself. Because a new mutation adds variance in proportion to the square of its effect for a time inversely proportional to that effect, it contributes in proportion to its effect whatever its size, so only the deleterious mutation rate and the mean effect set the variance that mutation maintains, and drift can only lower it. Site-frequency spectra constrain the mean effect more weakly than any other feature of a distribution of fitness effects, because rare mutations of large effect influence it, yet large-effect mutations hidden from the spectra, such as transposable-element insertions, could raise it to the required 16\% only if they made up a large share of new mutations. Molecular estimates imply a high deleterious mutation rate in humans~\cite{kondrashov1993}, recently put at 2 to 10 per generation~\cite{matheson2023} and above 3.8 once slightly deleterious mutations that fix are counted~\cite{hernandez2026}, with about 7--9\% of the non-coding genome under purifying selection~\cite{di2025}. However, counting even 15\% of the unannotated sequence of our assemblies as deleterious leaves the median population with four times the variance that mutation maintains, and in most bird populations the deleterious sequence required would be larger than the genome. When the target, the mean effect and the estimate vary together, the excess persists in 12 of 18 populations if 8\% of the unannotated sequence is deleterious, 1\% of new mutations have large effects and the estimate is twice the long-term variance, and in 6 under larger departures. So the outcome is firm in a few populations, those with the largest estimates and birds with small mean effects, and provisional for the median population. A higher deleterious mutation rate also removes more diversity through background selection{, yet even with the largest target we considered, the variance that mutation maintains removes at most about half of neutral diversity, so a mutational account narrows the maintenance gap without explaining the missing diversity.

Resolving what maintains the remaining variance requires distinguishing selection on phenotypes from its allelic causes (\autoref{sec:msdb}). At the phenotypic level, additive variance in relative fitness is directional selection, and it sets the rate of evolutionary change in every trait genetically correlated with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}. Under mutation--selection--drift balance, that directional selection consists only of the continual purging of deleterious alleles, so in the median population purifying selection accounts for about a twentieth of the directional selection on fitness at the mutational effects estimated in related species. Selection on breeding date shifts from year to year across 39 bird and mammal populations~\cite{devillemereuil2020}, and as a population lags behind a shifting optimum of this kind, standing variation in selected traits is continuously converted into additive variance in fitness without a larger mutational target~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Under stabilizing selection toward a slowly moving optimum, selection favors new mutations that move the trait toward the displaced optimum, so they rise to higher frequencies instead of being purged and amplify the genetic variance of the trait. Provided selection on individual alleles is strong relative to drift, this added variance depends much less on the mutation rate and the number of loci than the variance maintained under a fixed optimum~\cite{bertram2026}. Similarly, a single overdominant or negatively frequency-dependent locus displaced from its equilibrium by drift contributes as much additive variance in fitness as $10^2$ to $10^4$ loci at mutation--selection balance, particularly in small populations~\cite{connallon2026}. Migration--selection balance also adds variance whenever immigrant alleles adapted to neighboring habitats displace a local population from its optimum~\cite{lenormand2002}, as in three of our populations, the Mediterranean blue tits, whose laying date and other heritable traits diverge between deciduous and evergreen oak patches a few kilometers apart despite persistent gene flow~\cite{charmantier2016,perrier2020}. In Fisher's fundamental theorem, a shifting optimum, local maladaptation from gene flow and environmental change all count as a deterioration of the environment that offsets the selective gain in mean fitness, which explains why a large heritable variance in fitness need not produce runaway population growth~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018,bonnet2022}. Yet environmental deterioration offsets the change in mean fitness only within a generation, and not the drift that the underlying changes in allele frequency accumulate over the many generations that neutral diversity reflects. A pedigree cannot separate balancing selection from a moving optimum, but genomic and long-term phenotypic data can, because balancing selection maintains polymorphisms of large effect at intermediate frequency, whereas under a moving optimum breeding values shift while phenotypic stasis masks the change~\cite{merila2001}.

Shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that the animal model does not include can inflate pedigree estimates~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}, whereas an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection would understate the variance. Because both tests rely on the same long-term variance, which removes more diversity the larger it is but is harder to maintain, correcting an inflated estimate would narrow the maintenance gap and widen the diversity gap by the same factor. Our threshold analysis shows that, within every population with diversity data, the long-term variance that the diversity test requires is at least 108 times the variance that the maintenance test allows, and at least 81 times with linkage. Every population therefore fails our compatibility test, the condition that a single long-term variance pass both tests, and no error that scales the estimate up or down changes that verdict. A variance that changes over time is not such an error, because the two tests respond to it differently. Mutation maintains only the long-term mean, and while the variance is small, diversity also depends only on that mean. If the effects of loci on fitness are multiplicative, however, generations of exceptionally intense selection remove more diversity than their share of the mean, whereas selection that reverses direction within a generation or two removes less, because an ancestor's advantage then no longer accumulates through its descendants. Whether such generations remove a substantial share of neutral diversity is unknown, since no pedigree spans more than a few decades. A twentyfold overstatement, the factor that the median population requires to pass the maintenance test, would nonetheless reduce our two results to one already known, that the variance mutation maintains removes a few percent of neutral diversity at sites unlinked to selected loci. Even the diversity test alone is passed only by bighorn sheep and song sparrows, with linkage, if only one adult in ten breeds and the mutation rate lies at the lower end of its confidence interval. To reconcile the two tests, an assumption of unknown direction, such as discrete generations in iteroparous populations~\cite{engen2005}, a single deme per population, or distributions of fitness effects~\cite{lin2026} and diversity taken from related species, would have to shift one result relative to the other by about two orders of magnitude. Because the usual criticisms of pedigree estimates describe errors that scale the estimate, the gap reflects more the processes the equilibrium models leave out than error in the estimate.

Predicting the loss of diversity from the variance in fitness alone also requires that allele frequencies at selected loci change little while a neutral lineage remains associated with them~\cite{barton2000}, a condition met at mutation--selection--drift balance and while polygenic traits track a moving optimum (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Sweeps of positively selected alleles violate the condition and cause more drift per unit of variance, but the slow rate of molecular substitution limits their cumulative effect. If the effects of loci on fitness are multiplicative, the predicted drift comes from rare ancestors of exceptionally high fitness, and the variances that the observed deficits would require reach 70 to 95\% of the value at which a finite population contains fewer than one such ancestor per generation. As the variance approaches that value, lineages no longer coalesce steadily in pairs. Several lineages instead trace back at once to the same exceptional ancestor, so coalescence comes in bursts and the genealogy becomes a multiple-merger coalescent, the form that highly skewed offspring numbers, rapid adaptation and occasional very large families produce~\cite{eldon2006,neher2013,diamantidis2024}. Heritable variance in fitness large enough to explain the observed deficits would therefore change the shape of the genealogy as well as its depth, and the diversity it leaves would no longer follow from an effective size alone. Balancing selection departs further from the condition, because a polymorphism at its equilibrium contributes no additive variance in fitness, so whatever drift it causes comes from displacements away from the equilibrium. Slight oscillations of balanced polymorphisms can reduce linked diversity over wide chromosomal regions, and seasonally fluctuating balancing selection at 100 loci reduces genome-wide effective size by 30 to 76\%, largely independently of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}. Our conclusion that heritable variance in fitness is too small to explain the missing diversity therefore holds while purifying selection or a moving optimum maintains the variance, and whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms remove more diversity than their contribution to that variance predicts remains the open question.

The compatibility test (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) involves only the deleterious mutation rate, the mean effect of new mutations, the mating system and the loss of diversity. The conflict therefore lies between what mutation can maintain and the diversity that is missing, and the condition applies unchanged to any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness. Temporal change in allele frequencies provides one such estimate, because its covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. Replicate populations founded from a common base, as in long-term evolve-and-resequence experiments, come closer to the long-term variance that both tests concern, because the selected change in allele frequency across replicates over several generations gives the additive genetic covariance in fitness across replicates and generations rather than a variance in the environment of a single generation~\cite{geetaarun2026}. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction in effective size directly from the expected genetic contributions of ancestors to later generations~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}, although such a measure includes all sources of variance in reproductive success, of which heritable variance in fitness is one.}
