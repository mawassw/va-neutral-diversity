\section{Discussion}
Pedigree estimates of additive variance in fitness have been read as evidence of ongoing adaptation in wild vertebrates (Bonnet et al. 2022). Set against the two equilibrium expectations that classical theory attaches to such a variance, they pass neither test. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured $V_A$ roughly halves effective size in the median population, and even the multiplicative bound leaves the observed deficits, of 230- to 38,000-fold, between 80- and 31,000-fold unexplained. Set against MSDB, the same estimates require the average new mutation in the functional genome to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 15\%, a median 18 times the mean effect of the DFE assigned to each population. The two verdicts point in opposite directions, and the condition under which they could be reconciled, $\delta \ge e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}), contains no pedigree estimate. The tension therefore lies between the mutational parameters and the observed diversity themselves. The pedigree estimates locate each population between the two ends, which are about 390-fold apart on average.

A natural response is that pedigree estimates of $V_A$ in the wild are biased. Common environments shared by relatives, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects can all be absorbed into the additive component when they are not modelled (Kruuk and Hadfield 2007; Stopher et al. 2012; Baud et al. 2022; Mawass and Milot 2025), and the estimator is conditional by construction on the environments sampled (\autoref{sec:quantity}). The rescaling $c$ makes the consequence explicit. An estimate inflated by any of these sources implies $c < 1$, which eases maintenance but widens the diversity gap by the same factor. An estimate that misses episodes of strong selection implies $c > 1$, which does the reverse. Because a population has only one long-term variance, no correction to the estimate passes both tests: the values of $c$ that the two tests require are 108- to 1,890-fold apart within every population. The result is therefore robust to the standard criticism of pedigree $V_A$, because that criticism, whatever its size, moves the two verdicts in opposite directions. What the gap measures instead is what the two models leave out.

On the maintenance side, the gap implies that most of the measured variance, if it persists, is maintained by something other than recurrent deleterious mutation. Charlesworth (2015) reached this conclusion for fitness components in \textit{Drosophila}, and it now extends to lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates. Two escapes within MSDB are unlikely. A larger mutational target cannot supply the difference, because $U_d$ already counts every mutation in the exome and in an equal regulatory target as deleterious (Methods). A tail of strongly deleterious mutations that site-frequency spectra cannot detect is also an unlikely source. For the median population $s^* \approx 0.15$, and a class of heterozygous effect 0.5, held at frequencies far too low to appear in a sample, would have to make up about 30\% of new mutations in the functional target to raise the mean to that value. The alternatives lie outside the model. In classical theory balancing selection contributes no additive variance in fitness at equilibrium, but Connallon and Czuppon (2026) showed that drift and modest fluctuations of the equilibrium displace overdominant and negatively frequency-dependent polymorphisms from it far enough that a single balanced locus contributes as much $V_A(w)$ as hundreds to thousands of loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations like those in which fitness variance is measured. Migration--selection balance among the demes of a metapopulation would put immigration in the place of mutation, supplying alleles that local selection removes. Each predicts what MSDB does not, such as large-effect variants held at intermediate frequency, or a variance that tracks environmental change. The remaining possibility, that the measured variance does not persist, is $c < 1$, and the diversity test shows that it widens the gap on the other side.

On the diversity side, the gap implies that most of the missing diversity has causes other than heritable variance in fitness. The reduction that the measured variance produces, a median $N_e/N$ of 0.6 under the small-variance approximation and 0.5 under the multiplicative model, is of the same order as the demographic ratio of 0.1--0.5 (Frankham 1995). For the median population, the two reductions taken together, as if their effects multiplied, account for a deficit of about 20-fold at most, against observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. The remainder must come from processes acting over the coalescent window that neither a pedigree nor contemporary demography records: past bottlenecks and range contractions, which depress the harmonic mean of $N$ over some $4N_e$ generations, and census sizes that overstate the long-run breeding population. Linked selection is not that remainder either. Across species it does not account for the deficit at the strongest published parameters (Buffalo 2021), and the present results remove heritable variance in fitness at the magnitude that pedigrees measure from the remaining candidates. The independence of the inputs from diversity data (Methods) is what allows this conclusion: none of the predictions was fitted to the deficit it is set against.

At linked sites the measured variance deepens the local reduction substantially in birds, to $N_e/N$ = 0.019 in the great tits at Wytham Woods, but only when it is carried by weakly selected alleles. At the effect that would maintain it, the linked factor is 0.97--0.99 in every population, and at the variance that MSDB does maintain, linkage changes the predicted diversity by about 4\% on average across species. This reconciles two observations that otherwise sit uneasily together. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes (Murphy et al. 2023; Buffalo and Kern 2024), yet across species linked selection does not account for the deficit (Buffalo 2021). The local reduction varies with the density of functional sequence per Morgan, which such maps capture. The global reduction, by contrast, is shared by every neutral site, so it is absorbed into the reference level against which those maps measure local diversity (McVicker et al. 2009). Methods that quantify linked selection therefore cannot see the global reduction, which is one reason it has been left out of accounts of the deficit (Matheson and Masel 2024). Here it is of the same order as the local reduction in mammals. The model also makes a prediction for genomic data from these pedigreed populations: local diversity should decrease among chromosomes with their selected target per Morgan, along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes (Figure~\ref{fig:linked}C).

The two verdicts read differently to those who measure heritable variance in fitness and to those who model its genetic basis, and the difference is one of level rather than substance (\autoref{sec:quantity}). To a quantitative geneticist a non-zero $V_A$ is directional selection on phenotypes: every trait that covaries genetically with fitness is predicted to change each generation by that covariance (Robertson 1966; Price 1970; Morrissey et al. 2010), and this is how the estimates have been read as adaptation. To a population geneticist the same variance at MSDB is purifying selection on alleles: the favored alleles are those free of new deleterious mutations, and the predicted change in each breeding value is cancelled by the mutations that arise in the next generation. Both descriptions hold at once, and at MSDB $U_d\bar{s}$ is at the same time the variance, the gain in mean breeding value for fitness that selection makes, and the loss that mutation causes. The maintenance test therefore asks how much of the directional selection that the pedigrees imply can be purifying selection against new mutations, and the answer is about a twentieth in the median population. If the variance persists, the rest of the predicted change must be cancelled by something other than mutation. One candidate is a changing environment: climate, competitors and parasites that evolve in turn, or the population's own density. The alleles that respond are favored because conditions have changed, which on alleles is positive or fluctuating selection, and this is adaptation in the sense of Bonnet et al. (2022). The other is drift itself. Under the balancing selection of Connallon and Czuppon (2026), drift displaces balanced polymorphisms from equilibrium and selection returns them, so the predicted change restores a balance rather than adapting to new conditions. A pedigree cannot tell these apart. They also leave different traces. At MSDB, and around a balanced equilibrium, breeding values stay where they are on average; under a changing environment they change while phenotypes may not, one of the explanations offered for the evolutionary stasis of wild populations (Merilä et al. 2001).

The diversity verdict rests on firmest ground under MSDB. The prediction of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} assumes fixed effects, a retention that is the same from one generation to the next (Santiago and Caballero 1995), and allele frequencies at the selected loci that change little while an association persists (Barton 2000). MSDB meets all three at unlinked sites, and it also holds $V_A$ constant over the coalescent window. The alternatives meet them to different degrees. A polygenic response to a changing environment, carried by many alleles of small effect, changes each allele's frequency little, so it satisfies the infinitesimal model on which Santiago and Caballero's general result rests. What changes is the amount of variance, and Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic} and the multiplicative bound account for that. Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but the effect of substitutions is bounded by the slow observed rate of molecular evolution (Barton 2000), and models of recurrent hitchhiking do not account for the deficit across species (Buffalo 2021). The balancing selection of Connallon and Czuppon (2026) is the least favorable case for our prediction. Its variance exists only while polymorphisms are displaced from equilibrium, and an allele's average effect on fitness changes with its frequency. Barton (2000) also noted that slight fluctuations of a balanced polymorphism reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, and that fluctuating selection could in principle reduce it more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness, by an amount not yet known. The prediction therefore holds under its own assumptions for the variance that MSDB maintains, which removes a few percent of neutral diversity (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}), and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained. Whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms could remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is a question that the present models leave open. We computed the local reduction under MSDB, as background selection.

The two tests also differ in what can conceal a large variance. Fisher's fundamental theorem equates the partial change in mean fitness due to selection with $V_A$ per generation, the phenotypic reading above. That change can be offset, by new mutation at MSDB or by an environment that deteriorates at the same rate, Fisher's own deterioration term, so a large $V_A$ need leave no trace in population growth. Its effect on neutral diversity cannot be offset in either way. The allele-frequency changes that selection makes happen whatever the environment does, and every generation of them adds drift at linked and unlinked sites. Neutral diversity is therefore the record that integrates the variance over the coalescent window, whatever happens to mean fitness. In these populations it cannot exclude the measured variance (\autoref{sec:res_ceiling}), but it shows how little of the missing diversity that variance explains. Pedigreed populations with genome data make this record directly measurable. Expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give the variance of long-term contributions, and so the reduction in effective size, without passing through an estimate of $V_A$ (Wray and Thompson 1990; Chen et al. 2019).

Finally, these results should be read in light of the assumptions that remain (\autoref{sec:approx}). Generations are discrete, whereas the populations are iteroparous, and age structure changes the mapping from variance in family size to effective size (Engen et al. 2005). Only autosomes are included. In birds the Z chromosome is hemizygous in females and recombines in males only, so excluding it understates the linked reduction. Each population's DFE is assigned from related species, because few wild vertebrates have their own (Lin et al. 2026). The diversity ratio rests on census sizes estimated from density and range (Buffalo 2021), and in four populations on diversity measured in another population of the species. Selected loci are placed uniformly along each map, and clustering in regions of low recombination would raise the local reduction (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}). None of these approaches the gap between the two tests, which is at least 108-fold within any population without linkage and 81-fold with it.

The framework also leaves two results that others can use without our estimates. The variance needed to explain a loss of diversity, $V_{A,\delta}$, depends only on $\delta$ and the mating system, so any population with a diversity estimate can be placed against it. The compatibility condition, Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}, depends only on the mutational parameters and the diversity ratio, so any estimate of heritable variance in fitness, from pedigrees or from sequence data, has to answer to both tests at once. The question that pedigree estimates raise is therefore not whether $V_A$ in the wild is large or small. It is how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes, and in these wild vertebrates no single answer to that question passes both tests.
