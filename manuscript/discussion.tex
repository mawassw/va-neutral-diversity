\section{Discussion}
We tested pedigree estimates of additive variance in relative fitness from 19 wild vertebrate populations~\cite{bonnet2022} against the two equilibria that classical theory attributes to such a variance. Propagated to neutral diversity, the measured variance roughly halves effective size in the median population if, as the animal model behind the estimates assumes, the effects of loci on fitness multiply and breeding values for log-fitness are normally distributed. That reduction lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates, with an approximation that holds while the variance is small. Compared against the diversity each population has lost, however, it explains only a small part of each deficit, even if nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring. In the median population the same variance is more than an order of magnitude larger than mutation--selection--drift balance can maintain at the effects of deleterious mutations measured in related species. Most of the missing diversity therefore has causes other than heritable variance in fitness, and most of the measured variance must be maintained by forces other than recurrent mutation, so both equilibria leave most of what they are meant to explain to other processes. Whether any single long-term variance could pass both tests depends only on the deleterious mutation rate, the mean effect of new deleterious mutations, the mating system and how much diversity is missing (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}), and not on any pedigree estimate, so the conflict lies between what mutation can maintain and the diversity that is missing. The question these estimates raise is therefore not whether heritable variance in fitness in the wild is large or small, but how a variance measured over a few decades relates to the long-term variance that each equilibrium describes.

Our diversity test shows that heritable variance in fitness of the measured magnitude can explain only a small part of the deficit. Across species, Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} found that background selection and recurrent hitchhiking, at \textit{Drosophila} parameters or tenfold stronger ones, also explain only part of it, so most of the rest is likely to come from processes that act over the many generations that neutral diversity reflects and that neither a pedigree nor a contemporary census records. Heritable variance in fitness reduces diversity at a neutral site in two ways, through the chromosome segment that carries the site, an effect that varies along the genome, and through the individual that carries it, an effect shared by every site. Maps of background selection explain much of the variation in diversity along human chromosomes~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024} because they estimate the first effect, from how diversity varies along chromosomes relative to a genome-wide baseline that already includes the second~\cite{mcvicker2009,murphy2023}. Such maps are therefore compatible with Buffalo's result, and they cannot measure the genome-wide effect, which may be one reason it has been left out of accounts of the deficit, where it has been presumed small~\cite{matheson2024}; a pedigree, which measures variance in fitness among individuals, provides the variance on which it depends. Along chromosomes, by contrast, the reduction depends on what maintains the variance, because an association between a neutral site and linked selected alleles lasts only as long as those alleles do~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}, so it is large only when the variance is carried by alleles that selection removes slowly, the alleles least able to maintain a large variance. Genomic data from these pedigreed populations can test this prediction, since diversity on each chromosome should decrease with the amount of selected sequence per unit of map length, along a single curve shared by macrochromosomes and microchromosomes.

Our maintenance test shows that lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates, like the fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} that Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} studied, carries more additive variance than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain, so the long-standing question of how quantitative genetic variation is maintained~\cite{johnson2005} applies to fitness itself. For recurrent mutation to maintain the measured variance, the average new mutation in the functional genome would have to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 16\%, a median 21 times the mean effect estimated for related species and assigned to each population. Because we counted every point mutation in the exome, and in an equal amount of regulatory sequence, as deleterious, mutation alone could resolve that difference only with about 20 times as much deleterious sequence, or with a class of large-effect mutations that site-frequency spectra do not detect, such as the transposable-element insertions that contribute to the variance Charlesworth found in \textit{Drosophila}, which would have to make up about 30\% of new mutations at a heterozygous effect of 0.5. If the measured variance persists, most of it is therefore maintained by something other than recurrent deleterious mutation.

What else maintains the variance depends on whether selection is described on phenotypes or on alleles, because the same variance describes both (\autoref{sec:quantity}). On phenotypes, additive variance in fitness is directional selection, predicting change in every trait that covaries genetically with fitness~\cite{robertson1966,price1970,morrissey2010}. At MSDB the same variance is purifying selection on alleles, so our maintenance test shows how much of the directional selection implied by the pedigrees can be purifying, about a twentieth in the median population. A changing environment can maintain the rest. A moving or fluctuating optimum, consistent with the year-to-year variation in selection on breeding date across 39 populations of birds and mammals~\cite{devillemereuil2020}, turns standing variation in a trait into heritable variance in fitness through the lag of the population mean behind the optimum~\cite{burger1995,lande1996}. Drift around a balanced equilibrium can also maintain the variance, displacing overdominant and negatively frequency-dependent polymorphisms far enough that a single balanced locus contributes as much additive variance in fitness as hundreds to thousands of loci at mutation--selection balance, most of all in small populations~\cite{connallon2026}. Balancing selection that fluctuates with the seasons reduces genome-wide effective size substantially, by about 30\% for 100 loci in one model and about 50\%, up to 75\%, in another, largely independently of census size~\cite{wittmann2023,johnson2026}, but by far less than the deficits observed here. A pedigree cannot tell these apart, but they predict different patterns, large-effect variants at intermediate frequency under balancing selection and, under a changing environment, breeding values that change while phenotypes may not, one explanation offered for evolutionary stasis in the wild~\cite{merila2001}.

Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} interpreted their estimates through Fisher's fundamental theorem, as the rate at which selection raises mean fitness and so as rapid contemporary adaptation. The theorem equates the change in mean fitness that selection alone produces, with the environment held fixed, with the additive variance in fitness each generation, and Bonnet et al., like earlier reviews of the evolution of fitness in the wild~\cite{burt1995,hendry2018}, noted that this change can be offset by mutation, gene flow or an environment that deteriorates at the same rate, Fisher's own deterioration term, so a large heritable variance in fitness need not show in population growth. None of these offsets its effect on neutral diversity, because the allele-frequency changes that selection makes happen whatever the environment does, and each generation of them adds drift. Neutral diversity therefore reflects the variance accumulated over the many generations of its genealogy, whatever happens to mean fitness, whereas the theorem describes a single generation. In these populations diversity shows that the measured variance explains little of what is missing, although the diversity is consistent with a variance of that size.

Both tests concern the same long-term variance, and a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, so no single multiple of the pedigree estimate passes both tests in any population we tested. Shared environments, maternal effects and indirect genetic effects that the animal model does not include can inflate pedigree estimates~\cite{kruuk2007,stopher2012,baud2022,mawass2025}. If they do, the long-term variance is smaller than measured, which brings the maintenance test closer to passing but widens the diversity gap by the same factor, and an estimate that misses episodes of intense selection has the opposite effect. Within every population with diversity data, the long-term variance that the diversity test requires is more than a hundred times the variance that the maintenance test allows, so no error that scales the estimate up or down, however large, lets it pass both. Because the usual criticisms of pedigree estimates describe errors of this kind, the gap reflects what the two equilibrium models leave out more than error in the estimate.

A population whose size or structure has departed from equilibrium can fail the diversity test whatever its variance in fitness, because such departures lower diversity without changing the estimate. Neutral diversity reflects the harmonic mean of population size over the generations of its genealogy, which bottlenecks and range contractions hold close to the smallest sizes~\cite{charlesworth2022}, so a contemporary census overstates the population size from which we computed the diversity expected without selection. Recurrent extinction and recolonization lower species-wide diversity~\cite{pannell2000} by the same mechanism through which heritable fitness differences increase drift, acting on demes rather than individuals, because a deme's success, like an individual's heritable advantage, persists across generations~\cite{whitlock1997}. Relatives share their deme, so a pedigree within a single deme cannot measure this source of drift, which, like a bottleneck, raises the variance that the diversity test requires without implying that the estimate is too low. Of the processes considered here, demography and deme turnover are the likeliest to remove diversity on the scale observed, because they directly reduce the effective size that diversity reflects, whereas a changing environment mainly affects how the variance is maintained.

A higher deleterious mutation rate would ease both discrepancies at once, by maintaining more variance and by removing more diversity through background selection, whereas a history of changing population size would act on diversity alone. Molecular estimates imply a high deleterious mutation rate in humans~\cite{kondrashov1993}, recently put at 2 to 10 per generation~\cite{matheson2023} and above 3.8 once slightly deleterious mutations that fix are counted~\cite{hernandez2026}, and about 7--9\% of the human non-coding genome appears to be under purifying selection~\cite{di2025}. Counting up to 15\% of the unannotated sequence of our assemblies as deleterious raises the deleterious mutation rate several-fold and lowers the median excess of the measured variance over what mutation maintains from 21-fold to 4-fold, so a higher mutation rate narrows the maintenance gap but resolves it in only one further population, the meerkats. In most bird populations no mutation rate can resolve it, because at the mean effects of 0.1 to 0.2\% estimated for related species the deleterious sequence would have to be larger than the genome. Demography cannot maintain the rest, because selection on deleterious mutations in these populations is strong relative to drift, so the variance that mutation maintains does not depend on effective size. The two explanations separate more sharply in the diversity test. Background selection depends mainly on the number of new deleterious mutations per unit of map length rather than on their effects, yet even with the largest amount of deleterious sequence, the variance that mutation maintains removes at most about half of neutral diversity, including its effect through background selection, against deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. A higher deleterious mutation rate therefore cannot account for the missing diversity, whereas the coalescent history of population size and structure that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} emphasize can remove diversity on the scale observed. Demography rather than mutation is thus the likelier cause of the missing diversity, and selection other than purifying selection the likelier source of the excess variance.

The least variance that could explain a given loss of diversity depends only on how much diversity is missing and on the mating system, so it can be computed for any population with estimates of diversity and census size, and any estimate of heritable variance in fitness tested against it. The condition for a single long-term variance to pass both tests (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}) likewise involves only the deleterious mutation rate, the mean effect of new mutations, the mating system and the loss of diversity, so it applies unchanged to any future estimate of heritable variance in fitness, whether from pedigrees or from temporal change in allele frequencies, whose covariance across generations measures the heritable variance in fitness carried by linked selected loci~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020}. An estimate closer to the long-term variance that both tests concern can come from replicate populations founded from a common base population, because the expected change in allele frequency due to selection, inferred across replicates and measured over several generations, gives the additive genetic covariance in fitness across replicates and generations rather than a variance in the environment of a single generation~\cite{geetaarun2026}. Natural populations rarely provide such replicates, but long-term evolve-and-resequence experiments do, and repeating the estimate over successively longer intervals would show how far a short-term variance in fitness differs from its long-term value. Pedigreed populations with genome data can also measure the reduction in effective size without first estimating heritable variance in fitness, because expected genetic contributions computed on a pedigree give directly the variance of long-term contributions from which every reduction at unlinked sites here is derived~\cite{wray1990,chen2019}, and so the reduction in effective size from all sources of variance in reproductive success, of which heritable variance in fitness is one.

We chose the approximations so that each test is biased toward passing, which makes a failed test conservative (\autoref{sec:approx}). In the diversity test, we assumed that nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages, that socially monogamous partners share all their offspring, that purging does not shorten an ancestor's advantage and that all variance in family size beyond the Poisson expectation is heritable, and we used Haldane's map, which ignores crossover interference, a male-only map for macaques, and the measured variance rather than the smaller variance that mutation maintains on linked segments; each of these choices overstates the reduction. In the maintenance test, we assumed that selection on every deleterious mutation is strong relative to drift, which overstates the variance that mutation maintains, and counted every mutation in the target as deleterious. Assigning all of the measured variance to the autosomes also places on them the share carried by the sex chromosomes (the Z in birds, the X in mammals), which slightly overstates the reduction along the autosomes. A few choices go the other way. The mutational target itself, twice the exome, is close to or smaller than the annotated functional sequence, and a larger target brings the maintenance test closer to passing without making most populations pass it (above). Census sizes that count all individuals rather than breeding adults understate the share of its expected neutral diversity that a population retains by a factor of 2 to 10, and the two literature estimates of diversity from SNP sets filtered by minor-allele frequency are probably biased low, although by much less. Even if each population retained ten times the diversity we inferred, heritable variance in fitness would explain only part of the deficit at unlinked sites in every population, and with linkage it would come within a few percent of explaining it in bighorn sheep and song sparrows. The census sizes of the birds, predicted from body mass, err in the opposite direction, by about 1.2 orders of magnitude, so in birds the two census biases partly offset. In the maintenance test, counting only the autosomes lowers the deleterious mutation rate by 4 to 6\% relative to a target that includes the sex chromosome, and the directly estimated mutation rates that we used throughout are 7 to 60\% below the rates predicted from phylogenies, which raises the mean effect required to maintain the measured variance (median 0.16, against 0.12) but also raises the share of diversity each population retains, which favors the diversity test. Biases of this size could change the maintenance verdict only in snow voles, red squirrels and meerkats, where the measured variance exceeds what mutation maintains by less than threefold and adding the X chromosome would raise the deleterious mutation rate by only about 4\%; the next closest population exceeds it about ninefold. Two departures that are not measured in these populations would raise the reduction instead, a right-skewed distribution of breeding values, which concentrates the variance in a few lineages whose advantage is retained more fully from one generation to the next (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}), and selected loci clustered in regions of low recombination rather than placed independently of it (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}).

Other assumptions have no known direction. Generations are discrete, whereas the populations are iteroparous and age structure changes the mapping from variance in family size to effective size~\cite{engen2005}, and each study population is treated as a single deme. Each population's distribution of fitness effects is taken from related species, because few wild vertebrates have their own~\cite{lin2026}, and in four populations the share of diversity retained rests on diversity measured in another population of the species (Methods). The estimates count fitness over each individual's whole life, whereas the reduction is computed for breeding adults, so heritable survival before breeding is expressed in a breeder's offspring and its advantage persists one generation less. Treating it as part of a single variance therefore overstates its contribution to the reduction, unless survival and fecundity are negatively correlated genetically, as under a life-history trade-off. Within every population with diversity data, the long-term variance that the diversity test requires is at least 108 times the variance that the maintenance test allows without linkage, and 81 times with it, so reconciling the tests would require an assumption that shifts a result by about two orders of magnitude.

Predicting the loss of diversity from the variance alone also requires that allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while a neutral site remains associated with them~\cite{barton2000}, a condition met at MSDB and by a polygenic response to a changing environment (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies cause more drift than their variance in fitness indicates, but their substitutions are bounded by the slow rate of molecular evolution. Balancing selection meets the condition least well, because its variance exists only away from equilibrium and its effects change with allele frequency, and slight fluctuations of balanced polymorphisms reduce diversity over a wide region of linked sequence, possibly more than either substitutions or inherited variance in fitness; models of seasonally fluctuating balancing selection put the genome-wide effect at tens of percent (above), but its full extent at linked sites is not yet known. Our conclusion that the measured variance is too small to explain the missing diversity therefore holds under the model's assumptions for a variance maintained at MSDB, the case in which we computed the reduction along chromosomes as background selection, and approximately for a polygenic variance however it is maintained. Whether fluctuating balanced polymorphisms can remove the diversity that a variance of the measured size cannot is the open question most likely to change that conclusion.
