\section{Results}
\subsection{Heritable variance in fitness implies a modest reduction in effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:res_unlinked}
Table~\ref{tab:reduction} and Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked} give the reduction in effective size at unlinked sites implied by the pedigree estimates of the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$. \claude{Because the animal model of Bonnet et al.~assumes that loci act multiplicatively on fitness, the multiplicative model gives the prediction consistent with how the estimates were made. Under it, $N_e/N$ ranges from 0.021 to 0.98, with a median of 0.53 (Figure}~\ref{fig:unlinked}\claude{a, gray circles). The median reduction, 47\%, lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen}~\cite{charlesworth2022} \claude{obtained from the mean of the same estimates with the small-variance approximation.} This implies that heritable differences in reproductive success alone would make the median population \claude{lose neutral diversity as fast as one with 53\% of its breeding adults}, a reduction of the same order as those commonly attributed to variance in family size and unequal sex ratios~\cite{frankham1995}.
The largest reduction is predicted where $\hat{V}_A$ is largest, in the blue tits at Pirio and Muro and in the spotted hyenas\claude{. For the Muro blue tits, $N_e/N$ is 0.021, close to the value of 0.019 under the multiplicative bound, the largest reduction that a given $V_A$ can cause under multiplicative effects, reached when nothing limits the fitness of the most favored lineages and socially monogamous partners share all their offspring}. For the same $V_A$, socially monogamous populations have a lower $N_e/N$ than randomly mating ones \claude{because partners share each other's advantage (}\autoref{sec:mating}\claude{; Figure}~\ref{fig:unlinked}\claude{b).}

\claude{The small-variance approximation, the limit of this model and of any other smooth mapping from breeding value to fitness when $V_A$ is small, gives $N_e/N$ from 0.22 to 0.98, with a median of 0.61 (Figure}~\ref{fig:unlinked}\claude{a, open circles). The multiplicative model predicts a larger reduction because each increment of breeding value multiplies expected fitness rather than adding to it. Consequently, lineages in the upper tail of breeding values leave more descendants than the linear approximation allows, and under random mating the inequality of ancestors' shares, measured by the variance of long-term contributions, grows as $e^{4V_A} - 1$ rather than as $4V_A$. The difference between the model and its limit therefore measures how much the prediction depends on how loci combine to determine fitness, and it exceeds a quarter only in the five populations with $\hat{V}_A$ above 0.25}: the blue tits at Pirio and Muro, spotted hyenas, red deer and bighorn sheep\claude{, so in the other 14 the prediction does not depend on it}.

\subsection{Heritable variance in fitness accounts for a small share of each observed deficit}\label{sec:res_deficit}
\claude{Neutral diversity in the deficit set lies so far below what census size predicts that reductions in effective size as modest as those above are expected to account for only part of each deficit.} The \claude{estimated} diversity ratio, \claude{$\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$}, ranges from $10^{-2.36}$ in bighorn sheep to $10^{-4.58}$ in song sparrows\claude{, deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold} (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}). \claude{On the logarithmic scale on which successive reductions combine (}\autoref{sec:conditions}\claude{), $\hat{V}_A$ accounts under the multiplicative model for a median of 6.9\% of each deficit (quartiles 4.0--19.5\%), from 1.1\% in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe to 40\% in the blue tits at Muro (Table}~\ref{tab:reduction}\claude{). The share is 1.3 to 41\% (median 6.9\%) under the multiplicative bound and 1.0 to 15.5\% (median 5.6\%) under the small-variance approximation. The rest of each deficit is left to other processes.} At the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$, predicted diversity exceeds observed diversity even under the multiplicative bound, by a factor of 80 in bighorn sheep and of up to 31,000 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe. \claude{Because $\hat{\delta}$ substitutes the contemporary census for the long-term number of breeding adults (}\autoref{sec:quantity}\claude{), these factors are also the least amount by which $N_c$ would have to overstate that number for heritable variance in fitness to account for each observed deficit.} In terms of the least variance that could account for each deficit, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}\claude{; Figure}~\ref{fig:ceiling}\claude{, orange ticks}) ranges from 1.1 to 1.7, above the upper limit of the 95\% HPD interval of every one of the nine estimates, the largest of which is 0.70. This shortfall holds across the 95\% CI of the mutation rate, where the smallest ratio of the required variance to the upper HPD limit is 1.66 (Muro blue tits). Even \claude{combined with} the lower end of the demographic range, $N_{eV}/N = 0.1$~\cite{frankham1995}, \claude{the predicted reduction accounts} for a deficit of at most about 20-fold in the median population.

How large is a long-term $\bar{V}_A$ of 1.1 to 1.7? Under the multiplicative model, an individual whose breeding value lies one standard deviation above the mean would expect three to four times as many offspring as an individual at the mean, a large but not inconceivable difference in a single generation, and a much harder one to sustain over the roughly $4N_e$ generations that neutral diversity reflects. % AUTHOR: own translation (multiplicative model, w proportional to e^z, one SD = sqrt(V_A): e^1.05 = 2.9 at V_A = 1.1, e^1.30 = 3.7 at 1.7); keep, re-voice or cut. Fix 9F (2026-11-19): the aside at the median 0.10 (37%) was cut; Theory sec:quantity now gives V_A = 0.1 as "about a third more offspring".
\claude{Under the multiplicative bound, the reduction these values predict comes from ancestors far in the upper tail of breeding values. Each required value is 70 to 95\% (median 88\%) of the variance at which such ancestors become rarer than one per generation among $N_c$ breeding adults, and 48 to 83\% if one adult in ten breeds (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_tail}\claude{). Because diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$ over its genealogy (Equation}~\eqref{eq:harmonic}\claude{), the few decades that a pedigree covers contribute to that requirement only in proportion to their length.}
Counting only breeding adults, which would raise \claude{$\hat{\delta}$} up to tenfold (Table~\ref{tab:bias}), leaves every required variance above its upper HPD limit (smallest ratio 1.17, bighorn sheep at the lower end of the mutation-rate CI).

\subsection{Linkage amplifies the reduction only where purging is slow}\label{sec:res_linked}
Figure~\ref{fig:linked} gives genome-wide $N_e/N$ for the mapped set (\autoref{sec:linked_methods}), combining the global reduction under the multiplicative bound with the local reduction along each chromosome, at $\hat{V}_A$ and the empirical DFE (Methods). In mammals, linkage lowers $N_e/N$ from 0.25--0.63 to 0.15--0.44. The effect of linkage varies more in birds. With linkage, $N_e/N$ is 0.66 in collared flycatchers and 0.43 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe, but 0.066 in song sparrows and 0.016 in the great tits at Wytham Woods, differences that follow mainly from $\hat{V}_A$. \claude{With linkage, $\hat{V}_A$ accounts on the logarithmic scale for 4.7 to 40\% of each observed deficit (median 19\%) in the six mapped populations of the deficit set, from the collared flycatchers to the great tits at Wytham Woods, against 1.2 to 20\% under the multiplicative bound without linkage. The remainder at the posterior mode ranges from 53-fold (bighorn sheep) to 15,000-fold (great tits, Hoge Veluwe), the least factors by which $N_c$ would have to overstate the long-term number of breeding adults, and no value within the 95\% HPD interval accounts for the whole deficit at any mutation rate within its 95\% CI.} \claude{Equivalently, the variance each deficit requires with linkage, which depends on each population's maps and DFE, is 0.28--0.41 in the four bird populations of both test sets and about 1.0 in bighorn sheep and rhesus macaques, against 1.1--1.7 without linkage, and still exceeds the upper limit of each population's 95\% HPD interval (Figure}~\ref{fig:ceiling}\claude{, teal ticks).} Because every \claude{remainder} is larger with the global factor under the small-variance approximation instead (75- to 15,000-fold), the verdict does not depend on the form. The \claude{margin is narrowest for} bighorn sheep, whose upper HPD limit gives $N_e/N = 0.045$, against an observed diversity ratio of 0.0043\claude{, and for song sparrows}. If only one adult in ten breeds, the variance their deficits require comes within a few percent of the upper limit of their HPD intervals at the point value of the mutation rate, and is below it at the lower end of its CI; the song sparrows would also reach theirs if the bird census sizes were too high rather than too low.

The local reduction is large only when the variance is carried by slowly purged alleles\claude{, which stay on a neutral site's haplotype long enough for linkage to prolong their association with it}. \claude{The linked term is therefore an upper limit on what linkage adds, because it gives the measured variance to alleles purged as slowly as the assigned DFE implies, although alleles purged that slowly could not maintain that variance (}\autoref{sec:approx}\claude{).} When we instead give every allele the effect $s^*$ at which MSDB maintains $\hat{V}_A$, the linked factor\claude{, genome-wide $N_e/N$ with linkage relative to its value without,} is 0.97--0.99 in every population. Because alleles of that effect are purged within a few generations, before recombination would separate them from most linked sites, linkage adds almost nothing to the unlinked reduction. Chromosomes that carry more selected target per unit of map length\claude{, and therefore more of their variance within the map distance over which an association outlasts an unlinked one,} are predicted to lose more of their neutral diversity, and the chromosomes of both taxa, macrochromosomes and microchromosomes alike, lie on the same curve \claude{(Figure}~\ref{fig:S_omega}\claude{c). The contrast between birds and mammals follows the assigned DFEs rather than the maps, and neither partitioning the variance by gene count nor counting part of the unannotated sequence as target changes these results (Supplementary Results).} \claude{Small as it is against the deficits, the measured variance is, as we show next, larger in most populations than recurrent mutation maintains at the mean deleterious effects assigned from related species.}

\subsection{Maintaining the measured variance would require a mean deleterious effect of about 16\%}\label{sec:res_msd}
Figure~\ref{fig:selection} gives, for the 18 populations with an annotated assembly, the mean heterozygous selection coefficient that deleterious mutations would require for MSDB to maintain $\hat{V}_A$, $s^* = \hat{V}_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar} with $\bar{V}_A = \hat{V}_A$). The posterior modes range from 0.0064 in snow voles to 0.64 in the blue tits at Muro and the spotted hyenas, with a median of about 0.16\claude{, so the average new mutation in the functional genome would have to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 16\%}. The empirical DFE assigned to each population has a mean of 0.0013--0.0021 in birds and 0.010--0.020 in mammals. In 17 of the 18 populations, the posterior probability that $s^*$ exceeds that mean is at least 0.89. The exception is the snow vole, whose estimate is close to zero (0.72). At the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$ and the point value of the direct mutation rate, $s^*$ is a median of 21 times the assigned mean, 10 to 590 times in birds and up to 35 times in mammals\claude{. Equivalently, $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds the variance that recurrent mutation maintains at the assigned DFE, $U_d\bar{s}$ = 0.0004 to 0.02, in 16 of 18 populations, all but the snow voles and red squirrels, and by less than threefold only in the meerkats (Figure}~\ref{fig:S_gap}\claude{)}. \claude{At the annotated target and the mean effects assigned from related species, the share of $\hat{V}_A$ that recurrent mutation maintains, $U_d\bar{s}/\hat{V}_A$ (}\autoref{sec:conditions}\claude{), has a median of 4.7\% (quartiles 1.0--9.0\%), from 0.17\% in the blue tits at Muro to 155\% in the snow voles. It is 49\% in the meerkats and 0.17 to 11\% (median 1.4\%) in the deficit set (Table}~\ref{tab:reduction}\claude{).} In four populations, the spotted hyenas, the blue tits at Muro and Pirio, and the hihi on Tiritiri Matangi, more than 5\% of the posterior lies where $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds $U_d$ itself, a variance that no selection coefficient can maintain\claude{, because a mutation cannot remove more than all of its carrier's fitness}.

\claude{A tail} of large effects hidden from site-frequency spectra \claude{raises the maintained variance in proportion to its contribution to the mean effect (}\autoref{sec:msdb}\claude{), and it} would raise the mean that far only if about 30\% of new mutations had a heterozygous effect of 0.5. \claude{Combined with a larger target and an inflated estimate, a much smaller tail brings the median population close to passing, although most populations still fail (}\autoref{sec:meth_joint}\claude{, Figure}~\ref{fig:S_maintenance_joint}\claude{). Varied alone, no input passes most populations even at its largest value. Fifteen of the 18 still fail with 15\% of the unannotated sequence counted as target, 14 when 5\% of new mutations have an effect of 0.5, and 14 when the estimate is ten times the long-term variance. With 8\% of the unannotated sequence, a 1\% tail and a twofold inflated estimate, 12 populations still fail (7 birds and 5 mammals), 7 of them with posterior probability at least 0.95. However, the median $\hat{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$ decreases from 21 to 1.9, so recurrent mutation would maintain about half of the median population's variance rather than 4.7\%. With 15\%, a 2\% tail and a threefold inflation, 6 populations fail, the blue tits at Rouvi\`ere, Pirio and Muro, the hihi on Tiritiri Matangi, the great tits at Wytham Woods and the spotted hyenas, and the median ratio is 0.63. All 18 pass only when 15\%, a 5\% tail and a tenfold inflation are combined, and the two Corsican blue tit populations are the last to pass. Because a tail adds the same amount to every mean effect, it matters most where the assigned mean is small. In birds, whose assigned means are 0.0013 to 0.0021, a 1\% tail raises the mean three- to fivefold, so the bird verdict depends mainly on the tail. In mammals, whose assigned means are about ten times larger, the same tail raises the mean by a quarter to a half, so the mammal verdict depends on the target and the estimate. The mammals other than the spotted hyenas fail by 9- to 22-fold at the primary target (the meerkats by 2.0-fold) and by 2- to 5-fold with 8\% of the unannotated sequence, at which the meerkats pass.}

Wherever diversity gives $N_e$, $2N_es^*$ is 2,900 to 320,000 (11 populations). Selection is therefore strong relative to drift, and MSDB maintains $U_d\bar{s}$. A variance carried by weakly selected mutations would instead require an effective size well below $U_d/(2V_A)$, 3.3 individuals for the median population at the posterior modes of $s^*$ (0.78--78). With the phylogenetic mutation rate, the median $s^*$ is 0.12. Because the annotated functional target (coding sequence, untranslated regions and non-coding RNA exons) is 0.8 to 2.0 times the doubled exome in these assemblies, the primary $U_d$ is close to the value the annotation alone gives. Counting up to 15\% of the unannotated sequence as deleterious target as well lowers the median $\hat{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$ from 21 to 4, and to 6 or 7 at the 7--9\% of non-coding sequence estimated to be deleterious in humans~\cite{di2025}; the ratio newly drops below 1 only in the meerkats, beyond $\zeta \approx 3\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:S_noncoding}). Over this range, $U_d$ reaches 1.6--2.7 in birds and 3.9--15 in mammals. In seven of the nine bird populations, moreover, the target required to maintain $\hat{V}_A$ at the assigned DFE would exceed the genome itself. At the effects $\hat{V}_A$ requires, purging would also shorten an ancestor's advantage at unlinked sites, by the factor $1/(1 + s^*)^2$ of Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}, 0.37--0.99 (median 0.75)\claude{, so the $Q = 2$ used above overstates the global reduction}.

\subsection{No single long-term variance passes both tests}\label{sec:res_rescaling}
A larger long-term variance brings the diversity test closer to passing and the maintenance test further from it. We rescaled the pedigree estimate, $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$, for the deficit set (\autoref{sec:pi_methods}) and followed each test as $c$ changes (Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling}). The maintenance test is passed only for $c \le 0.024$ on average across species (0.0017--0.11 across populations), a long-term variance about forty times smaller than the pedigree estimate. The observed deficit is explained only for $c \ge 9.4$ on average (2.4--80 across populations, median 14), a long-term variance about ten times larger. The two requirements are separated by a factor of about 390 on average, and by 108 to 1,890 within every population. Every population in the deficit set therefore fails both tests at the pedigree estimate. No rescaling passes both, the case of Table~\ref{tab:c} in which the equilibrium assumed by the two models does not hold. Where the two average curves cross, at $c \approx 3.9$, the long-term variance would be 160 times more than MSDB maintains, and predicted diversity would still be 160 times the observed.

The variance that each population's DFE can maintain, 0.0007 to 0.013 in the deficit set, would lower neutral diversity at unlinked sites by only 0.6 to 5\% even under the multiplicative bound ($N_e/N$ = 0.95--0.99), against observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. The conflict between the two tests therefore does not depend on the pedigree estimates (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}). \claude{The 108- to 1,890-fold gap between the two requirements is set by $U_d\bar{s}$, the mating system and $\hat{\delta}$, and the estimates determine only on which side of each threshold a population lies and by how much.}

Adding linkage narrows the gap but does not resolve it (Figure~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}). For the six populations of both sets, the long-term variance that would explain the deficit with linkage is 3.3 to 22.6 times the pedigree estimate in birds and 3.8 to 9.0 times in mammals. That is still 81 to 442 times what MSDB at the same DFE maintains. At the variance MSDB does maintain, linkage barely changes the prediction. Averaged over species, predicted diversity exceeds observed diversity 4,240-fold with linkage and 4,430-fold without, so MSDB with background selection accounts for almost none of the missing diversity. With 15\% of the unannotated sequence counted as target, the variance MSDB maintains, with background selection, removes at most about half of neutral diversity in any population (rhesus macaques, $N_e/N = 0.51$). Predicted diversity still exceeds observed diversity 3,240-fold on average, so a larger deleterious target does not change this conclusion.
