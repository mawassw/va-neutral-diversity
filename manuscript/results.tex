\section{Results}
\subsection{Heritable variance in fitness implies a modest reduction in effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:res_unlinked}
Table~\ref{tab:reduction} and Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked} give the reduction in effective size at unlinked sites implied by the pedigree estimates of the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$. Under the multiplicative model, $N_e/N$ ranges from 0.021 to 0.98, with a median of 0.53 (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}a, gray circles). The median reduction of 47\% lies within the 16 to 55\% that Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} obtained from the mean of the same estimates with the small-variance approximation. The predicted reductions are of the same order as those commonly attributed to variance in family size and unequal sex ratios~\cite{frankham1995}. The largest predicted reductions occur where $\hat{V}_A$ is largest, in the blue tits at Pirio and Muro and in the spotted hyenas; at Muro, $N_e/N = 0.021$ is close to the 0.019 that the multiplicative bound gives. For the same $V_A$, socially monogamous populations have a lower $N_e/N$ than randomly mating populations because social partners share each other's advantage (\autoref{sec:mating}; Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}b).

The small-variance approximation understates the largest predicted reductions and gives $N_e/N$ from 0.22 to 0.98, with a higher median of 0.61 (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}a, open circles). The multiplicative model predicts a larger reduction because, with expected fitness exponential in the breeding value, the variance of long-term contributions grows exponentially, as $e^{4V_A}-1$, rather than linearly, as $4V_A$ (\autoref{sec:onemodel}). Lineages in the upper tail of breeding values therefore leave more descendants than the small-variance approximation allows. In the 14 populations with $\hat{V}_A$ below 0.25, the multiplicative model predicts an $N_e/N$ within a quarter of the small-variance approximation, so the prediction depends little on how loci combine to determine fitness. The difference exceeds a quarter only in the five populations with larger estimates, the blue tits at Pirio and Muro, spotted hyenas, red deer, and bighorn sheep, and is about tenfold in those two blue tit populations.

\subsection{Heritable variance in fitness accounts for a small share of each observed deficit}\label{sec:res_deficit}
Because neutral diversity in the deficit set lies orders of magnitude below what census size predicts, reductions this modest are expected to account for only part of each deficit. The diversity ratio, $\hat{\delta}$, ranges from $10^{-2.36}$ in bighorn sheep to $10^{-4.58}$ in song sparrows, deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}). On the logarithmic scale on which successive reductions combine (\autoref{sec:conditions}), $\hat{V}_A$ accounts under the multiplicative model for a median of 6.9\% of the deficit (quartiles 4.0--19.5\%), from 1.1\% in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe to 40\% in the blue tits at Muro (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}; Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}). The share is 1.2 to 41\% (median 6.9\%) under the multiplicative bound and 1.0 to 15.5\% (median 5.6\%) under the small-variance approximation. At the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$, predicted diversity exceeds observed diversity even under the multiplicative bound, by a factor of 80 (bighorn sheep) to 31,000 (great tits at Hoge Veluwe). These factors are also the smallest by which $N_c$ would have to overstate the long-term number of breeding adults for heritable variance in fitness to account for each deficit (\autoref{sec:conditions}). Equivalently, the minimum variance that could account for each deficit, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}; Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}, orange diamonds), is 1.1 to 1.7, above the upper limit of the 95\% HPD interval of all nine estimates (at most 0.70). It stays above that limit across the 95\% CI of the mutation rate (smallest ratio 1.66, Muro blue tits) and when only breeding adults are counted, which would raise $\hat{\delta}$ up to tenfold (Table~\ref{tab:bias}; smallest ratio 1.17, bighorn sheep at the lower end of the mutation-rate CI). Even combined with the lower end of the demographic range, $N_{eV}/N = 0.1$~\cite{frankham1995}, the predicted reduction accounts for a deficit of at most about 20-fold in the median population.

A long-term $\bar{V}_A$ of 1.1 to 1.7 is large. Under the multiplicative model, an individual whose breeding value lies one standard deviation above the mean would expect three to four times as many offspring as an individual at the mean, a large but not inconceivable difference in a single generation, and a much harder difference to sustain over the roughly $2N_e$ generations back to the common ancestor of two gene copies. Under the multiplicative bound, each required value is 70 to 95\% (median 88\%) of the variance at which the upper-tail ancestors that produce the reduction become rarer than one per generation among $N_c$ breeding adults, and 48 to 83\% if one adult in ten breeds (Appendix~\ref{app:B_tail}). Because diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$ over its genealogy (Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}), the variance measured over a pedigree counts toward each required value only in proportion to the share of the genealogy that the pedigree spans. That share is very small, because a pedigree covers a few decades, whereas the diversity of the deficit set reflects $\pi/\mu \approx 4N_e$, or about 90,000 to 1,000,000, generations.

\subsection{Linkage amplifies the reduction only where purging is slow}\label{sec:res_linked}
For the mapped set (\autoref{sec:linked_methods}), Figure~\ref{fig:linked} combines the reduction at unlinked sites under the multiplicative bound with the reduction at linked sites along each chromosome, at $\hat{V}_A$ and the assigned empirical DFE. Linkage lowers $N_e/N$ from 0.25--0.63 to 0.15--0.44 in mammals. In birds, $N_e/N$ with linkage is 0.66 in collared flycatchers and 0.43 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe but 0.066 in song sparrows and 0.016 in the great tits at Wytham Woods, differences that follow mainly from $\hat{V}_A$. With linkage, $\hat{V}_A$ accounts on the logarithmic scale for 4.7 to 40\% of each observed deficit (median 19\%) in the six mapped populations of the deficit set, against 1.2 to 20\% under the multiplicative bound without linkage. The shortfall at the posterior mode, which is also the factor by which $N_c$ would have to overstate the long-term number of breeding adults, ranges from 53-fold (bighorn sheep) to 15,000-fold (great tits, Hoge Veluwe). With the small-variance approximation as the unlinked factor it ranges from 75- to 15,000-fold, so the shortfall depends little on the fitness mapping assumed. The variance each deficit requires with linkage also exceeds the upper limit of each population's 95\% HPD interval at any mutation rate within its 95\% CI, although linkage lowers it from 1.1--1.7 to 0.28--0.41 in the four bird populations of both sets and to about 1.0 in bighorn sheep and rhesus macaques (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}, teal diamonds). The margin is narrowest for bighorn sheep and song sparrows. The upper HPD limit of bighorn sheep gives $N_e/N = 0.045$ against an observed diversity ratio of 0.0043. If only one adult in ten breeds, the variance that the deficits of these two populations require is within a few percent of their upper HPD limits at the point value of the mutation rate and below those limits at the lower end of its CI. The song sparrows would also reach their limit if the bird census sizes were too high and not too low.

The linked term is an upper limit on what linkage adds, because it attributes the measured variance to alleles purged as slowly as the assigned DFE implies, which stay associated with linked neutral sites longest but could not maintain that variance (\autoref{sec:linkage}). When we instead give every allele the effect $s^*$ at which MSDB maintains $\hat{V}_A$, the linked factor, which is the genome-wide $N_e/N$ with linkage relative to its value without, is 0.97--0.99 in every population, because alleles of that effect are purged within a few generations, before recombination would separate them from most linked sites. Chromosomes that carry more selected target per unit of map length are predicted to lose more of their neutral diversity, and the chromosomes of both taxa, macrochromosomes and microchromosomes alike, lie on the same curve (Figure~\ref{fig:S_omega}c). The contrast between birds and mammals follows the assigned DFEs rather than the maps. Partitioning the variance by gene count or counting part of the unannotated sequence as target does not change these results (Supplementary Results). The measured variance is small relative to the deficits. As we show next, it is nonetheless larger in most populations than recurrent mutation maintains at the mean deleterious effects assigned from related species.

\subsection{Maintaining the measured variance would require unrealistic mean deleterious effects}\label{sec:res_msd}
For the 18 populations with an annotated assembly, the mean heterozygous effect that MSDB would require to maintain $\hat{V}_A$, $s^* = \hat{V}_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}), has posterior modes from 0.0064 in snow voles to 0.64 in the blue tits at Muro and the spotted hyenas, with a median of about 0.16 (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}). The assigned empirical DFEs have means of 0.0013--0.0021 in birds and 0.010--0.020 in mammals, and the posterior probability that $s^*$ exceeds the assigned mean is at least 0.89 in 17 of the 18 populations (0.72 in snow voles, whose estimate is close to zero). At the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$ and the point value of the direct mutation rate, $s^*$ is 21 times the assigned mean in the median population, 10 to 590 times in birds and up to 35 times in mammals. Equivalently, $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds the variance that recurrent mutation maintains at the assigned DFE, $U_d\bar{s}$ = 0.0004 to 0.02, in 16 of 18 populations, all but the snow voles and red squirrels, and by less than threefold only in the meerkats (Figure~\ref{fig:S_gap}). The share of $\hat{V}_A$ that recurrent mutation maintains, $U_d\bar{s}/\hat{V}_A$ (\autoref{sec:conditions}), has a median of 4.7\% (quartiles 1.0--9.0\%), from 0.17\% in the blue tits at Muro to 155\% in the snow voles; it is 49\% in the meerkats and 0.17 to 11\% (median 1.4\%) in the deficit set (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}). In four populations, the spotted hyenas, the blue tits at Muro and Pirio, and the hihi on Tiritiri Matangi, more than 5\% of the posterior lies where $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds $U_d$ itself, a variance that no selection coefficient can maintain, because a mutation cannot remove more than all of its carrier's fitness.

In the 11 populations for which diversity gives an estimate of $N_e$, $2N_es^*$ is 2,900 to 320,000, so selection is strong relative to drift and MSDB maintains an expected variance $U_d\bar{s}$. A variance carried by weakly selected mutations would instead require an effective size well below $U_d/(2V_A)$, 3.3 individuals for the median population at the posterior modes of $s^*$ (0.78--78). With the phylogenetic mutation rate, the median $s^*$ is 0.12. The annotated functional target (coding sequence, untranslated regions, and non-coding RNA exons) is 0.8 to 2.0 times the doubled exome, so the primary $U_d$ is close to what the annotation alone gives. In seven of the nine bird populations, the target required to maintain $\hat{V}_A$ at the assigned DFE would exceed the genome itself. At the effects $\hat{V}_A$ requires, purging would also cut short an ancestor's advantage at unlinked sites and multiply $Q^2$ by $1/(1 + s^*)^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_purge}), 0.37--0.99 (median 0.75), so the $Q = 2$ used above overstates the reduction at unlinked sites.

A tail of large effects hidden from site-frequency spectra raises the maintained variance in proportion to its contribution to the mean effect (\autoref{sec:msdb}). It would raise the mean to the median $s^*$ only if about 30\% of new mutations had a heterozygous effect of 0.5. Counting part of the unannotated sequence as target ($\zeta$) lowers $\hat{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$ in every population but changes few verdicts. At $\zeta = 15\%$ the median ratio decreases from 21 to 4, and at the 7--9\% of non-coding sequence estimated to be deleterious in humans~\cite{di2025} it is 6 or 7. Of the 16 populations that fail at the primary target, 15 still fail at $\zeta = 15\%$; the exception is the meerkats, which pass once $\zeta$ exceeds about 3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:S_noncoding}). Fourteen populations still fail when 5\% of new mutations have an effect of 0.5, and fourteen when the estimate is ten times the long-term variance.

Combined, the three inputs bring the median population close to passing, although most populations still fail (\autoref{sec:meth_joint}, Figure~\ref{fig:S_maintenance_joint}). With 8\% of the unannotated sequence, a 1\% large-effect tail and a twofold inflated estimate, 12 populations fail (7 birds and 5 mammals), 7 of them with posterior probability at least 0.95, but the median ratio decreases from 21 to 1.9, so recurrent mutation would maintain about half of the median population's variance rather than 4.7\%. With 15\%, a 2\% large-effect tail and a threefold inflation, 6 populations fail, the blue tits at Rouvi\`ere, Pirio and Muro, the hihi on Tiritiri Matangi, the great tits at Wytham Woods and the spotted hyenas, and the median ratio is 0.63. All 18 pass only when 15\%, a 5\% tail and a tenfold inflation are combined, with the two Corsican blue tit populations last. Because a large-effect tail adds the same amount to every mean effect, a 1\% tail raises the bird means (0.0013 to 0.0021) three- to fivefold but the mammal means, about ten times larger, only by a quarter to a half, so the bird verdict depends mainly on the tail and the mammal verdict on the target and the estimate (Supplementary Results).

\subsection{No single long-term variance passes both tests}\label{sec:res_rescaling}
A larger long-term variance brings the diversity test closer to passing and the maintenance test further from it. For the threshold analysis, we rescaled the pedigree estimate, $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$, for the deficit set (\autoref{sec:pi_methods}) and followed each test as $c$ changes (Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling}). The maintenance test is passed only for $c \le 0.024$ on average across species (0.0017--0.11 across populations), a long-term variance about forty times smaller than the pedigree estimate, and the diversity test only for $c \ge 9.4$ (2.4--80 across populations, median 14), about ten times larger. Every population in the deficit set fails both tests at the pedigree estimate. The two thresholds are separated by a factor of about 390 on average, and by 108 to 1,890 in individual populations, so every population also fails the compatibility test, the case of Table~\ref{tab:c} in which the equilibrium assumed by the two models does not hold. Where the two average curves cross, at $c \approx 3.9$, the long-term variance would be 160 times what MSDB maintains, and predicted diversity would still be 160 times the observed.

The variance that recurrent mutation maintains at each population's assigned DFE, 0.0007 to 0.013 in the deficit set, would lower neutral diversity at unlinked sites by only 0.6 to 5\% even under the multiplicative bound ($N_e/N$ = 0.95--0.99). The outcome of the compatibility test therefore does not depend on the pedigree estimates, which determine only on which side of each threshold a population lies and by how much (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}).

Adding linkage narrows the gap but does not resolve it (Figure~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}). For the six populations of both sets, the long-term variance that would explain the deficit with linkage is 3.3 to 22.6 times the pedigree estimate in birds and 3.8 to 9.0 times in mammals, still 81 to 442 times what MSDB at the same DFE maintains. At the variance MSDB does maintain, linkage barely changes the prediction, so MSDB with background selection accounts for almost none of the missing diversity. Averaged over species, predicted diversity exceeds observed diversity 4,240-fold with linkage and 4,430-fold without. With 15\% of the unannotated sequence counted as target it does so 3,240-fold, and the variance MSDB then maintains removes no more than about half of neutral diversity in any population (rhesus macaques, $N_e/N = 0.51$).
