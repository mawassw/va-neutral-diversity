\section{Results}
\subsection{Heritable variance in fitness implies a modest reduction in effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:res_unlinked}
Table~\ref{tab:reduction} and Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked} give the reduction in effective size at unlinked sites implied by the pedigree estimates of $V_A$. Under the small-variance approximation, $N_e/N$ ranges from 0.22 to 0.99, with a median of 0.61 (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}a). Heritable differences in reproductive success alone therefore make the median population drift as fast as one with 61\% of its breeding adults, a reduction of the same order as those commonly attributed to variance in family size and unequal sex ratios~\cite{frankham1995}. % CITE: Frankham 1995, Genet. Res. 66:95-107; not yet in references.bib
The reduction is largest in the blue tits at Pirio and Muro and in the spotted hyenas, where $\hat{V}_A$ is largest. For a given $V_A$, socially monogamous populations have a lower $N_e/N$ than randomly mating ones (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}b; \autoref{sec:mating}).

The multiplicative model of fitness, which the animal model of Bonnet et al.\ also assumes, gives a larger reduction. Its prediction departs appreciably from the small-variance approximation only in the five populations with $\hat{V}_A$ above about 0.25: the blue tits at Pirio and Muro, spotted hyenas, red deer and bighorn sheep (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}a, triangles). The median prediction is 0.53. For the Muro blue tits, $N_e/N$ is 0.021, close to the multiplicative bound value of 0.019. The model's larger reduction requires individuals with very high breeding values. Because physical limits on reproduction cut off the highest breeding values, the model can only bound the expected reduction rather than predict it (\autoref{sec:onemodel}).

\subsection{The predicted reduction is orders of magnitude smaller than the observed deficits}\label{sec:res_deficit}
Neutral diversity in the deficit set (\autoref{sec:pi_methods}) is far below what census size predicts. The observed diversity ratio, $\delta = \pi/(4\mu N_c)$, ranges from $10^{-2.4}$ in bighorn sheep to $10^{-4.6}$ in song sparrows (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}). At the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$, even the multiplicative bound leaves these deficits unexplained, by a factor of 80 in bighorn sheep and of up to 31,000 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe. In terms of the least variance that could account for each deficit, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}) ranges from 1.1 to 1.7, above the upper limit of the 95\% HPD interval of every one of the nine estimates, the largest of which is 0.70. Combining the predicted reduction with the demographic reduction does not change this, because even at the lower end of the demographic range, $N_{eV}/N = 0.1$~\cite{frankham1995}, the two together account for a deficit of at most about 20-fold in the median population.

How large is a long-term $\bar{V}_A$ of 1.1 to 1.7? Under the multiplicative model, an individual whose breeding value lies one standard deviation above the mean would expect three to four times as many offspring as an individual at the mean, a large but not inconceivable difference in a single generation, and a much harder one to sustain over the roughly $4N_e$ generations that neutral diversity reflects. At the median $\hat{V}_A$ of 0.10, the same individual expects about 37\% more offspring. % AUTHOR: own translation (multiplicative model, w proportional to e^z, one SD = sqrt(V_A): e^1.05 = 2.9 at V_A = 1.1, e^1.30 = 3.7 at 1.7, e^0.32 = 1.37 at 0.10); keep, re-voice or cut.
These factors cannot be explained by the biases in the census sizes, about 1.2 orders of magnitude in the inferred bird densities and a factor of 2 to 10 from counting all individuals rather than breeding adults only, and the two act in opposite directions (Methods).

\subsection{Linkage amplifies the reduction only where purging is slow}\label{sec:res_linked}
The predicted reduction in $N_e$ is stronger when accounting for linkage. Figure~\ref{fig:linked}a gives genome-wide $N_e/N$ for the mapped set (\autoref{sec:linked_methods}), combining the global reduction under the small-variance approximation with the local reduction along each chromosome, at $\hat{V}_A$ and the empirical DFE (Methods). The linked factor $\overline{\Omega}$ measures the local reduction relative to the global reduction (Equation~\eqref{linked_penalty}). In mammals, linkage lowers $N_e/N$ from 0.42--0.68 to 0.24--0.48. The effect varies more in birds, where with linkage $N_e/N$ is 0.68 in collared flycatchers and 0.44 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe but 0.077 in song sparrows and 0.019 in the great tits at Wytham Woods, differences driven mainly by $\hat{V}_A$.

Not a single $\hat{V}_A$ gives a reduction that allows a population to reach its observed deficit, either at its posterior mode, where the shortfall ranges from 75-fold (bighorn sheep) to 15,000-fold (great tits, Hoge Veluwe), or anywhere within its 95\% HPD interval. The closest is the song sparrow, whose upper HPD limit gives $N_e/N = 7.6 \times 10^{-4}$, against an observed diversity ratio of $2.6 \times 10^{-5}$.

The contrast between birds and mammals does not stem from genomic differences. At the same selection coefficient and $V_A$, the recombination maps alone give similar linked factors in the two taxa ($\overline{\Omega}$ = 0.55--0.73; Figure~\ref{fig:linked}b), although the bird genomes recombine about twice as much per megabase. The contrast is due to differences in the underlying DFE. The DFEs assigned to the birds have mean heterozygous effects of 0.0013--0.0021, an order of magnitude lower than those assigned to the mammals, 0.010--0.020. How long a neutral allele remains associated with a deleterious neighbor is set by whichever acts faster, selection removing the deleterious allele or recombination separating the two. Fast purging ends each association before recombination matters, whereas slow purging leaves recombination to end it, so tightly linked neighbors depress neutral variation for many more generations. Slow purging therefore generates a much larger reduction from the same $V_A$.

The local reduction is also steeply nonlinear in the amount of variance. The two great tit populations share maps and a DFE, yet $\overline{\Omega}$ is 0.031 at Wytham Woods, where $\hat{V}_A = 0.086$, and 0.49 at Hoge Veluwe, where $\hat{V}_A = 0.016$ (Figure~\ref{fig:S_omega}). Interestingly, the local reduction follows a single relationship within each genome (Figure~\ref{fig:linked}c). Chromosomes that carry more selected target per unit of map length are predicted to lose more of their neutral diversity, and the chromosomes of both taxa, macrochromosomes and microchromosomes alike, lie on the same curve. Local effective size is therefore predicted to vary among chromosomes with the density of functional sequence per Morgan.

The local reduction is large only when the variance is carried by slowly purged alleles. When we instead give every allele the effect $s^*$ at which MSDB maintains $\hat{V}_A$, the linked factor is 0.97--0.99 in every population. Alleles of that effect are purged within a few generations, before recombination would separate them from most linked sites, so linkage adds almost nothing to the unlinked reduction. The alleles that would make linkage matter are thus the ones least able to maintain $\hat{V}_A$. Partitioning the variance among chromosomes by gene count rather than by coding length does not change these results (Figure~\ref{fig:S_fgene}).

\subsection{No estimate reaches the variance its deficit requires, even with linkage}\label{sec:res_ceiling}
No pedigree estimate exceeds the ceiling that its population's genetic diversity implies. Under the small-variance approximation, a measured variance would exceed the ceiling $V_{A,\max}$ only in a population that retains more than about a fifth of the genetic diversity expected under neutrality (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}). None of the 19 estimates does so at $\delta \le 0.2$, two do at $\delta$ = 0.25--0.3 (the blue tits at Pirio and Muro), and five at $\delta = 0.5$. Every observed $\delta$ lies two to four orders of magnitude below these values. Neutral diversity in these populations therefore does not set an upper limit on their fitness variance. Instead, the measured variance is too small to have generated the observed deficit in diversity.

With linkage, the variance needed is lower and depends on each population's maps and DFE (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}, green). At the observed deficits it is 0.28--0.41 in the four bird populations of both test sets and about 1.0 in bighorn sheep and rhesus macaques, against 1.1--1.7 without linkage. Even these lower values exceed the upper limit of the 95\% HPD interval of each population's $\hat{V}_A$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}, points). Small as it is against the deficits, the measured variance is, as we show next, far larger than recurrent mutation can maintain.

\subsection{Maintaining the measured variance would require a mean deleterious effect of about 15\%}\label{sec:res_msd}
Figure~\ref{fig:selection} gives, for the 18 populations with an annotated assembly, the mean heterozygous selection coefficient that deleterious mutations would require for MSDB to maintain $\hat{V}_A$, $s^* = V_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}). The posterior modes range from 0.0045 in snow voles to 0.64 in the blue tits at Muro and the spotted hyenas, with a median of about 0.15. The empirical DFE assigned to each population has a mean of 0.0013--0.0021 in birds and 0.010--0.020 in mammals. In 17 of the 18 populations the posterior probability that $s^*$ exceeds that mean is at least 0.89. The exception is the snow vole, whose estimate is close to zero (0.70). At the posterior mode, $s^*$ is a median of 18 times the assigned mean: 7 to 480 times in birds, and up to 33 times in mammals. In four populations, the spotted hyenas, the blue tits at Muro and Pirio, and the hihi on Tiritiri Matangi, more than 5\% of the posterior lies where $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds $U_d$ itself, a variance that no selection coefficient can maintain.

Maintaining $\hat{V}_A$ by recurrent mutation would therefore require the average new mutation in the functional genome to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 15\%. Estimates of the DFE place most new nonsynonymous mutations far below that, with mean effects of a fraction of a percent in birds and one to two percent in mammals, and a large share of them nearly neutral~\cite{eyrewalker2007,lin2026}. A tail of large effects hidden from site-frequency spectra cannot raise the mean that far, because it would require a heterozygous effect of 0.5 in about 30\% of new mutations. Drift does not change this conclusion. Wherever diversity gives $N_e$, $2N_es^*$ is 2,900 to 320,000 (11 populations), so these populations are in the regime where MSDB maintains $U_d\bar{s}$, and the weak-selection regime would require an effective size well below $U_d/(2V_A)$, which is 3.1 individuals for the median population (0.64--81). With the phylogenetic mutation rate, the median $s^*$ is 0.12. Applying the model prospectively, the variance that each population's DFE would maintain, $U_d\bar{s}$, is 0.0005 to 0.02. The posterior mode of $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds it in 16 of 18 populations, by a median factor of 21 (Figure~\ref{fig:S_gap}). At the effects $\hat{V}_A$ requires, purging would also shorten an ancestor's advantage at unlinked sites, by the factor $1/(1 + s^*)^2$ of Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}, 0.37--0.99 (median 0.75), so the $Q = 2$ used above overstates the global reduction.

\subsection{No single long-term variance passes both tests}\label{sec:res_rescaling}
The two tests ask opposite things of the long-term variance. We rescaled the pedigree estimate, $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$, for the deficit set (\autoref{sec:pi_methods}) and followed each test as $c$ changes (Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling}). The maintenance test is passed only for $c \le 0.024$ on average across species (0.0017--0.11 across populations), a long-term variance about forty times smaller than the pedigree estimate. The observed deficit is explained only for $c \ge 9.4$ on average (2.4--80 across populations, median 14), a long-term variance about ten times larger. The two requirements are separated by a factor of about 390 on average, and by 108 to 1,890 within every population. Every population in the deficit set therefore lies in the lower right quadrant of Table~\ref{tab:c}, where no rescaling passes both tests and the departure is from the equilibrium assumed by the two models. Where the two average curves cross, at $c \approx 3.9$, the long-term variance would be 160 times more than MSDB maintains and would still leave 160 times more diversity than is observed.

The tension here does not depend on the pedigree estimates (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}). The variance that each population's DFE can maintain, 0.0007 to 0.013 in the deficit set, would lower neutral diversity at unlinked sites by only 0.6 to 5\% even under the multiplicative bound ($N_e/N$ = 0.95--0.99), against observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. A fitness variance maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation is too small, by orders of magnitude, to account for the diversity these populations lack, whatever value the pedigrees return. The estimates locate where the populations sit between the two ends: roughly 10 to 600 times above what maintenance allows, and well short of what the deficit requires.

Adding linkage narrows the gap but does not resolve it (Figure~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}). For the six populations of both sets, the long-term variance that would explain the deficit with linkage is 3.3 to 22.6 times the pedigree estimate in birds and 3.8 to 9.0 times in mammals. That is still 81 to 442 times what MSDB at the same DFE maintains. At the variance MSDB does maintain, linkage barely changes the prediction. Averaged over species, predicted diversity exceeds observed diversity 4,240-fold with linkage and 4,430-fold without. MSDB with background selection, at the variance it can sustain, therefore accounts for almost none of the missing diversity. Linkage removes diversity substantially only at variances that MSDB cannot maintain, carried by alleles whose effects are too weak to maintain them.
