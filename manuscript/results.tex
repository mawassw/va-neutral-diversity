\section{Results}
We report the two comparisons in turn: first the effect of the measured variance on neutral diversity, then its maintenance. Each analysis uses the populations that have the data it needs. All 19 populations of Bonnet et al. (2022) enter the genome-wide reduction in effective size, and the 18 with an annotated assembly enter the maintenance comparison. Nine populations, in six species, have both a measure of neutral diversity and a census size; we call them the deficit set. Nine have linkage maps, the mapped set, and six populations belong to both. Throughout, $N_e/N$ is relative to the number of breeding adults (\autoref{sec:quantity}) and $\delta$ is the observed diversity ratio. None of the nine values of $\delta$ is at or above one, so no population was set aside as a failed model check.

\subsection{Heritable variance in fitness reduces effective size a few-fold}\label{sec:res_unlinked}
Table~\ref{tab:reduction} and Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked} give the reduction in effective size at unlinked sites implied by each pedigree estimate. At first order, $N_e/N$ runs from 0.22 in the blue tits at Muro to 0.99, with a median of 0.60 (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}A). Inherited differences in reproductive success alone therefore make the median population drift as fast as one with 60\% of its breeding adults, a reduction of the same order as those commonly attributed to variance in family size and unequal sex ratios (Frankham 1995). % CITE: Frankham 1995, Genet. Res. 66:95-107; not yet in references.bib
The reduction is largest where the variance is largest, in the blue tits at Pirio and Muro and the spotted hyenas, and negligible where the posterior mode is near zero. For a given variance, populations with a social pair bond lose more effective size than those without (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}B; \autoref{sec:mating}).

The multiplicative model of fitness, on which the animal-model estimate rests, gives a larger reduction. It departs appreciably from the first-order value only in the five populations with $\hat{V}_A$ above about 0.25: the blue tits at Pirio and Muro, spotted hyenas, red deer and bighorn sheep (Figure~\ref{fig:unlinked}A, triangles). Its median is 0.51. For the Muro blue tits $N_e/N$ falls to 0.021, close to the multiplicative bound, 0.019. These values rest on the upper tail of breeding values, which ceilings on reproduction truncate, so they bound the reduction rather than predict it (\autoref{sec:onemodel}).

\subsection{The reduction falls far short of the missing diversity}\label{sec:res_deficit}
Neutral diversity in the deficit set is far below what census size predicts. The observed diversity ratio, $\delta = \pi/(4\mu N_c)$, runs from 1/230 in bighorn sheep to 1/38,000 in song sparrows (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}). At the posterior modes even the multiplicative bound falls short of these deficits, by a factor of 80 in bighorn sheep and of up to 31,000 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe. Read the other way, the least variance that could account for each deficit, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}), is 1.1 to 1.7. That is above the upper limit of the 95\% HPD interval of every one of the nine estimates, the largest of which is 0.70.

A long-term variance of this size would mean that an individual whose breeding value lies one standard deviation above the mean expects three to four times as many offspring as an individual at the mean. It would have to do so in every generation, over the roughly $4N_e$ generations that neutral diversity reflects. At the median measured variance, 0.10, the same individual expects about 37\% more offspring. % AUTHOR: own translation (multiplicative model, w proportional to e^z, one SD = sqrt(V_A): e^1.05 = 2.9 at V_A = 1.1, e^1.30 = 3.7 at 1.7, e^0.32 = 1.37 at 0.10); keep, re-voice or cut.
Neither bias in the census sizes, about 1.2 orders of magnitude in the bird densities and a factor of 2 to 10 from counting all individuals rather than breeding adults, approaches these factors, and the two act in opposite directions (Methods).

\subsection{Linked selection deepens the local reduction, mostly in birds}\label{sec:res_linked}
At linked sites the reduction is stronger. Figure~\ref{fig:linked}A gives genome-wide $N_e/N$ for the mapped set, combining the global reduction at first order with the local reduction along each chromosome, at the measured variance and the assigned DFE (Methods). We write $\overline{\Omega}$ for the linked factor, the local reduction relative to the global one (Equation~\eqref{linked_penalty}). In mammals, linkage lowers $N_e/N$ from 0.42--0.68 to 0.24--0.48. In birds the effect varies much more: with linkage $N_e/N$ is 0.68 in collared flycatchers and 0.44 in the great tits at Hoge Veluwe, but 0.077 in song sparrows and 0.019 in the great tits at Wytham Woods.

No population reaches its observed deficit, either at its posterior mode, where the shortfall runs from 75-fold (bighorn sheep) to 15,000-fold (great tits, Hoge Veluwe), or anywhere within its 95\% HPD interval. The closest is the song sparrow, whose upper HPD limit gives $N_e/N = 7.6 \times 10^{-4}$, against an observed diversity ratio of $2.6 \times 10^{-5}$.

The contrast between birds and mammals does not come from their genomes. At a common selection coefficient and variance, the recombination maps alone give similar linked factors in the two taxa ($\overline{\Omega}$ = 0.55--0.73; Figure~\ref{fig:linked}B), although the bird genomes recombine about twice as much per megabase. The contrast comes from the DFE. The DFEs assigned to the birds have mean heterozygous effects of 0.0013--0.0021, an order of magnitude below those assigned to the mammals, 0.010--0.020. A weakly deleterious allele persists for many generations before selection removes it, and for as long as it persists it lowers the representation of the neutral variants on its own haplotype. The longer it persists, the farther along the chromosome that association extends before recombination breaks it. Weak purging therefore turns the same variance into a much larger local reduction.

The local reduction is also steeply nonlinear in the amount of variance. The two great tit populations share maps and a DFE, yet $\overline{\Omega}$ is 0.031 at Wytham Woods, where $\hat{V}_A = 0.086$, and 0.49 at Hoge Veluwe, where $\hat{V}_A = 0.016$ (Figure~\ref{fig:S_omega}). Within each genome the local reduction follows a single relationship (Figure~\ref{fig:linked}C). Chromosomes that carry more selected target per unit of map length lose more of their neutral diversity, and the chromosomes of both taxa, macrochromosomes and microchromosomes alike, fall along the same curve. Local effective size is therefore predicted to vary among chromosomes with the density of functional sequence per Morgan, the pattern that maps of background selection describe within genomes (McVicker et al. 2009).

The local reduction is large only when the variance is carried by weakly selected alleles. If each allele instead had the effect at which MSDB maintains the measured variance, $s^*$, the linked factor would be 0.97--0.99 in every population. Alleles of that effect are purged within a few generations, and their associations with linked sites do not spread far. The alleles that would make linkage matter are thus the ones least able to maintain the variance we measured, a point we return to below. Partitioning the variance among chromosomes by gene count rather than by coding length leaves these results unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:S_fgene}).

\subsection{Neutral diversity sets no upper limit on the measured variance}\label{sec:res_ceiling}
No pedigree estimate exceeds the ceiling that its population's diversity would set. At first order a measured variance would exceed the ceiling $V_{A,\max}$ only in a population that retains more than about a fifth of its neutral expectation (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}). None of the 19 estimates does so at $\delta \le 0.2$, two do at $\delta$ = 0.25--0.3 (the blue tits at Pirio and Muro), and five at $\delta = 0.5$. Every observed $\delta$ lies two to four orders of magnitude below these values. Neutral diversity in these populations therefore sets no upper limit on their fitness variance. The constraint runs the other way: the measured variance is too small to have produced the diversity they lack.

Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling} gives both forms: the first-order ceiling $V_{A,\max}$ where it holds, and the least variance $V_{A,\delta}$ at every $\delta$. With linkage, the variance needed is lower and depends on each population's maps and DFE (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}, teal). At the observed deficits it falls to 0.28--0.41 in the four bird populations of both sets, and to about 1.0 in bighorn sheep and rhesus macaques, from 1.1--1.7 without linkage. Even these lower values exceed the upper limit of the 95\% HPD interval of each population's estimate.

\subsection{Maintaining the measured variance would require strongly deleterious mutations}\label{sec:res_msd}
Figure~\ref{fig:selection} gives, for the 18 populations with an annotated assembly, the mean heterozygous selection coefficient that deleterious mutations would need for MSDB to maintain the measured variance, $s^* = V_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}), carrying the uncertainty in both $V_A$ and the mutation rate. The posterior modes run from 0.0045 in snow voles to 0.64 in the blue tits at Muro and the spotted hyenas, with a median of about 0.15. The empirical DFE assigned to each population has a mean of 0.0013--0.0021 in birds and 0.010--0.020 in mammals. In 17 of the 18 populations the posterior probability that $s^*$ exceeds that mean is at least 0.89. The exception is the snow vole, whose estimate is close to zero (0.70). At the posterior mode $s^*$ is a median of 18 times the assigned mean: 7 to 480 times in birds, and up to 33 times in mammals. In four populations, the spotted hyenas, the blue tits at Muro and Pirio, and the hihi on Tiritiri Matangi, more than 5\% of the posterior lies where $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds $U_d$ itself, a variance that no selection coefficient can maintain.

Maintaining the measured variance by recurrent mutation would therefore require the average new mutation in the functional genome to lower the fitness of its heterozygous carriers by about 15\%. Estimates of the DFE place most new nonsynonymous mutations far below that, with mean effects of a fraction of a percent in birds and one to two percent in mammals, and a large share of them nearly neutral (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Lin et al. 2026). Drift does not change this conclusion. Wherever diversity gives $N_e$, $2N_es^*$ is 2,900 to 320,000 (11 populations), so these populations are in the regime where MSDB maintains $U_d\bar{s}$, and the weak-selection branch would need an effective size well below $U_d/(2V_A)$, which is 3.1 individuals for the median population (0.64--81). The comparison is also conservative, because $U_d$ counts every mutation in the exome and an equal regulatory target as deleterious (Methods). With the phylogenetic mutation rate the median $s^*$ is 0.12. Read forward, the variance that each population's DFE would maintain, $U_d\bar{s}$, is 0.0005 to 0.02. The measured mode exceeds it in 16 of 18 populations, by a median factor of 21 (Figure~\ref{fig:S_gap}). At the effects the measured variance requires, purging would also shorten an ancestor's advantage at unlinked sites: the factor $1/(1 + s^*)^2$ of Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge} is 0.37--0.99 (median 0.75), so the $Q = 2$ used above overstates the global reduction. Charlesworth (2015) reached the same conclusion for fitness components in \textit{Drosophila}; it holds here for lifetime fitness in wild vertebrates.

\subsection{No single long-term variance satisfies both comparisons}\label{sec:res_rescaling}
The two comparisons ask opposite things of the long-term variance. Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling} shows how each responds when the pedigree estimate is rescaled, $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$, for the deficit set. MSDB at the assigned DFE is satisfied only for $c \le 0.024$ on average across species (0.0017--0.11 across populations), a long-term variance about forty times smaller than the pedigree estimate. The observed deficit is explained only for $c \ge 9.4$ on average (2.4--80 across populations, median 14), a long-term variance about ten times larger. The two requirements are separated by a factor of about 390 on average, and by 108 to 1,890 within every population. Where the two average curves cross, at $c \approx 3.9$, the long-term variance would be 160 times more than MSDB maintains and would still leave 160 times more diversity than is observed. Because any single correction to the estimate moves the two verdicts in opposite directions (\autoref{sec:quantity}), no account of what the pedigree estimate misses can close this gap.

The conflict does not depend on the pedigree estimates (Equation~\eqref{eq:compat}). The variance that each population's DFE can maintain, 0.0007 to 0.013 in the deficit set, would lower neutral diversity at unlinked sites by only 0.6 to 5\% even under the multiplicative bound ($N_e/N$ = 0.95--0.99), against observed deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold. A fitness variance maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation is too small, by orders of magnitude, to account for the diversity these populations lack, whatever value the pedigrees return. The estimates locate where the populations sit between the two ends: roughly 10 to 600 times above what maintenance allows, and well short of what the deficit requires.

Adding linkage narrows the gap but does not close it (Figure~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}). For the six populations of both sets, the long-term variance that would explain the deficit with linkage is 3.3 to 22.6 times the pedigree estimate in birds and 3.8 to 9.0 times in mammals. That is still 81 to 442 times what MSDB at the same DFE maintains. At the variance MSDB does maintain, linkage barely changes the prediction. Averaged over species, predicted diversity exceeds observed diversity 4,240-fold with linkage and 4,430-fold without. MSDB with background selection, at the variance it can sustain, therefore accounts for almost none of the missing diversity. Linkage removes diversity substantially only at variances that MSDB cannot maintain, carried by alleles whose effects are too weak to maintain them.

Taken together, the pedigree estimates of heritable variance in fitness in wild vertebrates sit between two equilibrium expectations that do not meet (Figure~\ref{fig:S_gap}). They are large enough to reduce effective size up to about fivefold at unlinked sites at first order, and more at linked sites in birds. They are not large enough to account for the deficits in neutral diversity, which would require a long-term variance above one. And they are a median of about 20 times, and up to several hundred times, larger than recurrent deleterious mutation can maintain at the DFEs measured in related species.
