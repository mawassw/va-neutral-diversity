\section{Materials and Methods}
All inputs pass through one reproducible data layer (\texttt{scripts/R/00\_prepare\_data.R}), which joins the sources below through a single population crosswalk and writes the tables every analysis reads.

\subsection{Additive variance in fitness}
Posterior distributions of $V_A$ come from Bonnet et al. (2022): 19 longitudinally monitored wild vertebrate populations, 11 socially monogamous (10 bird populations and the meerkat) and 8 without a pair bond (polygynous or polygynandrous mammals). Bonnet et al. fitted a zero-inflated over-dispersed Poisson animal model to lifetime breeding success, with both sexes in each data set and sex included in the model, and back-transformed the link-scale genetic covariance matrix to the observed scale as $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$. The estimates are therefore already additive variance in relative fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$), the quantity of \autoref{sec:quantity}, and no standardization was applied. For each population we use the 4,000 posterior draws, summarized by the posterior mode and the 95\% highest posterior density interval. The modes run from 0.003 to 0.50 (median 0.10).

\subsection{Mating system}
Each population was classed as socially monogamous or without a pair bond. For socially monogamous populations, the extra-pair paternity rate $\alpha$ was taken from published molecular parentage studies of the same or a conspecific population [author: list sources per population, and state whether $\alpha$ is the proportion of offspring, as Appendix B assumes, or of broods]. The 11 rates run from 0.047 (great tits) to 0.76 (superb fairy-wren). The mating-system scalar is $\kappa = 1$ without a pair bond and $\kappa_B(\alpha)$ (Appendix B, Model B) under social monogamy, giving $\kappa = 1.22$--$1.93$. Model A is the sensitivity bound. The genome-wide reduction sets the retention $Z = 1$, exact for a Gaussian, infinitesimal breeding value under the multiplicative model and an upper bound on the reduction when $V_A$ is carried by deleterious alleles (Appendix A.4), and $r_{mf} = 0$, for want of estimates of assortative pairing on fitness. Under the multiplicative model the reduction is computed exactly from Appendix A.3, and its upper bound uses $\kappa(0)$, the scalar without extra-pair paternity: 2 with a pair bond and 1 without (Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult_pair}).

\subsection{Genome assemblies, functional target size and mutation rates}
Chromosome-level assemblies and annotations were retrieved from NCBI for 18 of the 19 populations; the only superb fairy-wren assembly is unannotated. For each assembled autosome we recorded physical length, the number of annotated protein-coding genes, and coding length $L_{\text{coding},i}$. Coding length was computed by collapsing all CDS features into non-redundant intervals, so that isoforms and overlapping reading frames are not counted twice. Sex chromosomes, mitochondrial sequence and unplaced scaffolds were excluded. Two species lack a chromosome-level annotated assembly: the spotted hyena and the hihi. For these the genome-wide CDS total was scaled by the autosomal CDS share of the nearest chromosome-level relative: 0.965 from the meerkat for the hyena, and 0.948, the mean of four passerines, for the hihi.

Per-site mutation rates were carried under two estimates, not averaged. The first, $\mu_\text{phylo}$, is the phylogenetic mixed-model prediction of Wang and Obbard (2023). The second, $\mu_\text{direct}$, is a direct pedigree or trio estimate for the species or its closest relative, compiled by Lewin and Eyre-Walker (2026). The phylogenetic model is fitted to the direct estimates, so the two are not independent, and averaging their credible bounds would not produce a credible interval. Results use $\mu_\text{direct}$ and are repeated under $\mu_\text{phylo}$, which runs 7--60\% higher. The diploid deleterious mutation rate is $U_d = 4\mu L_\text{coding}$, summed over assembled autosomes (\autoref{msdb}), and runs 0.34--1.26 under $\mu_\text{direct}$. The factor 4 is $2\mu$ per base pair for the diploid genome, times two for a regulatory target equal in length to the annotated autosomal exome. Treating every mutation in that target as deleterious maximizes $U_d$ and so minimizes $s^*$, which makes the comparison with empirical DFEs favourable to mutation--selection balance.

\subsection{Mutation--selection--drift comparison}
For every posterior draw of $V_A$ we computed $s^* = V_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}), pairing each draw with a draw of $U_d$ from the lognormal implied by the 95\% CI of the direct mutation rate, so that both uncertainties are carried; the median under the phylogenetic rate is also reported. Each posterior is compared with $\bar{s}$, the mean heterozygous effect of the empirical DFE assigned to the population (\autoref{sec:rescaling_methods}; Lin et al. 2026), the same DFE used for the linked term and the rescaling. We report the posterior mode and HPD intervals of $s^*$, the posterior probability $P(s^* > \bar{s})$, and $P(s^* > 1)$, the probability that the measured variance exceeds $U_d$ itself (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}). We confirmed the strong-selection limit by computing $2N_es^*$, with $N_e = \pi/(4\mu)$, wherever $\pi$ is available. Read forward, $U_d\,\bar{s}$ is the variance mutation--selection balance maintains (Figure~\ref{fig:S_gap}).

\subsection{Linked interference}
Linkage maps were joined to assemblies chromosome by chromosome, by curated linkage-group correspondence. For the song sparrow, whose assembly numbers chromosomes by size, the join goes through gene-symbol orthology to the great tit assembly. Map lengths $M_i$ are in Morgans [author: confirm whether sex-averaged maps were used throughout]. Nine populations have mapped autosomes; the others contribute to the unlinked analyses only. Every autosome with a map length of at least 1 cM enters the linked term, microchromosomes included.

Additive variance was partitioned among autosomes in proportion to coding length, $v_i = V_A f_i$ with $f_i = L_{\text{coding},i}/\sum_i L_{\text{coding},i}$; the partition by annotated gene count is reported as a sensitivity analysis [author: confirm which partition is primary]. The variance on the focal haplotype is $v_h = v_i/2$. $\overline{Q^2}_i$ was evaluated in the closed form of Appendix C (Equation~\eqref{eq:C_closed_haldane}), which agrees with quadrature of Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} to better than one part in $10^9$ over the map lengths and effects used here. Each effect class is retained at $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), which does not depend on $V_A$, and the linked and unlinked terms are averaged over classes with weights $\nu_k$. We evaluate uniform effects $s = 0.005$, $0.01$ and $0.02$, spanning vertebrate DFE means, and $s = s^*$, the effect at which mutation--selection balance maintains the measured variance. For $s < s^*$ the measured $V_A$ exceeds what balance maintains, so those values give the linked reduction if the measured variance persisted as alleles of that effect. We also evaluate the gamma DFEs estimated by Lin et al. (2026), assigning each population the DFE of its own species where one exists, and otherwise an equal mixture of its closest relatives' DFEs (\autoref{sec:rescaling_methods}). This assigned DFE is used throughout: in Figures~\ref{fig:linked}--\ref{fig:selection}, and on both sides of the rescaling. For a gamma DFE of shape $\alpha$ and scale $\beta'$ the weights $\nu$ form the Gamma($\alpha + 1$, $\beta'$) density on $s$, which we discretize on 240 log-spaced classes between $10^{-8}$ and 1 (relative error below $10^{-3}$ against 960 classes). The chromosome-level reduction follows Equation~\eqref{total_red}, multiplicative across loci, with the same $Z$ in the linked and unlinked terms. The genome-wide value weights chromosomes by physical length. Where the linked reduction is set against an observed deficit (Figures~\ref{fig:linked} and~\ref{fig:ceiling}, and Figure~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}), the global factor is taken at $Q = 2$, its upper bound (\autoref{sec:unlinked}): at first order with $\kappa$ in Figure~\ref{fig:linked}, and as the multiplicative bound with $\kappa(0)$ in Figures~\ref{fig:ceiling} and~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}. $\overline{\Omega}$ is evaluated at the class retentions of the assigned DFE, so each combination is the most generous to explaining the deficit at its order, given that DFE.

\subsection{Nucleotide diversity and census size}
Neutral autosomal nucleotide diversity $\pi$ is available from Lewin and Eyre-Walker (2026) for 7 of the 19 populations, 5 of them for the study species itself. For four further populations we use published whole-genome estimates from another population of the same species. These are the two great tit populations (Barton and Zeng 2019), bighorn sheep (Wang et al. 2023) and song sparrow (Carbeck et al. 2023). The bighorn and song sparrow values come from MAF-filtered SNP sets and are likely biased low. Single-genome heterozygosity is recorded but not used as $\pi$. Census sizes $N_c$ are those published by Buffalo (2021), for the study species only (10 of 19). They are density $\times$ range estimates. His bird densities rest on body mass predicted from body length and are likely about 1.2 orders of magnitude too low, and his \emph{Ovis aries} value is domestic sheep, not the St Kilda Soay population. No population has a breeding-adult count. Using $N_c$ in place of $N$ lowers $\delta$ by the breeder fraction, a factor of 2--10, which widens the apparent gap. We record the instrument for each population.

Nine populations (six species) have both $\pi$ and $N_c$. For these we compare predicted and observed diversity in $\pi$ units, $\pi_\text{pred} = 4\mu N_c\,(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ against $\pi_\text{obs}$, with $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ at first order, $1/(1 + 4\kappa V_A)$, and at its multiplicative upper bound, $e^{-4\kappa(0)V_A}$. We report $\delta = \pi/(4\mu N_c)$ only as the axis of the $V_{A,\max}$ curves, and the smallest variance that could account for each observed $\delta$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}. Populations with $\delta \ge 1$ would make $V_{A,\max} \le 0$, a failed model check rather than a result, and are reported as uninformative. None of the nine falls in that class.

\subsection{Rescaling the long-run variance}\label{sec:rescaling_methods}
To ask whether any long-run variance satisfies both comparisons, we write $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}). For each of the nine populations we evaluate two ratios as functions of $c$. The first is $\bar{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$, with $\bar{s}$ the mean of an empirical DFE assigned to each population: its own species where one has been estimated (collared flycatcher), and otherwise an equal mixture of the DFEs of its closest relatives among those of Lin et al. (2026). These are pied and collared flycatchers for the other passerines, vaquita and fin whale for bighorn sheep, and human for rhesus macaques, giving $\bar{s}$ = 0.0013--0.0195. The same DFE sets the class retentions in the rescaling with linkage, so the two comparisons share one distribution of effects. The assignment rests on the phylogenetic structure of the DFE. Lin et al. (2026) found strong phylogenetic signal in the mean effect of new nonsynonymous mutations (Pagel's $\lambda = 0.84$ for $\log_{10}$ mean $|s|$), and species pairs within a class had more similar DFEs than pairs across classes, so the closest available relative is the best predictor of a species' DFE. Where two available species are equally related to the study species, as the two \emph{Ficedula} flycatchers are to the other passerines and the two cetaceans are to bighorn sheep within Cetartiodactyla, we use their equal mixture rather than choose between them. The avian assignments are the better constrained. Within mammals Lin et al.\ found the signal weak ($\lambda = 0.45$, not significant), and the human DFE assigned to rhesus macaques comes from the same pipeline as the others, although a lower mean has been reported for humans (Kim et al. 2017). Using one DFE on both sides also keeps the comparison even-handed. A smaller mean effect lowers the variance balance can maintain and raises the linked reduction, so a DFE chosen to favour one comparison would penalize the other. The assignments are recorded in data/dfe\_assignment.csv.

The second is $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}/\delta$: the diversity the model predicts is retained, over the diversity observed to be retained. It is computed with the multiplicative upper bound, $(N_e/N)_\text{pred} = e^{-4\kappa(0)\bar{V}_A}$, which is the form most favourable to explaining the deficit and is valid at every $\bar{V}_A$. The first-order form, $1/(1 + 4\kappa\bar{V}_A)$, is shown alongside it while $\bar{V}_A$ is small. A comparison is satisfied where its ratio is at most 1. Populations of a species share $\pi$ and $N_c$, so log ratios are averaged within species first. We then report the mean across species with a $t$-based 95\% interval (Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling}). With linkage, the diversity ratio is multiplied by $\overline{\Omega}(\bar{V}_A)$ at the assigned DFE, and we report it at $c = U_d\bar{s}/\hat{V}_A$, where the variance is what that DFE maintains, as the self-consistent prediction of mutation--selection balance with background selection.

\subsection{Independence of the inputs from diversity data}
None of the model's inputs is estimated from the neutral diversity it is compared against. $V_A$ comes from pedigrees and breeding records (Bonnet et al. 2022). $\alpha$ comes from parentage analyses, $M_i$ from linkage maps, and $f_i$ and $L_\text{coding}$ from genome annotations. $\mu$ comes from direct pedigree or trio sequencing, or from a phylogenetic model fitted to such estimates, and $N_c$ from density and range. The diversity side of the comparison is therefore a prediction tested against $\pi_\text{obs}$, not a fit to it. The one shared source is Lewin and Eyre-Walker (2026), which compiles both $\mu_\text{direct}$ and $\pi$; the two are measured independently.
