\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Pedigree estimates of additive variance in fitness}
Posterior distributions of $V_A$ come from Bonnet et al. (2022): 19 longitudinally monitored wild vertebrate populations, 11 socially monogamous (10 bird populations and the meerkat) and 8 randomly mating (polygynous or polygynandrous mammals). Bonnet et al. fitted a zero-inflated over-dispersed Poisson animal model (Henderson 1975; Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010) to lifetime breeding success, with both sexes in each data set and sex as a fixed effect, and back-transformed the estimates to the observed scale of relative fitness (\autoref{sec:quantity}), so no standardization was applied. Every data set includes individuals that never bred: zero lifetime breeding success makes up 53\% (yellow baboons, Amboseli) to 96\% (blue tits, Muro) of the individuals with a record. For each population we used the 4,000 posterior draws, summarized by the posterior mode and the 95\% highest posterior density (HPD) interval. The modes range from 0.003 to 0.50 (median 0.10).

\subsection{Mating system and the reduction at unlinked sites}
We classed each population as socially monogamous or randomly mating. For socially monogamous populations, we took the extra-pair paternity rate $\alpha$, the proportion of offspring not sired by the social male, from published molecular parentage studies (data/epp\_sources.csv): blue tits, Charmantier and Blondel (2003) and Charmantier et al. (2004); collared flycatchers, Sheldon and Ellegren (1999); superb fairy-wrens, Hajduk et al. (2018); song sparrows, Sardell et al. (2010); hihi, Morland et al. (2024); great tits, Patrick et al. (2012) for Wytham Woods and Bircher et al. (2020) for Hoge Veluwe; and meerkats, Herdtle et al. (2025), for whom $\alpha$ is the share of the dominant female's pups not sired by the dominant male. For the Karori hihi and the Hoge Veluwe great tits no estimate exists, and we used the Tiritiri Matangi value and the neighboring Westerheide value, respectively. The 11 rates range from 0.097 (meerkats) to 0.61 (superb fairy-wren). The mating-system scalar is $\kappa = 1$ under random mating and $\kappa(\alpha)$ under social monogamy (Equation~\eqref{eq:kappaB}; Model B of Appendix~B), giving $\kappa = 1.32$--$1.86$. The alternative in which extra-pair siring is independent of male breeding value (Model A) was used as a sensitivity analysis. The genome-wide reduction was computed at $Z = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$ (\autoref{sec:unlinked}), the latter for want of estimates of assortative pairing on fitness. Under the multiplicative model we computed the reduction exactly from Equation~\eqref{eq:B_moments} (Appendix~B), and its upper bound with $\kappa_\text{max}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult_pair}).

\subsection{Mutational target and deleterious mutation rate}
We retrieved chromosome-level assemblies and annotations from NCBI for 18 of the 19 populations; the only superb fairy-wren assembly is unannotated. For each assembled autosome we recorded physical length, the number of annotated protein-coding genes, and coding length $L_{\text{coding},i}$. Coding length was computed by collapsing all CDS features into non-redundant intervals, so that isoforms and overlapping reading frames are not counted twice. Sex chromosomes, mitochondrial sequence and unplaced scaffolds were excluded. Two species lack a chromosome-level annotated assembly: the spotted hyena and the hihi. For these we scaled the genome-wide CDS total by the autosomal CDS share of the nearest chromosome-level relative: 0.965 from the meerkat for the hyena, and 0.948, the mean of four passerines, for the hihi.

We carried per-site mutation rates under two estimates, without averaging them. The first, $\mu_\text{phylo}$, is the phylogenetic mixed-model prediction of Wang and Obbard (2023). The second, $\mu_\text{direct}$, is a direct pedigree or trio estimate for the species or its closest relative, compiled by Lewin and Eyre-Walker (2026). The phylogenetic model is fitted to the direct estimates, so the two are not independent and their intervals were not combined. Results use $\mu_\text{direct}$ and are repeated under $\mu_\text{phylo}$, which is 7--60\% higher. The diploid deleterious mutation rate is $U_d = 4\mu L_\text{coding}$, summed over assembled autosomes (\autoref{sec:msdb}), and ranges from 0.34 to 1.26 under $\mu_\text{direct}$. The factor 4 is $2\mu$ per base pair for the diploid genome, times two for a regulatory target equal in length to the annotated autosomal exome. Treating every mutation in that target as deleterious maximizes $U_d$ and so minimizes $s^*$, which biases the maintenance test toward passing.

\subsection{Assigning a distribution of fitness effects}\label{sec:dfe_methods}
Each population was assigned a single empirical distribution of fitness effects (DFE), used for the maintenance test, the linked term and the rescaling alike. We used the gamma DFEs of new nonsynonymous mutations estimated by Lin et al. (2026) with a single pipeline across species. A population received the DFE of its own species where one exists (collared flycatcher), and otherwise an equal mixture of the DFEs of its closest relatives among those available: pied and collared flycatchers for the other passerines, vaquita and fin whale for bighorn sheep, Soay sheep and red deer, human for rhesus macaques and yellow baboons, and house mouse for the rodents. The spotted hyenas and meerkats received the same cetacean mixture, the closest available within Laurasiatheria, because the carnivore (wolf) DFEs of Lin et al. have unconstrained heavy tails. The resulting mean heterozygous effects are $\bar{s}$ = 0.0013--0.0195. The assignment rests on the phylogenetic structure of the DFE: Lin et al. (2026) found strong phylogenetic signal in the mean effect of new nonsynonymous mutations (Pagel's $\lambda = 0.84$ for $\log_{10}$ mean $|s|$), and species pairs within a class had more similar DFEs than pairs across classes. Where two available species are equally related to the study species, as the two \emph{Ficedula} flycatchers are to the other passerines and the two cetaceans are to bighorn sheep within Cetartiodactyla, we used their equal mixture rather than choose between them. The avian assignments are the better constrained. Within mammals, Lin et al.\ found the signal weak ($\lambda = 0.45$, not significant), and the human DFE assigned to the primates comes from the same pipeline as the others, although a lower mean has been reported for humans (Kim et al. 2017). A single DFE for both tests keeps them even-handed, because a smaller mean effect lowers the variance MSDB can maintain and raises the linked reduction, so a DFE chosen to favor one test would penalize the other. For a gamma DFE of shape $b$ and scale $\beta$, the class weights $\nu_k$ form the Gamma($b + 1$, $\beta$) density on $s$, which we discretized on 240 log-spaced classes between $10^{-8}$ and 1 (relative error below $10^{-3}$ against 960 classes).

\subsection{Maintenance test}
For every posterior draw of $V_A$ we computed $s^* = V_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}), pairing each draw with a draw of $U_d$ from the lognormal implied by the 95\% CI of the direct mutation rate, so that both uncertainties are carried; the median under the phylogenetic rate is also reported. Each posterior was compared with the mean $\bar{s}$ of the population's assigned DFE (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}). We report the posterior mode and HPD intervals of $s^*$, the posterior probability $P(s^* > \bar{s})$, and $P(s^* > 1)$, the probability that the measured variance exceeds $U_d$ itself (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}). To check the strong-selection limit we computed $2N_es^*$, with $N_e = \pi/(4\mu)$ and $s^*$ at its posterior mode, wherever $\pi$ is available, and the effective size below which the weak-selection branch could hold, $U_d/(2V_A)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion}).

\subsection{Linkage maps and local effective size}\label{sec:linked_methods}
We joined linkage maps to assemblies chromosome by chromosome, by curated linkage-group correspondence. For the song sparrow, whose assembly numbers chromosomes by size, the join goes through gene-symbol orthology to the great tit assembly. Map lengths $M_i$ are sex-averaged, in Morgans. Nine populations have mapped autosomes; we call them the mapped set. The others are used in the unlinked analyses only. Every autosome with a map length of at least 1 cM, microchromosomes included, contributes to the linked term.

We partitioned additive variance among autosomes in proportion to coding length, $v_i = V_A f_i$ with $f_i = L_{\text{coding},i}/\sum_i L_{\text{coding},i}$, and report the partition by annotated gene count as a sensitivity analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:S_fgene}). $\overline{Q^2}_i$ was evaluated in the closed form of Appendix C (Equation~\eqref{eq:C_closed_haldane}), which agrees with quadrature of Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} to better than one part in $10^9$ over the map lengths and effects used here. Each effect class is retained at $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), and the local and global factors are averaged over the classes of the assigned DFE with weights $\nu_k$ (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}). To separate the maps from the DFE we also evaluated a uniform effect shared by all populations, $s = 0.01$ (Figure~\ref{fig:linked}b--c), and the effect $s = s^*$ at which MSDB maintains $\hat{V}_A$. The chromosome-level reduction follows Equation~\eqref{total_red}, with the same $Z$ in the local and global factors, and the genome-wide value weights chromosomes by physical length. Where the linked reduction is set against an observed deficit, each figure uses the form biased toward passing, with the global factor at $Q = 2$ (\autoref{sec:unlinked}), under the small-variance approximation with $\kappa$ in Figure~\ref{fig:linked}, and under the multiplicative bound with $\kappa_\text{max}$ in Figures~\ref{fig:ceiling} and~\ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}.

\subsection{Nucleotide diversity, census size and the diversity test}\label{sec:pi_methods}
Neutral autosomal nucleotide diversity $\pi$ is available from Lewin and Eyre-Walker (2026) for 7 of the 19 populations, 5 of them for the study species itself. For four further populations we used published whole-genome estimates from another population of the same species: the two great tit populations (Barton and Zeng 2019), bighorn sheep (Wang et al. 2023) and song sparrow (Carbeck et al. 2023). The bighorn and song sparrow values come from MAF-filtered SNP sets and are likely biased low. Single-genome heterozygosity was recorded but not used as $\pi$. Census sizes $N_c$ are those published by Buffalo (2021), for the study species only (10 of 19). They are density $\times$ range estimates. His bird densities rest on body mass predicted from body length and are likely about 1.2 orders of magnitude too low, and his \emph{Ovis aries} value is domestic sheep, not the St Kilda Soay population. No population has a breeding-adult count. Using $N_c$ in place of $N$ lowers $\delta$ by the breeder fraction, a factor of 2--10, which widens the apparent gap. We record the instrument for each population.

Nine populations (six species) have both $\pi$ and $N_c$; we call them the deficit set. Six of them also belong to the mapped set (\autoref{sec:linked_methods}). For these nine we compared predicted and observed diversity in $\pi$ units, $\pi_\text{pred} = 4\mu N_c\,(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ against $\pi_\text{obs}$, with $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ under the small-variance approximation, $1/(1 + 4\kappa V_A)$, and under the multiplicative bound, $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$. We report $\delta = \pi/(4\mu N_c)$ only as the axis of the $V_{A,\max}$ and $V_{A,\delta}$ curves. A population with $\delta \ge 1$ would give $V_{A,\max} \le 0$, a failed model check rather than a result, and would have been set aside; none of the nine has $\delta$ at or above one. We also report the least variance that could account for each observed $\delta$, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}). With linkage included, that variance is the root of Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} set equal to $\delta$, found by bracketed root-finding on $\log_{10} V_A$ (\texttt{uniroot} in R).

\subsection{Rescaling the long-term variance}\label{sec:rescaling_methods}
To ask whether any long-term variance passes both tests, we wrote $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}) and, for each of the nine populations, evaluated two ratios as functions of $c$. The first is $\bar{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$, at the assigned DFE (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}). The second is $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}/\delta$, the diversity the model predicts is retained over the diversity observed to be retained, computed with the multiplicative bound, $(N_e/N)_\text{pred} = e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}\bar{V}_A}$ ($\kappa_\text{max}$ = 1 under random mating, Barton and Etheridge 2011; 2 under strict monogamy, Weissman and Barton 2012). This form biases the diversity test toward passing, and it holds at every $\bar{V}_A$. The small-variance approximation, $1/(1 + 4\kappa\bar{V}_A)$, is shown alongside it while $\bar{V}_A$ is small. A test is passed where its ratio is at most 1. Populations of a species share $\pi$ and $N_c$, so we averaged log ratios within species first and report the mean across species with a $t$-based 95\% interval (Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling}). With linkage, the diversity ratio is multiplied by $\overline{\Omega}(\bar{V}_A)$ at the assigned DFE, and we report it at $c = U_d\bar{s}/\hat{V}_A$, where the variance is what that DFE maintains, as the self-consistent prediction of MSDB with background selection.

\subsection{Independence of the inputs from diversity data}
None of the model's inputs is estimated from the neutral diversity it is compared against. $V_A$ comes from pedigrees and breeding records (Bonnet et al. 2022). $\alpha$ comes from parentage analyses, $M_i$ from linkage maps, and $f_i$ and $L_\text{coding}$ from genome annotations. $\mu$ comes from direct pedigree or trio sequencing, or from a phylogenetic model fitted to such estimates, and $N_c$ from density and range. The diversity test is therefore a prediction checked against $\pi_\text{obs}$, not a fit to it. The one shared source is Lewin and Eyre-Walker (2026), which compiles both $\mu_\text{direct}$ and $\pi$; the two are measured independently.
