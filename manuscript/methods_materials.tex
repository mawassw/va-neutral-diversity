\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Pedigree estimates of additive genetic variance in fitness}\label{sec:meth_va}
Posterior distributions of the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, were obtained from Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} across 19 longitudinally monitored wild vertebrate populations: 11 socially monogamous populations (10 bird populations and the meerkat) and 8 polygynous or polygynandrous mammal populations (modeled under the $\kappa = 1$ random-mating baseline). Bonnet et al. fitted a zero-inflated overdispersed Poisson animal model~\cite{henderson1975,kruuk2004,wilson2010} to lifetime breeding success, including both sexes in each dataset with sex modeled as a fixed effect. Because their estimates were already back-transformed to the observed scale of relative fitness via the multivariate delta method (\autoref{sec:quantity}), we applied no further standardization. For each population, we used the 4,000 posterior draws of $\hat{V}_A$ from these model fits to compute the posterior distribution of each derived quantity, summarizing results by the posterior mode and the 95\% highest posterior density (HPD) interval. Across the 19 populations, the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$ range from 0.003 to 0.50 (median 0.10).

Every dataset includes individuals that died before reproductive age or otherwise never bred. In the phenotypic records of Bonnet et al. (their Data~S1, excluding individuals with missing lifetime breeding success), individuals with zero lifetime breeding success account for 53\% (yellow baboons, Amboseli) to 96\% (blue tits, Muro) of the cohort. Consequently, the empirical estimates $\hat{V}_A$ include heritable variance in pre-breeding survival, whereas our demographic parameters $N$, $Q$, and $Z$ refer strictly to breeding adults (\autoref{sec:quantity}). Because a gene copy sampled in a breeding adult ($t = 0$) has already survived to adulthood, an inherited advantage in pre-breeding survival can no longer change that adult's own contribution; the advantage first acts on the adult's offspring, raising their chance of surviving to breed ($t \ge 1$). The $t = 0$ term therefore drops out of the cumulative transmission factor, which becomes $Q = \sum_{t=1}^\infty (1/2)^t = 1$ rather than $Q = 2$~\cite{robertson1961,santiago1995}. Treating the entire $\hat{V}_A$ as adult fecundity variance ($Q = 2$) therefore overstates the predicted reduction in $N_e$ unless pre-breeding survival and adult fecundity are negatively genetically correlated (as under a life-history trade-off); we note the direction of this bias (\autoref{sec:approx}) given we cannot partition the two components with the immediately available data.

\subsection{Mating system and the reduction at unlinked sites}\label{sec:meth_mating}
We classified populations as socially monogamous when offspring are reared by a social pair---or by a dominant breeding pair in the cooperatively breeding superb fairy-wrens and meerkats---and as randomly mating otherwise. For each socially monogamous population, we obtained the extra-pair paternity rate $\alpha$---defined as the proportion of offspring not sired by the focal social male---from published molecular parentage studies of the same population (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). For superb fairy-wrens, offspring sired by within-group subordinate helpers were counted alongside extra-group paternity as not sired by the dominant male; for meerkats, $\alpha$ represents the proportion of the dominant female's pups not sired by the dominant male. Because no published parentage estimates were available for the Karori hihi or the Hoge Veluwe great tit populations, we substituted estimates from the source Tiritiri Matangi hihi population and the neighboring Westerheide great tit population ($\alpha = 0.177$; earlier estimates in that population ranged from $0.065$ to $0.125$~\cite{vanoers2008}), respectively. Across the 11 socially monogamous populations, $\alpha$ ranges from $0.097$ (meerkats) to $0.61$ (superb fairy-wrens).

We set the mating-system scalar to $\kappa = 1$ under random mating and evaluated $\kappa(\alpha)$ under social monogamy using Equation~\eqref{eq:kappaB} (Model~B of Appendix~\ref{app:B_kappa}), yielding $\kappa(\alpha)$ values between $1.32$ and $1.86$. We computed the reduction in $N_e$ at unlinked sites (\autoref{sec:unlinked}) assuming that a lineage keeps its full fitness advantage from one generation to the next and that mates are uncorrelated in their breeding values for fitness, the latter because no empirical estimates of assortative mating for total fitness exist. For each population, we evaluated \claude{the exact multiplicative reduction from the moment equation for long-term contributions (Equation}~\eqref{eq:B_moments}\claude{, Appendix}~\ref{app:B_mult}\claude{); the multiplicative bound using $\kappa_\text{max}$ (Equation}~\eqref{eq:ne_mult_pair}\claude{); and the small-variance approximation, the limit of both for small $V_A$ (Equation}~\eqref{unlinked_mating}\claude{).}

\subsection{Mutational target and deleterious mutation rate}\label{sec:meth_ud}
We downloaded genome assemblies and annotations from NCBI Datasets (accessions in Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). Sixteen populations have an annotated chromosome-level assembly, representing their own species in all cases except the yellow baboons and Soay sheep, for which we used the olive baboon (\emph{Papio anubis}) and domestic sheep (\emph{Ovis aries}, Rambouillet) reference assemblies, respectively. The spotted hyena and hihi assemblies are annotated only at the scaffold level, with the hihi annotation taken from the B10K consortium~\cite{feng2020}. Because the sole available superb fairy-wren assembly~\cite{penalba2020} is chromosome-level but unannotated, we applied the maintenance test to the remaining 18 populations. For each assembled autosome $i$, we recorded the physical length $L_i$, the number of annotated protein-coding genes, and the coding sequence length $L_{\text{coding},i}$. Coding length was computed by collapsing all annotated CDS (coding sequence) features into non-redundant genomic intervals so that alternative isoforms and overlapping reading frames were never double-counted. Sex chromosomes, mitochondrial sequences, and unplaced scaffolds were excluded. For the scaffold-level spotted hyena and hihi assemblies, we scaled the total genome-wide CDS length by the autosomal CDS fraction of their nearest chromosome-level relatives: $0.965$ for the hyena (from the meerkat) and $0.948$ for the hihi (the mean across the collared flycatcher, great tit, song sparrow, and blue tit assemblies).

We evaluated our models using two separate estimates of the per-site mutation rate $\mu$. The first, $\mu_\text{direct}$, is a direct pedigree or parent--offspring trio estimate for the focal species or its closest available relative, as compiled by Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026}. Species-specific direct estimates were available for the blue tit, collared flycatcher, and rhesus macaque~\cite{smeds2016,wang2020,bergeron2021,bergeron2023}; the remaining populations were assigned the direct estimate of their closest relative (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}, including feliform carnivore estimates from Armstrong et al.~\cite{armstrong2024}). The second estimate, $\mu_\text{phylo}$, is the phylogenetic mixed-model prediction of Wang and Obbard~\cite{wangobbard2023}, matched to the same species as $\mu_\text{direct}$ (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}) except for the meerkat, where we used the domestic cat (\emph{Felis catus}) prediction. Because the phylogenetic model is trained on the direct pedigree estimates, the two rates are not statistically independent and their uncertainty intervals were not combined. We report primary results under $\mu_\text{direct}$ and replicate all analyses under $\mu_\text{phylo}$, which is $7\%$ to $60\%$ higher across taxa.

In our baseline model, we defined the diploid autosomal deleterious mutation rate as $U_d = 4\mu L_\text{coding}$, where $L_\text{coding} = \sum_i L_{\text{coding},i}$ is summed over assembled autosomes (\autoref{sec:msdb}), yielding baseline values of $U_d$ from $0.34$ to $1.26$ under $\mu_\text{direct}$. Here, the factor of $4$ accounts for $2\mu$ mutations per base pair across the diploid genome and assumes an autosomal regulatory target equal in length to the protein-coding exome. The assumption we make here by treating every mutation across this combined target (including synonymous coding sites) as deleterious deliberately biases the maintenance test toward passing (\autoref{sec:approx}). Under this baseline, each chromosome's share of functional sequence in the linked model (\autoref{sec:linkage}) is $f_i = L_{\text{coding},i}/L_\text{coding}$. To test sensitivity to larger mutational targets, we also constructed an expanded target directly from each genome annotation: for each autosome $i$, we took the collapsed union of coding sequences, untranslated regions (UTRs) of protein-coding transcripts, and exons of non-coding RNA genes ($L_{\text{annot},i}$), plus a variable fraction $\zeta$ of all remaining unannotated autosomal sequence ($L_{\text{other},i} = L_i - L_{\text{annot},i}$). We then evaluated $U_d(\zeta) = 2\mu\sum_i(L_{\text{annot},i} + \zeta L_{\text{other},i})$ for $\zeta$ ranging from $0\%$ to $15\%$, updating the chromosomal shares $f_i$ accordingly (Figures~\ref{fig:S_noncoding} and~\ref{fig:S_noncoding_linked}). Because the B10K hihi annotation includes only protein-coding CDS features, we imputed its UTR and non-coding RNA fractions using the mean proportions across the four chromosome-level passerine assemblies.

\subsection{Assigning a distribution of fitness effects}\label{sec:dfe_methods}
We assigned each population an empirical distribution of fitness effects (DFE) using the gamma DFEs of new nonsynonymous mutations estimated across species with a uniform pipeline by Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026}. In their parameterization, the gamma scale parameter is expressed in units of $s$---obtained by dividing the population-scaled selection parameter by twice each species' ancestral effective size---and under their assumption of additive allelic effects, $\bar{s}$ represents the mean heterozygous selection coefficient. Because Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026} detected strong phylogenetic signal in the mean deleterious effect across vertebrates (Pagel's $\lambda = 0.84$ for $\log_{10}\bar{s}$) and greater DFE similarity within than between vertebrate classes, we assigned DFEs by phylogenetic proximity (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). Where a species-specific estimate was available (collared flycatcher), we assign its corresponding DFE; where two reference species were equally related to the focal taxon, we used an equal mixture of their DFEs. Specifically, the remaining passerines received an equal mixture of the pied and collared flycatcher (\emph{Ficedula} genus) DFEs; the ruminants (bighorn sheep, Soay sheep, and red deer) received an equal mixture of the vaquita and fin whale DFEs (the closest available relatives within Cetartiodactyla); the primates (rhesus macaques and yellow baboons) received the human DFE; and the rodents received the house mouse DFE. Because the carnivore (wolf) DFE fits in Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026} exhibited unconstrained heavy tails, we assigned the spotted hyena and meerkat the cetacean mixture as the closest well-constrained reference within Laurasiatheria. Across all populations, the resulting mean heterozygous effects range from $\bar{s} = 0.0013$ to $0.0195$.

While the avian assignments are tightly constrained, phylogenetic signal within mammals alone was weaker (Pagel's $\lambda = 0.45$, non-significant), and the human DFE assigned to the primates from the shared pipeline of Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026} has a higher mean than earlier human estimates~\cite{kim2017}. Crucially, however, shifting $\bar{s}$ moves our two tests in opposite directions: a smaller $\bar{s}$ reduces the variance maintainable at MSDB ($U_d\bar{s}$), making the maintenance test harder to pass, while simultaneously slowing purging ($Z_k = 1 - s_k$) and strengthening linked selection, making the diversity test easier to pass. Consequently, no alternative choice of DFE can simultaneously relax both equilibrium tests, and we applied the same assigned DFE consistently across the maintenance test, the linked reduction, and the scaling analysis. \claude{For a gamma DFE, the variance weights $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (}\autoref{sec:linkage}\claude{) follow a gamma density on $s$ with the same scale and a shape larger by one.} We discretized this density across 240 logarithmically spaced classes over $s \in [10^{-8}, 1]$, normalizing the class weights to sum to unity (yielding a relative error in $\overline{\Omega}$ below $2\times10^{-3}$ compared against 960 classes).

\subsection{Maintenance test}\label{sec:meth_maintenance}
For every posterior draw of $V_A$, we computed $s^* = V_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}), pairing each draw of $V_A$ with a draw of $U_d$ from the log-normal distribution implied by the 95\% confidence interval of the direct mutation rate so that both sources of uncertainty were fully propagated. (An equivalent log-normal propagation was applied under the phylogenetic rate $\mu_\text{phylo}$, for which we report posterior medians alongside the direct-rate results). Each posterior sample of $s^*$ was compared against the mean $\bar{s}$ of the population's assigned DFE (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}). We report the posterior mode and 95\% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals for $s^*$, as well as the posterior probabilities $P(s^* > \bar{s})$ and $P(s^* > 1)$ (the probability that the measured fitness variance exceeds the total deleterious mutation rate itself; Figure~\ref{fig:selection}). To empirically verify the strong-selection assumption of MSDB ($N_es \gg 1$), we evaluated $2N_es^*$ across all populations where neutral diversity data were available, setting $N_e = \pi/(4\mu)$, where $\pi$ is the genome-wide average pairwise nucleotide diversity, and $s^*$ at its posterior mode, and compared these against the critical threshold $U_d/(2V_A)$ below which the weak-selection drift regime would apply (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion}).

\subsection{Linkage maps and local effective size}\label{sec:linked_methods}
Genetic map lengths were compiled from published pedigree linkage maps (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). For yellow baboons, we used the map of a captive \emph{Papio hamadryas} colony~\cite{rogers2000}, substituting chromosomes 5, 12, and 18 from its second-generation revision~\cite{cox2006}. The rhesus macaque map~\cite{versoza2024} is male-only and derived from a captive colony of Indian origin rather than the Cayo Santiago population; because female recombination rates exceed male rates in most mammals, this male-only map systematically understates genome-wide recombination and thus yields a conservative upper bound on the linked reduction. All other maps are sex-averaged (though sex-averaging could not be independently confirmed for the baboon map). We integrated each linkage map with its corresponding genome assembly chromosome by chromosome, matching linkage groups to annotated chromosomes by name; for microchromosomes and other ambiguous groups, we matched them based on concordance between physical length and genetic map length. For the song sparrow, whose assembly numbers chromosomes strictly by physical size, each chromosome was assigned to the great tit chromosome~\cite{laine2016} sharing the plurality of its annotated protein-coding genes (shared fraction $0.59$ to $1.0$), and through it to the corresponding song sparrow linkage group. Map lengths $M_i$ are expressed in Morgans. Because the published blue tit map~\cite{hansson2010} is highly fragmentary, and the hihi~\cite{tan2024} and superb fairy-wren~\cite{penalba2020} lack chromosome-level assemblies, these three species could not be mapped, leaving 9 populations in our mapped set, with the remaining 10 analyzed exclusively at unlinked sites. Every autosome with a genetic map length of at least $1$~cM, including microchromosomes, contributed to the linked term.

We partitioned additive genetic variance among autosomes in proportion to coding length, setting $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$ with $f_i = L_{\text{coding},i}/\sum_i L_{\text{coding},i}$, and report the alternative partition based on annotated gene counts as a sensitivity analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:S_fgene}). For each chromosome, we calculated $\overline{Q^2}_i$, the squared transmission factor averaged over every position a neutral site can occupy along the chromosome's genetic map. Under Haldane's map function, this average has an exact formula (Appendix~C, Equation~\eqref{eq:C_closed_haldane}), so no numerical approximation was required. As a check, the formula agreed with direct numerical integration of Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} to within one part in $10^{11}$ for every map length and selection coefficient we examined. \claude{We did not use the linear-map midpoint approximation}~\cite{buffaloKern2024}\claude{, which is designed for short, densely mapped segments and departs from the chromosome average on whole chromosomes (Appendix}~\ref{app:C_linear}\claude{, Figure}~\ref{fig:S_maps}\claude{).} Each effect class was retained at $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), and local and global factors were integrated over the classes of the assigned DFE with weights $\nu_k$ (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}). To decouple the topological effect of the linkage maps from the biological assumptions of the DFE, we also evaluated a uniform selection coefficient ($s = 0.01$; Figure~\ref{fig:S_omega}b,c) and the population-specific selection coefficient $s = s^*$ at which MSDB maintains $\hat{V}_A$. Chromosome-level reductions followed Equation~\eqref{total_red} using the same retention factor $Z$ in both local and global terms, with genome-wide averages weighted by physical chromosome length $L_i$. Whenever we evaluated the linked reduction against an observed neutral deficit, we used the conservative bound pairing in which the global factor is evaluated at $Q = 2$ under the multiplicative bound (Figures~\ref{fig:linked}, \ref{fig:ceiling}, \ref{fig:S_rescaling_linked}, and \ref{fig:S_noncoding_linked}); corresponding shortfalls under the small-variance global approximation are reported in Results.

\subsection{Nucleotide diversity, census size, and the diversity test}\label{sec:pi_methods}
For 7 populations, we used the species-mean nucleotide diversity $\pi$ compiled by Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026}, which averages published estimates across fourfold-degenerate, synonymous, or genome-wide sites~\cite{leffler2012,corbettdetig2015,xue2016,dutoit2017,chen2017,bergeron2023}. None of these estimates derives directly from a focal study population; 5 are for the exact study species, whereas the estimates for red deer and yellow baboons are based on \emph{Cervus elaphus yarkandensis} and \emph{Papio anubis}, respectively. For 4 additional populations, we used published whole-genome estimates from alternate populations of the same species: for the two great tit populations, diversity across all sites in 10 European males (potentially including the Z chromosome)~\cite{barton2019}; for bighorn sheep, 6 Rocky Mountain genomes mapped to the domestic sheep reference~\cite{wang2023}; and for song sparrows, the coastal subspecies \emph{M.\ m.\ rufina}~\cite{carbeck2023}. Because the bighorn and song sparrow values were derived from minor allele frequency (MAF)-filtered SNP panels, they are likely biased downward. Furthermore, because none of these values are restricted strictly to neutral autosomal sites\claude{, and even fourfold and synonymous sites experience linked selection,} $\pi$ serves here as an approximation of neutral diversity (single-genome heterozygosity was recorded but excluded from $\pi$). \claude{Lewin and Eyre-Walker}~\cite{lewin2026} \claude{treat $\pi/(4\mu)$ as the long-term, or coalescent, effective size, an estimate of half the mean time to coalescence of two sequences, in generations, that includes every past change in population size and breeding system. We did not use trajectories of effective size inferred from genome sequences by sequentially Markovian coalescent or site-frequency-spectrum methods. The diversity test concerns the harmonic mean of $N_e$ over the genealogy (Equation}~\eqref{eq:harmonic}\claude{), which $\pi/(4\mu)$ estimates directly. Moreover, every genomic estimate of $N_e$ already includes the drift caused by heritable variance in fitness, so none supplies the long-term number of breeding adults against which the test is posed.} 

Total census sizes $N_c$ were extracted from the dataset of Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} for the 10 study species (yielding $N_c$ values for 9 populations once the domestic sheep value for the St.~Kilda Soay sheep was excluded). These represent density $\times$ geographic range estimates\claude{, a contemporary quantity that counts all adults and, in some density surveys, juveniles}~\cite{lewin2026}\claude{. Because} Buffalo calculated bird population densities from body masses predicted via body length, recomputing bird densities from measured body masses~\cite{myhrvold2015} using Damuth's avian allometry~\cite{damuth1987} upwardly revises blue tit and collared flycatcher densities by approximately $1.2$ orders of magnitude, indicating that Buffalo's avian census sizes are likely substantial underestimates. Moreover, because no population possesses a direct empirical count of breeding adults ($N$), substituting total census size $N_c$ in place of $N$ lowers the \claude{estimated} diversity ratio \claude{below $\delta$} by the breeder fraction (a factor of $2$ to $10$), artificially widening the apparent gap between predicted and observed diversity. \claude{We therefore estimate the diversity ratio as $\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$, in which a contemporary total census stands in for the harmonic mean number of breeding adults over the genealogy to which $\delta$ refers (}\autoref{sec:quantity}\claude{).} \claude{The species-level values describe the two Corsican blue tit populations (Pirio and Muro) less closely than the mainland population at Rouvière. Corsican blue tits have been isolated from the mainland for about 4,500 generations and have a long-term effective size about 7.5 times smaller and 13\% lower heterozygosity at polymorphic sites}~\cite{perrier2020}\claude{. Because two Corsican lineages have only about a 4\% chance of coalescing on the island within that time, most of their genealogy predates the isolation and is described by the species-level values, but neither value describes the island population since then.} Table~\ref{tab:S_sources} records the source of each parameter, and Table~\ref{tab:bias} summarizes the directional impact of each source of error on the apparent diversity gap.

Nine populations across 6 species possess empirical estimates for both $\pi$ and $N_c$, forming what we term the \emph{deficit set} (6 of which also belong to the mapped set of \autoref{sec:linked_methods}). For these 9 populations, we tested predicted against observed diversity in units of $\pi$ by comparing $\pi_\text{pred} = 4\mu N_c\,(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ against $\pi_\text{obs}$. We evaluated $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ under both the small-variance approximation ($1/(1 + 4\kappa V_A)$) and the multiplicative bound ($e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$). We report \claude{$\hat{\delta}$} strictly as the axis scaling for the $V_{A,\delta}$ curves. \claude{A population with $\hat{\delta} \ge 1$ would be a failed model check; none of our 9 populations reached this threshold.} Finally, we evaluated the minimum long-term variance required to account for each observed deficit, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}), and computed its linked counterpart by numerically solving for the root of Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} set equal to \claude{$\hat{\delta}$} using the \texttt{uniroot} function in R across $\log_{10} V_A$.

\subsection{Rescaling the long-term variance}\label{sec:rescaling_methods}
To evaluate whether any single long-term variance can satisfy both equilibrium tests simultaneously, we expressed the required variance as $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}) and examined two diagnostic ratios as functions of the scaling factor $c$ across each of the 9 populations in the deficit set. The first ratio, $\bar{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$, tests the maintenance requirement under the population's assigned DFE (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}), where a value of at most $1$ indicates that mutation can maintain the variance. The second ratio, \claude{$(N_e/N)_\text{pred}/\hat{\delta}$}, compares the predicted retained diversity against the observed diversity ratio. This second ratio was evaluated under the multiplicative bound, $(N_e/N)_\text{pred} = e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}\bar{V}_A}$---a formulation that conservatively biases the diversity test toward passing across all magnitudes of $\bar{V}_A$---while the small-variance approximation ($1/(1 + 4\kappa\bar{V}_A)$) is shown alongside it when $\bar{V}_A$ remains small (taken as $\bar{V}_A < 1$). A test is satisfied whenever its respective ratio is $\le 1$. Because certain species contribute multiple monitored populations sharing identical empirical values of $\pi$ and $N_c$, we first averaged the log diagnostic ratios within each species and then report the cross-species mean accompanied by a $t$-based 95\% confidence interval (Figure~\ref{fig:rescaling}), ensuring that the interval reflects true macro-evolutionary variation among species rather than within-population estimation noise. When physical linkage is included, the predicted diversity ratio is multiplied by the genome-wide linked multiplier $\overline{\Omega}(\bar{V}_A)$ evaluated at the assigned DFE; we report this linked prediction at $c = U_d\bar{s}/\hat{V}_A$ (where the variance equals the exact amount sustainable by that DFE) to yield the fully self-consistent prediction of MSDB combined with background selection.

\subsection{Uncertainty carried by each test}\label{sec:uncertainty_methods}
Posterior uncertainty in $V_A$ was fully propagated through every analytical step, yielding 95\% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals for $N_e/N$, $s^*$, and all linked factors derived from the 4,000 posterior draws of each animal model fit. For the maintenance test, we additionally propagated uncertainty in the deleterious mutation rate \claude{(}\autoref{sec:meth_maintenance}\claude{)}; for the diversity ratio, we incorporated mutation-rate uncertainty via the 95\% confidence interval of $\mu$ (Figures~\ref{fig:linked} and~\ref{fig:ceiling}). All other input parameters were treated as fixed point values without joint resampling: total census sizes $N_c$ (noting our separate evaluation of avian density biases; \autoref{sec:pi_methods}); nucleotide diversity $\pi$ (including the 4 proxy values derived from alternate populations); DFE parameters (beyond the inherent mixture spread between reference species); extra-pair paternity rates $\alpha$; and genetic linkage maps $M_i$. Consequently, statements that an estimated parameter lies above or below a critical threshold across its HPD interval reflect exclusively the posterior uncertainty of $V_A$, and we evaluate census-size errors separately where they could potentially change the biological interpretation.

\subsection{Independence of the inputs from diversity data}\label{sec:independence_methods}
None of the core parameters of our two equilibrium tests are estimated or fitted from the neutral nucleotide diversity ($\pi_\text{obs}$) against which the diversity predictions are evaluated; $\pi$ is invoked downstream strictly to verify the strong-selection assumption via the check $2N_es^*$. Specifically, additive fitness variance $V_A$ derives entirely from pedigree breeding records~\cite{bonnet2022}; extra-pair paternity rates $\alpha$ from molecular parentage analyses; map lengths $M_i$ and coding fractions $f_i$ from linkage maps and genome annotations; per-site mutation rates $\mu$ from direct pedigree sequencing or calibrated phylogenetic models; and census sizes $N_c$ from density-by-range censuses. Consequently, our diversity test constitutes a rigorous out-of-sample prediction rather than a curve fit to $\pi_\text{obs}$. The sole shared empirical source across parameters is Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026}, which compiles both direct mutation rates $\mu_\text{direct}$ and nucleotide diversity proxies $\pi$; however, these two quantities are measured by independent methods ($\mu_\text{direct}$ by counting de novo mutations in sequenced \claude{parent--offspring} trios, $\pi$ from pairwise differences at putatively neutral sites among unrelated individuals in population resequencing data).
