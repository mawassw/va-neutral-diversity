\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Pedigree estimates of additive genetic variance in fitness}\label{sec:meth_va}
Posterior distributions of the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, were obtained from Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} for 19 longitudinally monitored wild vertebrate populations: 11 socially monogamous populations (10 bird populations and the meerkat) and 8 polygynous or polygynandrous mammal populations. Bonnet et al. fitted a zero-inflated overdispersed Poisson animal model~\cite{henderson1975,kruuk2004,wilson2010} to lifetime breeding success, including both sexes in each dataset with sex modeled as a fixed effect. We applied no standardization to these estimates because they were already back-transformed to the observed scale of relative fitness via the multivariate delta method, $V_A = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive genetic covariance matrix of the latent predictors and $\mathbf{d}$ is the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to them. For each population, we used the 4,000 posterior draws of $\hat{V}_A$ from these model fits to compute the posterior distribution of each derived quantity, summarizing results by the posterior mode and the 95\% highest posterior density (HPD) interval. Across the 19 populations, the posterior modes of $\hat{V}_A$ range from 0.003 to 0.50 (median 0.10).

Individuals with zero lifetime breeding success make up 53\% (yellow baboons, Amboseli) to 96\% (blue tits, Muro) of the records of Bonnet et al. (their Data~S1, excluding missing lifetime breeding success), so $\hat{V}_A$ includes heritable variance in pre-breeding survival as well as fecundity (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). Offspring were counted when first marked, from shortly after birth in mammals to ringing or fledging in birds~\cite{bonnet2022}, so each individual's survival to breeding age counts toward its own lifetime breeding success rather than its parent's. Robertson's $Q$ sums an inherited advantage over the generations in which it acts. An advantage in fecundity acts first in the ancestor's own reproduction ($t = 0$) and then, halved each generation, in that of its descendants, so $Q = 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots = 2$. An advantage in survival acts one stage earlier, before reproduction, and the ancestors whose contributions set drift are breeding adults that have already survived. A survival advantage therefore changes nothing at $t = 0$ and first acts on whether the ancestor's offspring, which inherit half of it, survive to breed. The sum starts one generation late, $Q = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots = 1$, so, per unit of $V_A$, survival to breeding age adds a quarter as much as fecundity to the variance of long-term contributions ($Q^2 = 1$ rather than 4)~\cite{robertson1961,santiago1995}. Because the data do not separate these two components of fitness, we applied $Q = 2$ to all of $\hat{V}_A$, which overstates the predicted reduction in $N_e$ unless pre-breeding survival and adult fecundity are negatively genetically correlated (\autoref{sec:approx}).

\subsection{Mating system and the reduction at unlinked sites}\label{sec:meth_mating}
We classified populations as socially monogamous when offspring are reared by a social pair (or by a dominant breeding pair in the cooperatively breeding superb fairy-wrens and meerkats) and as randomly mating otherwise. For each socially monogamous population, we obtained the extra-pair paternity rate $\alpha$, the proportion of offspring not sired by the social male, from published molecular parentage studies of the same population (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). For superb fairy-wrens, offspring sired by within-group subordinate helpers were counted alongside extra-group paternity as not sired by the dominant male; for meerkats, $\alpha$ was the proportion of the dominant female's pups not sired by the dominant male. For the Karori hihi and the Hoge Veluwe great tits, which lack parentage estimates, we used the estimates from the Tiritiri Matangi source population and from the neighboring Westerheide population, respectively (Westerheide $\alpha = 0.177$; earlier estimates $0.065$--$0.125$~\cite{vanoers2008}). Across the 11 socially monogamous populations, $\alpha$ ranges from $0.097$ (meerkats) to $0.61$ (superb fairy-wrens).

We set $\kappa = 1$ under random mating and evaluated $\kappa(\alpha)$ under social monogamy with Equation~\eqref{eq:kappaB} (Model~B of Appendix~\ref{app:C_kappa}), giving values from $1.32$ to $1.86$. We computed the reduction in $N_e$ at unlinked sites (\autoref{sec:unlinked}) at $Z = 1$ and with no correlation between mates' breeding values for fitness, because no estimate of that correlation exists. For each population, we evaluated the multiplicative model from the moment equation for long-term contributions (Equation~\eqref{eq:C_moments}, Appendix~\ref{app:C_mult}); the multiplicative bound using $\kappa_\text{max}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:C_mult_pair}); and the small-variance approximation, the limit of both for small $V_A$ (Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating}).

\subsection{Mutational target and deleterious mutation rate}\label{sec:meth_ud}
We downloaded genome assemblies and annotations from NCBI Datasets (accessions in Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). Sixteen populations have an annotated chromosome-level assembly, representing their own species in all cases except the yellow baboons and Soay sheep, for which we used the olive baboon (\emph{Papio anubis}) and domestic sheep (\emph{Ovis aries}, Rambouillet) reference assemblies, respectively. The spotted hyena and hihi assemblies are annotated only at the scaffold level, with the hihi annotation taken from the B10K consortium~\cite{feng2020}. Because the sole available superb fairy-wren assembly~\cite{penalba2020} is chromosome-level but unannotated, we applied the maintenance test to the remaining 18 populations. For each assembled autosome $i$, we recorded the physical length $L_i$, the number of annotated protein-coding genes, and the coding sequence length $L_{\text{coding},i}$, the union of all annotated CDS features, so that isoforms and overlapping reading frames were counted once. Sex chromosomes, mitochondrial sequences, and unplaced scaffolds were excluded. For the scaffold-level spotted hyena and hihi assemblies, we scaled the total genome-wide CDS length by the autosomal CDS fraction of their nearest chromosome-level relatives: $0.965$ for the hyena (from the meerkat) and $0.948$ for the hihi (the mean across the collared flycatcher, great tit, song sparrow, and blue tit assemblies).

We used two estimates of the per-site mutation rate $\mu$. The first, $\mu_\text{direct}$, is a pedigree or parent--offspring trio estimate for the focal species or its closest available relative, as compiled by Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026}. Species-specific direct estimates were available for the blue tit, collared flycatcher, and rhesus macaque~\cite{smeds2016,wang2020,bergeron2021,bergeron2023}; the remaining populations were assigned the direct estimate of their closest relative (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}, including feliform carnivore estimates from Armstrong et al.~\cite{armstrong2024}). The second estimate, $\mu_\text{phylo}$, is the phylogenetic mixed-model prediction of Wang and Obbard~\cite{wangobbard2023}, matched to the same species as $\mu_\text{direct}$ (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}) except for the meerkat, for which we used the domestic cat (\emph{Felis catus}) prediction. Because the phylogenetic model is trained on the direct estimates, the two rates are not independent, so we did not combine them; we report results under $\mu_\text{direct}$ and repeated every analysis under $\mu_\text{phylo}$, which is $7\%$ to $60\%$ higher.

The primary diploid autosomal deleterious mutation rate was $U_d = 4\mu L_\text{coding}$, with $L_\text{coding} = \sum_i L_{\text{coding},i}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}), which ranges from $0.34$ to $1.26$ under $\mu_\text{direct}$. The factor of $4$ counts $2\mu$ mutations per base pair of the diploid genome and a regulatory target equal in length to the exome. Treating every mutation in this target as deleterious, synonymous sites included, biases the maintenance test toward passing (\autoref{sec:approx}). Each chromosome's share of the target in the linked model (\autoref{sec:linkage}) is $f_i = L_{\text{coding},i}/L_\text{coding}$. For a larger target, we took for each autosome the union of coding sequences, untranslated regions (UTRs) of protein-coding transcripts, and exons of non-coding RNA genes ($L_{\text{annot},i}$), plus a fraction $\zeta$ of the remaining autosomal sequence ($L_{\text{other},i} = L_i - L_{\text{annot},i}$), and evaluated $U_d(\zeta) = 2\mu\sum_i(L_{\text{annot},i} + \zeta L_{\text{other},i})$ for $\zeta$ from $0\%$ to $15\%$, updating $f_i$ accordingly. Because the B10K hihi annotation includes only CDS features, we imputed its UTR and non-coding RNA fractions from the mean of the four chromosome-level passerine assemblies.

\subsection{Assigning a distribution of fitness effects}\label{sec:dfe_methods}
We assigned each population an empirical distribution of fitness effects (DFE) from the gamma DFEs of new nonsynonymous mutations that Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026} estimated across species with a uniform pipeline. Lin et al. converted population-scaled selection coefficients to $s$ with each species' ancestral effective size and assumed additive effects, so $\bar{s}$ is the mean heterozygous selection coefficient. Because they detected strong phylogenetic signal in the mean deleterious effect across vertebrates (Pagel's $\lambda = 0.84$ for $\log_{10}\bar{s}$) and greater DFE similarity within than between vertebrate classes, we assigned DFEs by phylogenetic proximity (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). Where a species-specific estimate was available (collared flycatcher), we assigned its own DFE; where two reference species were equally related to the focal taxon, we used an equal mixture of their DFEs. The remaining passerines received an equal mixture of the pied and collared flycatcher (\emph{Ficedula} genus) DFEs; the ruminants (bighorn sheep, Soay sheep, and red deer) received an equal mixture of the vaquita and fin whale DFEs (the closest available relatives within Cetartiodactyla); the primates (rhesus macaques and yellow baboons) received the human DFE; and the rodents received the house mouse DFE. Because the carnivore (wolf) DFE fits in Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026} had unconstrained heavy tails, we assigned the spotted hyena and meerkat the cetacean mixture as the closest well-constrained reference within Laurasiatheria. The resulting mean heterozygous effects range from $\bar{s} = 0.0013$ to $0.0195$. Because the mean of a gamma DFE fitted to site-frequency spectra depends on its poorly constrained tail of large effects, and the assigned means came from species as distant as the flycatchers for every passerine and the cetaceans for the ruminants and carnivores, we also varied the mean together with the target and the estimate in our joint analysis (\autoref{sec:meth_joint}).

Phylogenetic signal within mammals alone was weaker (Pagel's $\lambda = 0.45$, non-significant), and the human DFE of Lin et al.~\cite{lin2026} has a higher mean than earlier human estimates~\cite{kim2017}. However, a smaller $\bar{s}$ makes the maintenance test harder to pass, by lowering $U_d\bar{s}$, and the diversity test easier, by slowing purging ($Z_k = 1 - s_k$) and strengthening linked selection, so no choice of DFE favors both tests. We applied the same assigned DFE to the maintenance test, the linked reduction, and the threshold analysis. For a gamma DFE, the variance weights $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:linkage}) follow a gamma density with the same scale and a shape larger by one, which we discretized into 240 logarithmically spaced classes over $s \in [10^{-8}, 1]$ (relative error in $\overline{\Omega}$ below $2\times10^{-3}$ against 960 classes).

\subsection{Maintenance test}\label{sec:meth_maintenance}
For every posterior draw of $V_A$, we computed $s^* = V_A/U_d$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}), pairing each draw with a draw of $U_d$ from the log-normal distribution implied by the 95\% confidence interval of the mutation rate (direct or phylogenetic), and tested each draw of $s^*$ against the mean $\bar{s}$ of the assigned DFE (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}). We report the posterior mode and 95\% HPD interval of $s^*$ and the posterior probabilities $P(s^* > \bar{s})$ and $P(s^* > 1)$, the second of which is the probability that the variance exceeds the deleterious mutation rate itself. To check the strong-selection assumption of MSDB ($N_es \gg 1$), we evaluated $2N_es^*$ in populations with diversity data, at $N_e = \pi/(4\mu)$ and $s^*$ at its posterior mode, and checked whether $N_e$ exceeds the effective size $U_d/(2V_A)$ below which selection is weak relative to drift (Appendix~\ref{app:A_inversion}).

\subsection{Joint sensitivity of the maintenance test}\label{sec:meth_joint}
We varied the deleterious target, the mean deleterious effect, and the pedigree estimate together for the 18 populations with an annotated assembly. The target was either the primary target or the annotated functional sequence plus a fraction $\zeta$ = 0, 3, 8, or 15\% of the unannotated sequence. To represent large-effect mutations that site-frequency spectra do not detect, we gave 0, 0.5, 1, 2, or 5\% of new mutations a heterozygous effect of 0.5 and the remainder the assigned DFE, which raised the mean effect by 0.0025 to 0.025; applying this tail to the whole target, coding or not, biases the test toward passing. To represent inflation of the pedigree estimate, for instance by shared environments, we divided it by 1, 2, 3, 5, or 10. In each of the 125 combinations, we counted the populations in which the estimate, at its posterior mode and the point value of the direct mutation rate, still exceeded the variance that mutation maintains, and computed the posterior probability of that excess as in the maintenance test.

\subsection{Linkage maps and effective size at linked sites}\label{sec:linked_methods}
Genetic map lengths were compiled from published pedigree linkage maps (Table~\ref{tab:S_sources}). For yellow baboons, we used the map of a captive \emph{Papio hamadryas} colony~\cite{rogers2000}, substituting chromosomes 5, 12, and 18 from its second-generation revision~\cite{cox2006}. The rhesus macaque map~\cite{versoza2024} is male-only and from a captive colony of Indian origin rather than the Cayo Santiago population; because female recombination rates exceed male rates in most mammals, it overstates the linked reduction, which biases the diversity test toward passing. All other maps are sex-averaged (unconfirmed for the baboon map). We matched linkage groups to assembly chromosomes by name, and microchromosomes and other ambiguous groups by concordance between physical and genetic map length. For the song sparrow, whose assembly numbers chromosomes by physical size, each chromosome was assigned to the great tit chromosome~\cite{laine2016} sharing the plurality of its annotated protein-coding genes (shared fraction $0.59$ to $1.0$), and through it to the corresponding song sparrow linkage group. Map lengths $M_i$ are in Morgans. Because the published blue tit map~\cite{hansson2010} is fragmentary, the hihi assembly~\cite{tan2024} is not chromosome-level, and the superb fairy-wren assembly~\cite{penalba2020} is unannotated, the mapped set comprises 9 populations, and the remaining 10 were analyzed only at unlinked sites. Every autosome with a map length of at least $1$~cM, including microchromosomes, contributed to the linked term.

We partitioned $\hat{V}_A$ among autosomes in proportion to coding length, $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, and report the partition by annotated gene counts as a sensitivity analysis. We computed $\overline{Q^2}_i$ from its closed form under Haldane's map function (Equation~\eqref{eq:D_closed_haldane}), which agreed with numerical integration of Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} to within one part in $10^{11}$ for every map length and selection coefficient examined. The association with each effect class was retained at $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), and the linked and unlinked terms were integrated over the classes of the assigned DFE with weights $\nu_k$ (\autoref{sec:dfe_methods}), using the same retention factor in both terms of Equation~\eqref{total_red}. To separate the effect of the linkage maps from that of the DFE, we also evaluated a uniform $s = 0.01$ and the population-specific $s = s^*$ at which MSDB maintains $\hat{V}_A$. When testing against an observed deficit, we evaluated the unlinked factor at $Q = 2$ under the multiplicative bound, a combination biased toward passing, and also under the small-variance approximation.

\subsection{Nucleotide diversity, census size, and the diversity test}\label{sec:pi_methods}
For 7 populations, we used the species-mean nucleotide diversity $\pi$ compiled by Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026}, which averages published estimates across fourfold-degenerate, synonymous, or genome-wide sites~\cite{leffler2012,corbettdetig2015,xue2016,dutoit2017,chen2017,bergeron2023}. None of these estimates comes from a focal study population; 5 are for the study species itself, whereas the estimates for red deer and yellow baboons are based on \emph{Cervus elaphus yarkandensis} and \emph{Papio anubis}, respectively. For 4 additional populations, we used published whole-genome estimates from other populations of the same species: for the two great tit populations, diversity across all sites in 10 European males (potentially including the Z chromosome)~\cite{barton2019}; for bighorn sheep, 6 Rocky Mountain genomes mapped to the domestic sheep reference~\cite{wang2023}; and for song sparrows, the coastal subspecies \emph{M.\ m.\ rufina}~\cite{carbeck2023}. The bighorn and song sparrow values come from SNP panels filtered on minor allele frequency and are therefore probably biased downward. Because none of these values is restricted to neutral autosomal sites, and fourfold and synonymous sites are themselves affected by linked selection, $\pi$ only approximates neutral diversity. We excluded estimates of heterozygosity from a single genome. We treated $\pi/(4\mu)$ as the coalescent effective size~\cite{lewin2026}, which estimates the harmonic mean of $N_e$ over the genealogy that the diversity test concerns (Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}). Every genomic estimate of $N_e$, including trajectories inferred by sequentially Markovian coalescent and site-frequency-spectrum methods, already includes the drift caused by heritable variance in fitness, so none supplies the long-term number of breeding adults that the test requires.

Total census sizes $N_c$ came from Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} for 10 of the 19 populations (9 after excluding the domestic sheep value for the St.~Kilda Soay sheep). These are density $\times$ geographic range estimates, a contemporary quantity that counts all adults and, in some density surveys, juveniles~\cite{lewin2026}. Because Buffalo calculated bird densities from body masses predicted from body length, the resulting bird census sizes are probably too low; recomputing densities from measured body masses~\cite{myhrvold2015} with Damuth's avian allometry~\cite{damuth1987} raises blue tit and collared flycatcher densities by about $1.2$ orders of magnitude. Because no population has a count of breeding adults, $\hat{\delta}$ (\autoref{sec:conditions}) uses $N_c$ in place of $N$, which lowers it below $\delta$ by the breeder fraction (a factor of $2$ to $10$) and widens the apparent gap between predicted and observed diversity. The species-level values describe the two Corsican blue tit populations (Pirio and Muro) less closely than the mainland population at Rouvière. Corsican blue tits have been isolated from the mainland for about 4,500 generations, with a long-term effective size about 7.5 times smaller and 13\% lower heterozygosity at polymorphic sites~\cite{perrier2020}, but because two Corsican lineages have only about a 4\% chance of coalescing on the island within that time, most of their genealogy predates the isolation and is described by the species-level values. Table~\ref{tab:S_sources} records the source of each parameter, and Table~\ref{tab:bias} summarizes the direction in which each source of error shifts the apparent diversity gap.

Nine populations from 6 species have estimates of both $\pi$ and $N_c$. We call them the \emph{deficit set}; 6 of them also belong to the mapped set of \autoref{sec:linked_methods}. For these 9 populations, we tested $\pi_\text{pred} = 4\mu N_c\,(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ against $\pi_\text{obs}$, with $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$ under both the small-variance approximation and the multiplicative bound. A population with $\hat{\delta} \ge 1$ would be a failed model check; none reached it. We evaluated the minimum long-term variance required to account for each observed deficit, $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}), and its linked counterpart as the root of Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} set equal to $\hat{\delta}$, found with \texttt{uniroot} in R on $\log_{10} V_A$.

\subsection{Threshold analysis and compatibility test}\label{sec:rescaling_methods}
We expressed the long-term variance as $\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}) and followed two ratios as functions of $c$ in each of the 9 populations in the deficit set: the maintenance ratio $\bar{V}_A/(U_d\bar{s})$ at the assigned DFE, and the ratio of predicted to observed diversity, $(N_e/N)_\text{pred}/\hat{\delta}$. Each test passes where its ratio is $\le 1$. The ratio of predicted to observed diversity was evaluated under the multiplicative bound, which is biased toward passing at every $\bar{V}_A$, with the small-variance approximation shown alongside it for $\bar{V}_A < 1$. The values of $c$ at which the two ratios reach 1 are the thresholds $c_\text{MSD}$ and $c_\text{div}$ (\autoref{sec:conditions}), and a population passes the compatibility test where $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$. Because some species contribute several populations that share $\pi$ and $N_c$, we averaged the log ratios within each species and reported the cross-species mean with a $t$-based 95\% confidence interval. With linkage, the predicted $N_e/N$ was multiplied by $\overline{\Omega}(\bar{V}_A)$ at the assigned DFE, and we report the linked prediction at $c = U_d\bar{s}/\hat{V}_A$ as the prediction of MSDB with background selection.

\subsection{Uncertainty carried by each test}\label{sec:uncertainty_methods}
Posterior uncertainty in $V_A$ was propagated through every derived quantity ($N_e/N$, $s^*$, and all linked factors), and uncertainty in the mutation rate through $U_d$ in the maintenance test (\autoref{sec:meth_maintenance}) and through its 95\% confidence interval in the diversity ratio. All other inputs were fixed at point values: total census sizes $N_c$, nucleotide diversity $\pi$ (including the 4 proxy values from other populations), DFE parameters (beyond the spread of the mixture between reference species), extra-pair paternity rates $\alpha$, and linkage maps $M_i$. A quantity that lies above or below a reference value across its HPD interval therefore does so given the posterior uncertainty of $V_A$ alone, and we evaluated census-size errors separately where they could change a verdict.

\subsection{Independence of the inputs from diversity data}\label{sec:independence_methods}
None of the parameters of either test was estimated from the diversity $\pi_\text{obs}$ against which the diversity predictions are evaluated. Diversity is otherwise used only to check the strong-selection assumption through $2N_es^*$, so the diversity test is a prediction from independently measured inputs, not a fit. The one source shared across parameters, Lewin and Eyre-Walker~\cite{lewin2026}, compiles both $\mu_\text{direct}$ and $\pi$, which are measured by independent methods, de novo mutations in parent--offspring trios and pairwise differences among unrelated individuals.
