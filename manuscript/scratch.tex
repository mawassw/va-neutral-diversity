\section{Introduction}
Additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, is the immediate currency of adaptive change: under Fisher’s fundamental theorem, the partial change in mean fitness attributable strictly to natural selection equals $V_A$ per generation on the mean-standardized scale, saying nothing on subsequent generations (Fisher 1930; Price 1972). Its magnitude bounds both the contemporary rate of adaptation and the capacity for evolutionary rescue (Crow 1958; Lande 1982; Gomulkiewicz and Holt 1995), making its estimation in natural populations a long-standing objective.

In an idealized neutral Wright–Fisher population, family sizes follow a Poisson distribution and the variance effective size approximately equals the census adult size, $N_e \approx N$ (Wright 1938). Because high-fitness parents transmit the alleles responsible for their selective advantage, their genetic contributions compound across generations. This heritable variance overdisperses long-term family sizes, accelerates the drift of neutral alleles, and depresses $N_e$ below the number of breeding adults, $N$ (Robertson 1961; Buffalo and Coop 2020). For a neutral site assorting independently of every selected locus in an otherwise Poisson background, Santiago and Caballero (1995, 1998) derived the closed form:

\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A},
\end{equation}
where $Q = 2$ ($Q^2 = 4$) represents the cumulative transmission factor across generations, and $V_A$ corresponds to their mean-standardized additive variance, $C^2$. Expressed as the reciprocal, $N/N_e = 1 + 4 V_A$ scales linearly with $V_A$. Unlike linked background selection—which reduces diversity exponentially—the unlinked Robertson effect can impose only a modest reduction for realistic single-generation variances. Because pairwise nucleotide diversity reflects long-term coalescence ($\pi = 4 N_e \mu$), any sustained $V_A$ implies an upper bound on genome-wide neutral diversity, which in wild populations remains orders of magnitude below census expectations (Lewontin 1974; Leffler et al. 2012; Buffalo 2021). While this framework has been extended into a quantitative-genetic theory of linked background selection (Santiago and Caballero 2016; Buffalo and Kern 2024), the genome-wide unlinked Robertson reduction has largely been omitted from empirical accounting (Matheson and Masel 2025).

The pedigree-based animal model made $V_A$ estimable in unmanipulated wild populations by using deep monitoring records to separate additive genetic variance from environmental and maternal confounding (Henderson 1975; Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010). Applied to composite lifetime breeding success in long-term vertebrate systems—including St. Kilda Soay sheep and Wytham Woods great tits—it estimates additive variance in relative fitness directly (Postma 2014). Syntheses across these systems report substantial heritable variance across wild taxa, with estimates of order $V_A \sim 0.1$ (Bonnet et al. 2022).

Yet a sustained variance of that magnitude must also be mechanistically maintained. At mutation–selection–drift balance, standing additive variance in relative fitness is determined by the influx of new mutations and the distribution of fitness effects (DFE); when purifying selection dominates genetic drift ($2 N_e s_{\text{het}} \gg 1$), this expectation collapses to $V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}_{\text{het}}$ (Crow 1970; Barton 1990; Charlesworth 2015), where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}_{\text{het}}$ is the mean heterozygous selection coefficient. Both parameters are now independently measured in vertebrates through pedigree sequencing (Bergeron et al. 2023) and DFE inference (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017). There is, consequently, an established mutational expectation for standing fitness variance that is derived entirely independently of neutral diversity.

Two distinct theoretical frameworks thus constrain the same biological quantity—one through neutral coalescent diversity, the other through mutation–selection balance. To our knowledge, the large fitness variances inferred from pedigree animal models have not been evaluated against either constraint. This is a one-to-one comparison: animal-model estimates represent observed-scale, mean-standardized additive variance in relative fitness ($I_A$)—the exact currency of Santiago and Caballero's $C^2$ and mutation–selection balance models alike. However, while both theoretical frameworks govern the long-run equilibrium variance, animal models estimate a local, single-generation realization conditioned on contemporary environmental states. Evaluating measured variances against these models tests what contemporary estimates would imply were they sustained across evolutionary time.

Here, we formulate an integrated analytical framework that couples the Robertson baseline—corrected for extra-pair paternity and mating systems—with linked selection integrated across empirical recombination maps and functional genomic annotations. Inverting this model yields the maximum additive fitness variance compatible with observed levels of neutral diversity. Applying this dual constraint to published pedigree estimates across monitored wild vertebrates reveals a systematic discordance among contemporary quantitative-genetic estimates, neutral diversity, and the mutational capacity of vertebrate genomes.

\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The quantity and the two equilibria that constrain it}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$: the variance of individual additive genetic breeding values for lifetime reproductive contribution, standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). This corresponds to the mean-standardized additive variance $I_A$ (Crow 1958; Houle 1992) and matches Santiago and Caballero's (1995) $C^2$, providing a unified currency across all models evaluated below.

Both theoretical constraints require the long-run equilibrium value of this variance. Because pairwise nucleotide diversity ($\pi = 4 N_e \mu$) reflects neutral genealogies with an expected coalescent time of $2N_e$ generations (and a total pairwise branch length of $4N_e$ generations), standing diversity integrates the history of fitness variance over thousands of generations. Similarly, mutation–selection–drift balance defines the stationary distribution of standing variance under recurrent mutation and purging, rather than a transient, single-generation realization.

These dynamics operate across two distinct persistence windows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The transmission memory ($Q$):} For a neutral site assorting independently of all selected loci, the inherited parental breeding value halves each generation due to Mendelian segregation. The cumulative transmission factor,
    \begin{equation}
    Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2 \implies Q^2 = 4,
    \end{equation}
    is dominated by the earliest generational steps ($>85\%$ achieved within two generations). It is the special case $G = 1$, $r_{mf} = 0$ of Santiago and Caballero's (1995) $Q = 2/[2 - G(1+r_{mf})]$. $G$ is the fraction of additive variance surviving selection in each generation, and $r_{mf}$ is the correlation between mates' breeding values. Unless breeding values for fitness are strongly right-skewed, $G \le 1$, so $G = 1$ gives the largest $Q$ and therefore the largest reduction in $N_e$ (Appendix A). Consequently, establishing the unlinked Robertson baseline requires only that the direction of selective advantages remains consistent across roughly two consecutive generations—a condition satisfied under mutation–selection–drift balance, directional sweeps, and temporally fluctuating selection provided environmental fluctuations are positively autocorrelated across generations ($\tau_{\text{env}} \ge 2$).
    \item \textbf{The coalescent memory ($\pi$):} In contrast, the timescale required for neutral diversity to reach mutation–drift equilibrium following a shift in variance is categorically longer, requiring on the order of $2N_e$ to $4N_e$ generations.
\end{enumerate}

Connecting these frameworks requires equating two definitions of effective population size. Santiago and Caballero's derivation yields the asymptotic variance effective size, $N_{eV}$, which quantifies the per-generation rate of neutral drift resulting from the inherited compounding of family-size overdispersion. Nucleotide diversity measures the coalescent effective size, $N_{eC}$, which determines the timescale of the neutral genealogy. Under long-run demographic and selective equilibrium, the asymptotic rates of drift, inbreeding, and coalescence converge ($N_{eV} = N_{eC} = N_e$), providing the mathematical bridge that allows neutral diversity to constrain the standing additive variance in fitness. \subsection{The unlinked Robertson baseline}
In an idealized diploid Wright--Fisher population of constant census size $N$, family size follows a binomial distribution that converges to Poisson in large populations ($V_k \approx \bar{k} = 2$), yielding an effective size approximately equal to the census adult size, $N_e \approx N$ (Wright 1938; Crow and Kimura 1970). This neutral benchmark assumes that variance in reproductive success is entirely non-heritable: each parent's genetic contribution represents an independent draw, generating no persistent fitness differences among genealogical lineages.

The Robertson effect (Robertson 1961) formalizes the consequence of violating that assumption. When reproductive success has an additive genetic basis, high-fitness parents transmit their favorable alleles to their offspring, establishing positive multi-generational autocovariance in lineage reproductive success (Buffalo and Coop 2020). In a population of constant size, the sustained compounding of high-fitness lineages accelerates the extinction of low-fitness lineages. This inflates the long-term variance of ancestral genetic contributions far beyond the Poisson expectation, depressing the asymptotic variance effective size, $N_e$, below the number of breeding adults.

For a neutral allele assorting independently of all selected loci ($r = 1/2$), Appendix~A integrates these selective-variance contributions across generations alongside baseline neutral sampling drift. Substituting the cumulative transmission factor established above ($Q^2 = 4$) yields the Santiago and Caballero (1995) unlinked baseline:
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4V_A}, \label{unlinked_baseline}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the mean-standardized additive genetic variance in relative fitness ($I_A \equiv C^2$), evaluated in an otherwise Poisson demographic background.

Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} carries an important scope condition. Because it depends strictly on the Mendelian dilution of transmitted breeding values under free assortment, it requires no assumptions regarding the genealogical persistence or haplotype structure of the selected background. It therefore applies across diverse selective regimes—including mutation-selection-drift balance, directional sweeps (with $V_A$ interpreted as the time-averaged variance, $\bar{V}_A$), and autocorrelated fluctuating selection—without requiring the stationarity parameters introduced below. Everything from \autoref{sec:linkage} onward requires explicit parameterization of haplotype longevity and recombination architecture; Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} serves as an invariant, parameter-sparse ceiling on the effective population size.

\subsection{Mating system structure and extra-pair paternity}
With separate sexes, half of every genome passes through each sex, and Santiago and Caballero (1995) combine the two through $1/N_e = 1/(4N_{e,m}) + 1/(4N_{e,f})$ (after Crow and Denniston 1988). With an equal sex ratio this averages the selective variance over the sexes; it does not add it. Mating systems differ in what sets a parent's family size. Without a pair bond, each parent's own breeding value does. Under social monogamy the unit of productivity is the nest, so a parent's family size also carries its social partner's breeding value. Extra-pair paternity (EPP) at rate $\alpha$ moves paternity away from the nest without changing how many offspring it produces.

Appendix~B applies the expected long-term contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva (1999) to these mating systems. The result is a mating-system scalar, $\kappa(\alpha)$, that scales the selective variance driving the Robertson effect:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}, \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, common to both sexes, as estimated by Bonnet et al. (2022). Without a pair bond (polygynous and polygynandrous systems) each parent's family size depends on its own breeding value alone, and separating the sexes leaves Appendix A unchanged: $\kappa = 1$. In socially monogamous systems both partners' breeding values set the productivity of a shared nest, and the functional form of $\kappa(\alpha)$ depends on how extra-pair siring relates to male breeding value:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model A (Non-selective EPP):} extra-pair siring is a lottery independent of male breeding value:
    \begin{equation}
    \kappa_A(\alpha) = \frac{(4-\alpha)^2\left[1+(1-\alpha)^2\right] + (4-3\alpha+\alpha^2)^2 + (4-3\alpha)^2}{32},
    \end{equation}
    falling from $\kappa_A(0) = 2$ to $\kappa_A(1) = 7/16$.
    \item \textbf{Model B (Selective EPP):} extra-pair gains are proportional to male breeding value and replace the within-pair loss, so a male's breeding value enters his own success with the same weight as a female's:
    \begin{equation}
    \kappa_B(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2,
    \end{equation}
    falling monotonically from $\kappa_B(0) = 2$ to $\kappa_B(1) = 9/8$.
\end{enumerate}

Under strict monogamy both models give $\kappa = 2$, which is the pair-mated base model of Santiago and Caballero (1995): each nest pools two independent breeding values, so the variance of family fitness is $2V_A$. A socially monogamous population therefore incurs a given depression in neutral diversity at half the fitness variance required without a pair bond. The eleven socially monogamous populations in our data have extra-pair rates of 5--76\%, which places them at $\kappa_B = 1.22\text{--}1.93$; the eight without a pair bond sit at $\kappa = 1$. Both values assume $G = 1$ and random pairing with respect to breeding value ($r_{mf} = 0$); Appendices A and B give the general forms.

\subsection{Linked interference along the genetic map}\label{sec:linkage}
For a neutral site physically linked to loci affecting fitness, statistical associations do not halve unconditionally each generation. Recombination only partially breaks gametic linkage disequilibrium, causing ancestral selective advantages to persist across multiple generations and amplifying selective interference far beyond the unlinked baseline. 

Generalizing this process along a chromosome requires compounding the per-generation covariance between a focal neutral site and its linked background, weighted by the joint probability that their physical linkage and the background's selective variance survive into the next generation. That joint persistence requires the survival parameter $Z = 1 - V_m/V_A$, where $V_m$ is the per-generation mutational influx of fitness variance and $V_A$ is standing additive fitness variance. Defining $Z$ presupposes a stationary selected background—an assumption unnecessary for the unlinked baseline, but met at mutation--selection--drift balance (Barton 1998, 2000). Under strong purifying selection ($2N_e s_\text{het} \gg 1$), mutational variance input ($V_m \approx U_d \mathbb{E}[s_\text{het}^2]$) balances standing variance ($V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}_\text{het}$), yielding $V_m/V_A \approx \mathbb{E}[s_\text{het}^2]/\mathbb{E}[s_\text{het}] = \bar{s}_\text{het}(1 + \mathrm{CV}_s^2)$ and $Z \approx 1 - \mathbb{E}[s_\text{het}^2]/\mathbb{E}[s_\text{het}]$. This reduces to $1 - s_\text{het}$ only when all effects are equal. For a leptokurtic DFE it is much smaller: $1 - 6\bar{s}_\text{het}$ for a gamma DFE of shape 0.2 (Appendix D). More generally, $Z$ can be parameterized directly from empirical estimates of $V_m$ and $V_A$, or adjusted for finite population size via the neutral drift loss rate ($1/(2N_e)$), as detailed in \autoref{msdb}.

Physical map distance $\ell$ (in Morgans) translates to recombination frequency via the Haldane mapping function, $r(\ell) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - e^{-2\ell})$. While a linear approximation ($r \approx \ell$) suffices across short genetic intervals, our integration spans complete chromosomes that frequently exceed $0.5\text{ M}$. Beyond this scale, a linear map artificially allows recombination frequencies to exceed $0.5$, which overestimates recombination and erroneously purges the persistent selective interference of distant loci ($Q^2 \to 0$). In contrast, Haldane's function correctly saturates at independent assortment ($r \to 1/2$).

The joint probability that a neutral--selected association survives one generation of recombination and selection is $(1 - r(\ell))Z = \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})$. Summing the geometric series of this survival probability across generations and integrating over selected loci uniformly distributed along a chromosome of map length $M$ yields the position-specific interference profile for a neutral site at position $y$:
\begin{equation}
Q^2(y) = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^M \left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2|x-y|})}\right)^2 dx.
\end{equation}
Averaging this profile over all neutral positions reduces the double integral to a single integral over pairwise genetic distances $\ell = |x - y|$ weighted by the triangular density $(M - \ell)$ (Appendix~C):
\begin{equation}
\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked} = \frac{2}{M^2}\int_0^M (M - \ell)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})}\right)^2 d\ell. \label{eq:haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
The kernel inside this integral is the standard one: with $Z = 1 - s$ it is the background-selection kernel $1/[s + r(1-s)]$ (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996; Santiago and Caballero 1998, 2016; Buffalo and Kern 2024). Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} has a closed form, with a dilogarithm (Appendix~C). The linear-map form given there is a lower bound.

Because a neutral allele resides physically on a single chromosome, it is linked in cis only to selected loci on its own haplotype; associations with the homologous chromosome segregate freely at meiosis and do not generate directional linkage disequilibrium across generations (Buffalo and Kern 2024). Across dense genetic maps, linked background selection operates as an inhomogeneous Poisson process along the chromosome (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Santiago and Caballero 2016). Integrating these infinitesimal linked contributions yields an exponential reduction in effective size for chromosome $i$, set by the focal haplotype fitness variance $v_h$:
\begin{equation}
\left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked}\,v_h\right). \label{eq:ne_chrom}
\end{equation}
The linked term does not carry the full mating-system scalar. In socially monogamous systems, $\kappa > 1$ comes from the social partner's breeding value, and the partner's genes are never in cis with the focal copy. Only the carrier's own breeding value is linked to it, and $\kappa_\text{cis}$ is the weight that value has in the carrier's family size (Appendix~B). That weight is 1 without a pair bond and under Model B, so $\kappa_\text{cis} = 1$ in all primary results. Under Model A it lies between $[(2-\alpha)/2]^2$ under tight linkage and $[1+(1-\alpha)^2]/2$ under loose linkage.
While Buffalo and Kern (2024) omitted homologous ($Q''$) associations entirely, we reassign that homologous variance to the unlinked background rather than discarding it—an accounting detailed alongside the parameterization of $v_h$ in \autoref{variance_partitioning}.

Table~1 gives the two limits, but neither is reached by real chromosomes. Complete linkage requires the whole map to lie within the kernel's decay distance, $M \ll 1 - Z$. Free recombination requires $M \gg 1/(1-Z)$ Morgans: at $Z = 0.99$ and $M = 10$, $\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked} = 24.8$ against the limit 3.9. Chromosomes in between sit near $\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked} \approx 2/[M(1-Z)]$ (Santiago and Caballero 1998; Appendix~C), so interference scales inversely with map length and with $1 - Z$. With one obligate crossover per bivalent, even avian microchromosomes have map lengths of order 0.5 M or more and do not approach complete linkage. Our analysis excludes chromosomes shorter than 20 Mb.

\subsection{Total reduction and genomic architecture}\label{variance_partitioning}
Under an infinitesimal genetic architecture, heritable fitness variance is distributed across the genome in proportion to functional annotation size. Chromosome $i$ carries total additive variance $v_i = V_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is its fraction of the genome's total functional sequence (coding sequence and conserved non-coding elements). Assuming additive gene action, the heritable variance residing on a single physical haplotype is $v_h = v_i / 2$. Because the homologous chromosome segregates freely at meiosis, its variance ($v_h$) assorts independently of the focal neutral site and enters the unlinked background alongside the variance distributed across all other chromosomes ($V_A - v_i$). The freely assorting variance acting on a neutral site on chromosome $i$ is therefore $(V_A - v_i) + v_h = V_A - v_h$.

A focal neutral allele on chromosome $i$ consequently experiences two concurrent selective interference regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linked background selection in cis:} Selected loci on the same physical haplotype maintain gametic linkage disequilibrium that decays under recombination along the chromosome. Compounding this interference across generations yields the exponential background selection reduction, $\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right)$ (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Santiago and Caballero 2016).
    \item \textbf{Unlinked Robertson overdispersion in trans:} All remaining selected loci in the genome—the homologous haplotype and all other autosomes—segregate independently of the focal allele ($r = 1/2$), driving family-size overdispersion through the Robertson geometric series evaluated at the remaining variance, $V_A - v_h$. Under social monogamy the trans background also includes the social partner's entire breeding value. Appendix~B splits $\kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}$ accordingly. Only the own part loses $v_h$ to the cis term, and $\kappa_\text{own} = 1$ without a pair bond and under Model B.
\end{enumerate}
Because cross-chromosomal linkage disequilibrium is negligible under random mating and independent assortment, these two selective regimes act across independent coalescent branch segments. Their joint effect combines multiplicatively to determine the chromosome-specific relative effective size:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \frac{\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right)}{1 + Q_u^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h)}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
which under Model B, and without a pair bond, is $\exp(-\overline{Q^2}_i v_h)/[1 + Q_u^2(\kappa V_A - v_h)]$. Here $Q_u = 2/(2-Z)$ is the unlinked cumulative factor with the same per-generation persistence $Z$ as the linked term: it is the $M \to \infty$ limit of $\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked}$ (Appendix~C), and it returns Appendix A's $Q = 2$ at $Z = 1$. Using one persistence for both terms is what makes $\Omega_i \le 1$ hold exactly (below).

To isolate the specific reduction imposed by chromosome structure beyond the unlinked baseline, we define the relative linkage penalty, $\Omega_i$, as the ratio of the chromosome-level effective size to the genome-wide unlinked baseline (Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating}):
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right) \cdot \frac{1 + Q_u^2\kappa V_A}{1 + Q_u^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h)}. \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
The second factor in Equation~\eqref{linked_penalty} strictly exceeds unity: sequestering variance $v_h$ onto a physical chromosome slightly relieves the global family-size overdispersion imposed by the unlinked background. The parameter $\Omega_i$ thus represents the net balance between this modest global variance relief and the severe local coalescent compression driven by physical linkage (Charlesworth et al. 1993).

Assuming neutral sites are uniformly distributed across the physical genome, the genome-wide expected relative effective size is the physical-length-weighted average across all $n$ autosomes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \cdot \frac{\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right)}{1 + Q_u^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h)},
\end{equation}
where $L_i$ is the physical length (in base pairs) of autosome $i$, and $L_\text{total} = \sum L_i$. The genome-wide net linkage effect, $\overline{\Omega}$, is the corresponding length-weighted mean:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \Omega_i.
\end{equation}
A value of $\overline{\Omega} = 1$ indicates that genomic architecture imposes no selective interference beyond independent assortment; values below unity quantify the net coalescent compression attributable directly to physical linkage along chromosomes.

\subsection{The spatial distribution of selective variance}\label{sec:spatial_variance}
The chromosomal integration developed in \autoref{sec:linkage} spreads additive genetic variance uniformly along the genetic map of each chromosome (i.e., $1/M$). This formalizes a spatially uniform realization of the infinitesimal model, wherein heritable fitness variance arises from a dense continuum of loci with individually negligible effects (Fisher 1918; Barton et al. 2017). Because the true genetic architecture of relative fitness in wild populations remains an unresolved empirical question spanning the continuum from highly polygenic to oligogenic architectures (Barton and Keightley 2002), we treat the uniform assumption as a theoretical baseline and characterize the directional consequences of non-uniform spatial dispersion.

Let $g(x) \ge 0$ represent an arbitrary density of selective variance along chromosome $i$, normalized such that $\int_0^M g(x)\,dx = 1$. The position-specific interference profile experienced by a neutral site at genetic position $y$ is:
\begin{equation}
    Q^2(y, g) = \int_0^M g(x)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}\left(1 + e^{-2|x-y|}\right)}\right)^2 dx.
\end{equation}
Assuming neutral sites are uniformly distributed along the genetic map, the chromosome-wide average interference is $\overline{Q^2}(g) = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^M Q^2(y, g)\,dy$. Because the underlying interference kernel $K(x,y) = \left(1 - \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2|x-y|})\right)^{-2}$ is symmetric ($K(x,y) = K(y,x)$), Fubini's theorem permits exchanging the order of integration:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}(g) = \int_0^M g(x) \left[\frac{1}{M}\int_0^M K(x,y)\,dy\right] dx = \int_0^M g(x)\,Q^2(x)\,dx, \label{general_g}
\end{equation}
where $Q^2(x) \equiv Q^2(x; g_\text{unif})$ denotes the baseline spatial profile derived in \autoref{sec:linkage} (Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg}). When selective variance is distributed uniformly ($g(x) = 1/M$), Equation~\eqref{general_g} recovers $\overline{Q^2}_i$. 

For an oligogenic architecture comprising $J$ discrete loci of equal effect at map positions $x_1, \ldots, x_J$, the variance density collapses to a sum of Dirac delta functions, $g(x) = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \delta(x - x_j)$, reducing Equation~\eqref{general_g} to a discrete sample mean:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}(g) = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J Q^2(x_j). \label{oligogenic_mean}
\end{equation}
Because the distance-decay function $f_\text{H}(\ell)$ is strictly decreasing ($f_\text{H}'(\ell) < 0$; see \autoref{app:chromosapp}), the baseline spatial profile $Q^2(x)$ is strictly concave ($d^2 Q^2(x)/dx^2 < 0$). It achieves its unique global maximum at the chromosome midpoint ($x = M/2$) and reaches symmetrical minima at the telomeres ($x \in \{0, M\}$), reflecting that a centrally positioned locus exerts physical interference across two flanking chromosomal arms, whereas a telomeric locus interferes with only one.

The aggregate reduction in effective population size depends on the expectation of the convex function $\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h Q^2\right)$. For a single causal locus placed at a uniformly distributed random map position $x_0 \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,M]$, Jensen's inequality guarantees:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{x_0}\left[\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h Q^2(x_0)\right)\right] > \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h\,\mathbb{E}[Q^2(x_0)]\right) = \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h \overline{Q^2}_i\right).
\end{equation}
Because higher values of $(N_e/N)_i$ represent a weaker depression of effective size, a localized locus yields, on average, a smaller reduction in neutral diversity than spreading that same variance uniformly across the chromosome. A second-order Taylor expansion reveals that for an oligogenic architecture of $J$ independently placed loci, this Jensen gap scales inversely with locus number:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h \overline{Q^2}(g)\right)\right] - \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h \overline{Q^2}_i\right) \approx \frac{(\kappa_\text{cis} v_h)^2}{2J}\exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis} v_h \overline{Q^2}_i\right)\mathrm{Var}_x\left[Q^2(x)\right].
\end{equation}
Consequently, under random genomic placement, the spatially uniform model maximizes the predicted reduction in neutral diversity. It provides a conservative lower bound on the retained effective size (a theoretical ceiling on the magnitude of selective reduction), ensuring that our analytical baseline does not underestimate diversity losses due to arbitrary polygenic clustering.

This conservative bound holds strictly when causal loci are distributed independently of local recombination rates. If selected alleles are physically concentrated in regions of suppressed recombination—such as pericentromeric heterochromatin or chromosomal inversions, where quantitative trait loci and adaptive variants frequently accumulate (Kirkpatrick and Barton 2006; Yeaman 2013)—their genetic density $g(x)$ spikes sharply in map distance. Such structural clustering elevates local values of $Q^2(x_j)$ above the chromosome-wide average $\overline{Q^2}_i$, closing and potentially reversing the Jensen gap. Where chromosome-level assemblies and fine-scale genetic maps are available, the uniform expectation can be replaced directly by an empirical annotation-based estimator:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_i(\hat{g}_i) = \frac{1}{G_i}\sum_{j=1}^{G_i} Q^2(x_j^{(i)}),
\end{equation}
where $x_j^{(i)}$ represents the genetic position of the $j$-th annotated functional element (e.g., coding sequence or conserved non-coding element) across the $G_i$ elements on chromosome $i$.

\subsection{The coalescent ceiling on heritable fitness variance}\label{sec:ceiling}
By inverting the unlinked Robertson baseline (Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating}), we can derive the theoretical ceiling on additive genetic variance in relative fitness compatible with maintaining a specified fraction of neutral diversity. Let $\delta$ denote the empirical diversity retention ratio:
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N},
\end{equation}
which quantifies the coalescent effective size relative to the census adult size, benchmarked against an idealized neutral Wright--Fisher population. Under the long-run demographic and selective equilibrium established in \autoref{sec:quantity} ($N_{eC} = N_{eV} = N_e$), setting Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} equal to $\delta$ and solving for variance yields the analytical upper bound:
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\text{max}} = \frac{1 - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta}. \label{eq:vamax}
\end{equation}

This ceiling depends exclusively on the observed diversity fraction $\delta$ and the mating-system scalar $\kappa(\alpha)$. Strict social monogamy ($\kappa = 2$) exactly halves $V_{A,\text{max}}$ relative to a system without a pair bond ($\kappa = 1$): each nest pools two partners' breeding values, so the same neutral diversity deficit is generated by half the underlying fitness variance. Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax} assumes $G = 1$. With selection depleting the variance it acts on ($G = 1 - V_A$, Appendix A), the unlinked reduction never exceeds $N/N_e = 2$ without a pair bond, or 3 under strict monogamy, for any $V_A$ in the model's domain ($V_A < 1$). Below $\delta = 1/2$ (respectively $1/3$) no $V_A$ in that domain meets the bound, and the ceiling is meaningful only as the $G = 1$ upper limit.

Critically, Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax} establishes a conservative, most-permissive ceiling on heritable fitness variance due to two structural properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Monotonic compression by linkage ($\overline{\Omega} \le 1$):} Because physical linkage can only intensify selective interference along chromosomes (\autoref{variance_partitioning}), accounting for genome architecture strictly lowers the allowable variance. The bound is exact when both terms use the same persistence $Z$, as in Equation~\eqref{total_red}. Write $x = \kappa_\text{cis}\overline{Q^2}_iv_h$, $y = \kappa_\text{own}Q_u^2v_h$ and $K = Q_u^2\kappa V_A$. Then $x \ge y$, because $\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q_u^2$ and $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$ in the primary model. Also $K \ge y$. Since $e^{-x} \le 1/(1+x)$, $\Omega_i \le (1+K)/[(1+y)(1+K-y)] \le 1$. Mixing persistences breaks this: with $Z < 1$ in the linked term but $Q = 2$ in the unlinked one, $\Omega_i$ exceeds 1 on long maps at large $1 - Z$ (up to 1.08 for $1 - Z \le 0.6$).
    \begin{equation}
    V_{A,\text{max}}^{(\text{linked})} = \frac{\overline{\Omega} - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta} \le \frac{1 - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta} = V_{A,\text{max}}.
    \end{equation}
    No arrangement of recombination rates or functional gene densities can elevate neutral diversity above the unlinked expectation. An empirical estimate of $V_A$ that exceeds $V_{A,\text{max}}$ is therefore fundamentally incompatible with observed neutral diversity, even before the additional coalescent compression of linked selection is applied.
    \item \textbf{Idealized demographic baseline ($V_{k,\text{env}} = 2$):} The derivation attributes all family-size overdispersion beyond the Poisson expectation strictly to additive genetic variance in fitness. If environmental stochasticity, social hierarchies, or non-additive genetic effects inflate baseline reproductive variance ($V_{k,\text{env}} > 2$), the variance in relative fitness permitted by a given $\delta$ is depressed even further.
\end{enumerate}

Because $V_{A,\text{max}}$ is derived directly from the unlinked Mendelian transmission baseline ($Q^2 = 4$), it inherits that result's independence from the fine-scale dynamics of the selected background. It holds under stationary mutation--selection--drift balance, during directional sweeps (where $V_A$ represents the coalescent time-averaged variance, $\bar{V}_A$), and under fluctuating selection provided generational fitness autocovariances are non-negative ($\tau_\text{env} \ge 2$). In the Results, we evaluate Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax} across the continuum $\delta \in (0, 1]$, confronting published vertebrate pedigree estimates directly against the coalescent boundaries imposed by their census and genomic diversity.

\subsection{The variance sustainable at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{msdb}
Three evolutionary fluxes govern the standing additive genetic variance in relative fitness at stationarity. New mutations enter the diploid genome at rate $V_m = U_d \mathbb{E}[s_\text{het}^2]$, where $U_d$ is the total diploid deleterious mutation rate and $s_\text{het}$ is the heterozygous selection coefficient. Purifying selection depletes standing variance at rate $s_\text{het} V_A$ per generation as deleterious alleles are purged, while genetic drift removes variance through stochastic gametic sampling at rate $V_A/(2N_e)$. Setting the net flux to zero ($\Delta V_\text{mut} + \Delta V_\text{sel} + \Delta V_\text{drift} = 0$) and solving for the stationary variance yields (Appendix~D):
\begin{equation}
V_A = \frac{2N_e U_d s_\text{het}^2}{1 + 2N_e s_\text{het}}. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}

When purifying selection dominates genetic drift ($2N_e s_\text{het} \gg 1$), the drift term $1/(2N_e)$ becomes negligible compared to selective purging, and Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} collapses to the classical mutation--selection balance regime (Crow 1970; Charlesworth 1990):
\begin{equation}
V_A \approx U_d s_\text{het} \qquad (2N_e s_\text{het} \gg 1). \label{eq:HoC}
\end{equation}
In this deterministic regime, deleterious alleles are held at rare mutation--selection equilibrium ($q \approx \mu/s_\text{het}$), and standing variance reflects the product of the mutational influx rate and the selective effect size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} yields the exact positive root as a quadratic in $s_\text{het}$ (Appendix~D); under the strong-selection limit, this reduces directly to:
\begin{equation}
s^* = \frac{V_A}{U_d}. \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
Crucially, when integrated across a continuous distribution of fitness effects (DFE) with density $\phi(s)$, the linearity of the approximation ensures that $V_A \approx U_d \int_0^\infty s \phi(s)\,ds = U_d \bar{s}_\text{het}$. The quantity $s^*$ is therefore not an abstract single-locus toy parameter, but the implied arithmetic mean of the vertebrate DFE ($\bar{s}_\text{het}^*$) necessary to maintain the observed variance at stationary balance.

To evaluate Equation~\eqref{eq:sstar}, we set the diploid deleterious mutation rate to $U_d = 4\mu L_\text{coding}$: a baseline of $2\mu$ per base pair for the diploid genome, multiplied by an additional factor of two to account for functional non-coding regulatory sequences of equal aggregate length to the annotated exome ($L_\text{coding}$). Assuming that all mutations across this combined functional target are deleterious ($f_d = 1$) maximizes $U_d$, thereby minimizing $s^*$ and rendering our evaluation against empirical DFE estimates strictly conservative.

The alternative boundary condition where selection is weak ($2N_e s_\text{het} \ll 1$) reduces Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} to $V_A \approx 2N_e U_d s_\text{het}^2$. Solving for the implied selection coefficient yields $s^*_\text{weak} = \sqrt{V_A / (2N_e U_d)}$. Testing the foundational premise of this regime requires $2N_e s^*_\text{weak} \ll 1$, which demands an effective population size $N_e \ll U_d / (2V_A) \approx 5\text{--}10$. Because all monitored vertebrate populations maintain sequence-derived $N_e$ orders of magnitude larger than this threshold, the near-neutral regime refutes its own premise and collapses (Appendix~D).

Finally, under stationary strong selection, the ratio of mutational influx to standing variance simplifies to $V_m/V_A \approx (U_d s_\text{het}^2)/(U_d s_\text{het}) = s_\text{het}$. This formalizes the per-generation survival of selective variance as $Z = 1 - V_m/V_A \approx 1 - s_\text{het}$, closing the analytical loop with the linked chromosome integration developed in \autoref{sec:linkage}. If alleles are as strongly selected as pedigree estimates require ($s^*$ median 0.16, range 0.006--0.78), ancestral associations decay even faster than under neutral Mendelian segregation ($Q(s) = 2/(1+s) < 2$; Charlesworth 2012, Appendix; Appendix~D), further confirming that the baseline Robertson model ($Q^2 = 4$) represents a strictly conservative upper bound on selective interference.

\subsection{Genomic Architecture and Functional Target Size}
For species with chromosome-level genome assemblies, reference sequences and gene annotation files (GTF or GFF format) were retrieved from NCBI. Analysis was restricted to fully assembled autosomes with unambiguous correspondence to a major linkage group in the published linkage map (see below); unplaced scaffolds, unlocalized contigs, sex chromosomes, and mitochondrial sequence were excluded. Each assembled autosome was assigned to its corresponding linkage group on the basis of marker content and ordering reported in the linkage map source publication.

The between-chromosome partition of additive fitness variance was computed from annotated protein-coding gene counts following Equation (19):
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{f}i = \frac{G_i}{G_{\text{total}}}
\end{equation*}
where $G_i$ is the number of annotated protein-coding loci on autosome $i$ and $G_{\text{total}} = \sum_i G_i$ over all included autosomes. Gene counts were taken from the feature tables of NCBI assembly annotation reports. For species without available genome assemblies, $f_i$ was approximated as proportional to physical chromosome length, $f_i \approx L_i / L_{\text{total}}$.

For the mutational influx calculation (below), total genome-wide coding sequence length $L_{\text{coding}}$ was estimated independently of the gene-count partition. All CDS features in the annotation file were extracted and overlapping intervals on each strand collapsed into non-redundant contiguous ranges to prevent multiple isoforms and overlapping reading frames from inflating the functional target; the genome-wide collapsed coding length is $L_{\text{coding}} = \sum_i L_{\text{coding},i}$. This quantity enters only the $U_d$ calculation and does not affect the $\hat{f}_i$ partition.

For chromosomes substantially longer than 0.5 Morgan, the linear approximation overestimates the recombination fraction between distant loci relative to the Haldane mapping function, causing a mild underestimate of $Q^2$ from those pairs; because this produces a conservative (upward) bias in the predicted $N_e/N$, the approximation errs in the direction of understating constraint. \begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}
where $\mu$ is the published per-base-pair per-generation point mutation rate for each species and the factor of four converts from a per-haplotype per-base-pair rate to a diploid rate over both coding and regulatory sequence. This was evaluated across the joint 95\% credible intervals of the published $V_A$ posterior and the empirical uncertainty in $\mu$, enabling a test of whether the maintenance of observed standing variance requires selection coefficients consistent with known vertebrate DFE parameters and tolerable levels of mutational load.

\section{Results}



\subsection{Intragenomic Variation in Reduction}
While the expected species-wide diversity, $\mathbb{E}[N_e/N]$, provides a global summary of evolutionary constraint, the continuous spatial framework predicts highly heterogeneous topographies of diversity within the genome. By evaluating the Main Equation across independent chromosomes, we can analytically partition the sources of intragenomic variance.

For a focal chromosome $i$, the genome-wide unlinked baseline generated by independent assortment is captured by the denominator of the Main Equation: $1 + Q_u^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_i/2)$. Because the variance segregating on a single haplotype represents a minor fraction of the genome-wide total or $v_i/2 \ll V_A$, this unlinked constraint is effectively uniform across the genome. Consequently, any predicted variance in standing neutral diversity between chromosomes is driven strictly by the localized, constraint of physical linkage.

Because the localized selective variance is proportional to the functional target size of the chromosome, i.e. $v_i = V_A f_i$, the survival of neutral lineages is governed by the structural ratio of functional density to recombinational map length. Chromosomes with high coding densities and restricted recombination suffer severe exponential decay in local $N_e$, driving rapid lineage sorting. Conversely, chromosomes with expansive map lengths and sparse functional targets effectively isolate neutral alleles from selective backgrounds. These structural dynamics predict the existence of macro-genomic regions that maintain robust neutral diversity even when the global, genome-wide constraint is strong.

This translates to sequence-derived $N_e/N$ estimates of [XX]–[XX] (Corbett-Detig et al. 2015; Buffalo 2021; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026)—values two to three orders of magnitude above our theoretical predictions. The magnitude of this mismatch between ecologically estimated fitness variance and coalescent-scale diversity forms a quantitative paradox that cannot be resolved by parametric uncertainty alone.

The magnitude of this discrepancy persists even under permissive parameterizations of the genomic and mutational inputs. Expanding the functional target size to include a conserved regulatory component equivalent in length to the annotated exome, and evaluating the diploid deleterious mutation rate $U_d$ across the full range of empirical uncertainty, maximizes the potential for mutational turnover to replenish fitness variance. Despite this, the per-generation variance survival rate ($Z = 1 - V_m/V_A$) approaches its theoretical maximum across all species. Because the mutational influx to fitness variance ($V_m \approx U_d s^2$) is bounded by empirical $U_d$ and realistic selection coefficients, the massive $V_A$ estimated from contemporary pedigrees necessarily drives $Z \to 1$. Biologically, $Z \to 1$ indicates that statistical associations between neutral alleles and their selected backgrounds persist across many generations before dissipating via recombination or independent assortment. This sustained covariance amplifies the chromosome-wide interference ($\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked}$) toward its ceiling, concentrating the coalescent histories of neutral alleles into the reproductive trajectories of the linked selected sites.

Furthermore, because the Relative Linkage Effect ($\Omega_i$) is bounded at or below one, no spatial arrangement of selected loci can restore neutral diversity above the global unlinked expectation. Physical linkage can only exacerbate the interference imposed by independent assortment; it cannot relieve it. The Global Linkage Effect ($\overline{\Omega}$) ranges from [XX] to [XX] across species, confirming that chromosomal architecture universally amplifies the unlinked reduction in $N_e$, with the most severe interference localized to gene-dense chromosomes possessing short genetic map lengths.

This dynamic is starkly evident in the avian taxa: the bimodal avian karyotype sequesters a substantial fraction of functional targets on microchromosomes. With one obligate crossover per bivalent, microchromosomes still have map lengths of order 0.5 Morgans or more. They are far from complete linkage, which requires $M \ll 1 - Z$. But in the intermediate regime $\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked} \approx 2/[M(1-Z)]$, so the predicted reduction in local diversity scales with $1/(1-Z)$ and is sensitive to the parameterization of $Z$, and through it to the assumed distribution of fitness effects ($Z \approx 1 - \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s]$). The inability of bounding parameterizations to resolve this mismatch in any species establishes a fundamental incompatibility between the magnitude of ecologically estimated fitness variance and the mechanics of the neutral coalescent.

This incompatibility bears directly on Lewontin’s paradox: the observation that genome-wide nucleotide diversity varies across taxa by far less than their census population sizes would predict, implying a systematic, non-linear decoupling of effective and census sizes (Lewontin 1974). Extensive comparative genomic surveys confirm that $N_e$ inferred from nucleotide diversity varies by roughly three orders of magnitude, whereas census sizes span more than eight (Leffler et al. 2012; Buffalo 2021). Linked selection—comprising background selection against recurrent deleterious mutations and interference from selective sweeps—is the consensus proximate mechanism suppressing $N_e$ relative to $N$. Furthermore, the magnitude of this reduction ($N_e/N$) correlates positively with species-level proxies for the total intensity of linked selection (Corbett-Detig et al. 2015).

The theoretical framework presented here formalizes this dynamic by specifying the coalescent mechanism—heritable variance in fitness amplifying the Robertson effect across both unlinked and physically linked architectures—that drives the reduction in $N_e/N$. However, substituting pedigree-derived variance estimates into this framework reverses the standard theoretical gap. While classical background selection models frequently underestimate the reduction in $N_e$ required to match observed diversity (Corbett-Detig et al. 2015; Comeron 2017), our model parameterized with empirical $V_A$ predicts reductions two to three orders of magnitude more severe than those observed. Although the predicted coalescent mechanism correctly suppresses diversity, the magnitude of fitness variance estimated by animal models in the wild is mathematically irreconcilable with the deep evolutionary maintenance of nucleotide variation. This structural mismatch restates Lewontin’s paradox in quantitative genetic terms, establishing the transmissible component of fitness variance as a deeply constrained observable that must jointly satisfy both contemporary phenotypic models and historical coalescent diversity.

The structural mismatch between contemporary variance estimates and historical diversity strongly suggests that a substantial fraction of the $V_A$ estimated by pedigree-based animal models in wild populations does not constitute stable, transmissible additive genetic variance. Linear mixed models structured by pedigree relatedness detect statistical associations between phenotypic similarity and genealogical proximity. However, high statistical fit does not imply biological validity in partitioning transmission genetics (Kruuk 2004; Hadfield et al. 2010). Because related individuals in structured wild populations frequently experience spatially or socially autocorrelated environments—such as high-quality territories, maternal provisioning, or localized resource patches linked to natal dispersal—common-environment effects generate phenotypic covariance among relatives that the basic animal model erroneously absorbs into the additive genetic component (Kruuk and Hadfield 2007; Stopher et al. 2012). In kin-structured populations, indirect genetic effects—where an individual's phenotype depends on the genotypes of social partners—further confound this separation, producing substantial upward bias in $V_A$ estimates when unmodeled (Baud et al. 2022; Mawass and Milot 2025).

These biases are most acute for fitness traits, as realized reproductive success is phenotypically correlated with nearly all life-history traits sensitive to environmental quality. Furthermore, models using unweighted lifetime reproductive success (LRS) as a fitness proxy systematically overestimate Fisherian additive variance; age-structured demographic models establish that LRS fails to appropriately discount delayed reproduction and generation-time variation, artificially inflating variance estimates for iteroparous vertebrates (Sæther and Engen 2015; de Villemereuil et al. 2020). The shallow, unbalanced pedigrees typical of wild populations also lack the structural power required to separate additive from dominance and epistatic contributions, allowing non-additive variance to inflate $V_A$ estimates (Wolak and Keller 2014; [Insert citation to your JEB paper here]). This instability is empirically supported by temporal fluctuations in $V_A$ estimates across years within the same monitored populations, a pattern incompatible with a stable additive architecture [CITE temporal instability of $V_A$ results for specific monitored populations in dataset].

The coalescent framework developed here quantifies this bias. By mapping the theoretical ceiling of sustainable variance ($V_{A,\text{max}}$) directly to observed nucleotide diversity, it provides a strict upper bound on the stable, transmissible variance a population can harbor without undergoing coalescent collapse. The fraction of reported $V_A$ that exceeds $V_{A,\text{max}}$ ranges from [XX]\% to [XX]\% across the analyzed species. This excess defines the minimum non-transmissible fraction of the reported variance, indicating that the statistical signal driving these animal models is fundamentally disconnected from the evolutionary mechanics governing long-term molecular diversity.

Alternatively, this discrepancy may arise from violations of the demographic assumptions inherent in the coalescent framework rather than statistical bias in the ecological variance estimates. The monitored populations in our dataset are defined by the practical geography of fieldwork—nest box grids, island boundaries, or marked cohorts—rather than the biological boundaries of the effective breeding unit. Because wild study populations are typically demes within larger, structured metapopulations, cryptic immigration from unsampled conspecifics continuously replenishes local variation. If this immigration occurs at rates comparable to or exceeding the local coalescent rate, it maintains diversity even when local selective interference suppresses the within-deme $N_e$. Theoretical work on the interaction of gene flow and linked selection establishes that the restoration of diversity depends on the ratio of immigration to local drift and selection, with modest gene flow capable of maintaining diversity well above the local selective equilibrium (Whitlock and Barton 1997; Charlesworth et al. 2003).

Gene flow at rate $m$ per generation introduces neutral haplotypes with distinct selective histories, driving local diversity toward the metapopulation average over a timescale of approximately $1/m$ generations. For the passerine populations most heavily represented in the quantitative genetics literature, natal dispersal rates suggest $m$ on the order of [XX] per generation. This implies a restoration timescale of [XX] generations—long relative to the pedigree observation window, but short relative to the coalescent timescale governing the maintenance of $\pi$. The capacity of this demographic replenishment to maintain observed $\pi$ against the predicted local reduction in $N_e/N$ depends directly on metapopulation geometry, dispersal distance distributions, and the spatial scale of the environmental covariance underlying $V_A$. Future extensions of this framework must couple spatially explicit metapopulation models with the Robertson effect to determine the threshold at which locally estimated $V_A$ ceases to predict species-wide sequence variation.

A final resolution involves violations of the stationarity assumption underlying the derivation of the continuous interference profile $\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked}$. The chromosome-wide constraint assumes that $V_A$ remains approximately constant across generations—a condition satisfied under mutation-selection-drift equilibrium on a stable fitness landscape. This assumption fails if the direction or magnitude of selection fluctuates. Temporally variable selection attenuates the trans-generational autocovariance in relative fitness that drives the Robertson effect: lineages expressing high fitness in one generation may be selected against in the next as the optimum shifts. This oscillation disrupts the multi-generational association between neutral alleles and their selected genetic backgrounds, collapsing the $Q^2$ amplification of selective interference (Buffalo and Coop 2019). The critical requirement for this attenuation is that fitness reversals occur on a timescale comparable to or shorter than $1/s$ generations, where $s$ is the mean fitness differential; reversals operating on longer timescales permit substantial coalescent compression before selection alters direction.

Rapidly oscillating environmental optima, interannual variation in favored genotypes, frequency-dependent dynamics, and shifting spatial fitness gradients can all generate this required attenuation in wild populations. Genomic evidence tracking temporal allele-frequency changes confirms the operation of selective interference at genome-wide scales, yet the observed magnitudes fall well below the expectations generated by pedigree-derived $V_A$ (Buffalo and Coop 2020). If temporally fluctuating selection drives this paradox, it demonstrates that pedigree-based $V_A$ estimated over short observation windows systematically overstates the temporal mean selective autocovariance governing the neutral coalescent. The relevant parameter for coalescent predictions is not the instantaneous statistical $V_A$ isolated in a single generation, but the long-run, time-averaged transmissible component. Because these three resolutions—pedigree estimation bias, metapopulation gene flow, and temporally fluctuating selection—are not mutually exclusive, identifying their relative contributions remains a major empirical challenge. By defining the theoretical limits of sustainable variance, the $V_{A,\text{max}}$ diagnostic framework provides a quantitative baseline to structure this empirical program.

The implied selection coefficients (Appendix D) demonstrate that contemporary $V_A$ estimates are incompatible with steady-state mutation-selection-drift balance. In the strong-selection limit, the deterministic rare-allele balance $V_A \approx U_d s$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Charlesworth 2012) defines the required average heterozygous selection coefficient as $s^* = V_A/U_d$. Given posterior modes of $V_A$ from 0.003 to 0.50 (median 0.10) and diploid deleterious mutation rates of $U_d = 0.34$--$1.26$ for the analyzed vertebrates, this relationship requires $s^* = 0.006$--$0.78$ (median 0.16). This expectation exceeds empirical estimates of the mean heterozygous selection coefficient by one to two orders of magnitude, as vertebrate distributions of fitness effects consistently cluster around $\bar{s} \approx 10^{-3}–10^{-2}$ (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017). The Robertson formula compounds this implausibility: it treats $V_A$ as a sufficient statistic for the unlinked interference effect, but this equivalence holds only in the small-$t$ limit where heterozygous selection coefficients are well below one. When fitness variance is concentrated in alleles of large heterozygous effect—precisely the regime implied by $\bar{t}^* = V_A/U_d$ at the reported $V_A$ magnitudes—the per-locus interference at unlinked sites scales as $t/(1+t)^2$ rather than $t$ (equivalently $8\mu t/(1+t)^2$ against $8\mu t$ per site), so that strongly deleterious alleles contribute less genome-wide interference per unit variance than the Robertson formula predicts (Charlesworth 2012, Appendix; Santiago and Caballero 1998). As shown in Appendix D, this correction multiplies the unlinked term $4\kappa V_A$ by about $1/(1+\bar{t}^*)^2$, 0.31--0.99 across populations. It lowers the predicted reduction in diversity, so it widens the diversity gap rather than closing it.

The weak-selection regime is similarly incompatible. Solving for the implied selection coefficient under the near-neutral approximation ($V_A \approx 2N_e U_d s^2$) and substituting the resultant values yields $N_e s \gg 1$ for all populations in our dataset (Appendix D). Because $N_e s \gg 1$ indicates that directional selection overwhelms genetic drift, this outcome invalidates the foundational premise of the weak-selection limit. Consequently, neither strong-selection nor weak-selection boundaries can recover the reported $V_A$ magnitudes when constrained by empirically calibrated mutation rates and selection coefficients. This inability to reconcile pedigree-derived variance with steady-state mutational limits establishes an independent, demographic-free analogue to the coalescent paradox.

The theoretical framework developed here relies on several structural approximations that define the boundaries of its application. First, the equilibrium derivation (Appendix D) assumes a uniform heterozygous selection coefficient ($s$) across all deleterious loci. Empirical distributions of fitness effects (DFE) in vertebrates, however, are strongly leptokurtic, characterized by a bulk of mildly deleterious mutations and a long tail of large-effect alleles. In the strong-selection limit the sustainable variance is $V_A = U_d\bar{s}$ whatever the shape of the DFE, so $s^* = V_A/U_d$ estimates its arithmetic mean (Appendix D). Shape matters only through nearly neutral mutations ($4N_es \lesssim 1$), which contribute less than $U_ds$. A more leptokurtic DFE with the same mean puts more mass there, lowers the sustainable $V_A$, and widens the gap. The uniform-$s$ approximation therefore understates the mismatch. DFEs inferred from site-frequency spectra do not constrain the strongly deleterious tail, so the comparison of $s^*$ with their mean rests partly on an extrapolation. Second, the analytical framework models discrete, non-overlapping generations. The wild vertebrate populations analyzed here are iteroparous, necessitating age-structured demographic models that modify the mapping between family-size variance and $N_e$ (Engen et al. 2005). While this age-structure correction is moderate for species with low interannual survival variance, it must be integrated into future demographic modeling of specific iteroparous study systems. Third, the current formulation is restricted to autosomal inheritance. For avian taxa harboring substantial functional content on the Z chromosome, the hemizygous Z in females is transmitted without recombination, placing it in the complete-linkage limit (map length $M = 0$ in the heterogametic sex). Excluding sex chromosomes therefore systematically underestimates the total genome-wide selective interference for these species. Fourth, the spatial distribution of fitness variance within chromosomes is modeled as uniform per Morgan, conforming to the infinitesimal model. As established mathematically via Jensen’s inequality, distributing variance uniformly minimizes local interference compared to randomly positioned large-effect loci, generating an upper bound on $N_e/N$. However, if unobserved causal loci cluster in regions of suppressed recombination, this approximation reverses, underestimating the local interference. Because the empirical genomic locations of loci contributing to pedigree-derived $V_A$ remain unmapped for these monitored populations, this architectural uncertainty imposes a fundamental limit on the precision of spatial interference predictions. Finally, the predictions assume demographic and environmental stationarity. Populations experiencing recent bottlenecks, expansions, or habitat fragmentation violate this assumption. Because several monitored populations in our dataset occupy restricted island habitats or fragmented ranges, their current molecular diversity ($\pi$) reflects non-equilibrium historical demography. In such systems, resolving the fraction of the $N_e$ reduction attributable to historical bottlenecks versus contemporary selective interference requires independent demographic inference prior to applying the equilibrium framework.

This study establishes a quantitative bridge between the heritable fitness variance estimated from contemporary pedigrees and the neutral diversity predicted by coalescent theory, demonstrating that these two bodies of empirical data are mutually irreconcilable. By integrating the Robertson effect with mating-system structure, continuous chromosomal architecture, and empirical functional target sizes, this framework derives the theoretical maximum additive genetic variance ($V_{A,\text{max}}$) compatible with maintaining observed nucleotide diversity. The observation that reported $V_A$ exceeds this threshold by factors of [XX]–[XX] across [X\_OUT\_OF\_N] species constitutes a structural incompatibility that persists across all bounding parameterizations. Consequently, pedigree-based animal models likely capture a conflated mixture of stable Mendelian transmission and environmentally structured covariance among relatives. This non-genetic covariance inflates statistical estimates of $V_A$ but fails to generate the multi-generational selective interference necessary to reduce long-term $N_e$. Metapopulation gene flow and temporally fluctuating selection further decouple instantaneous phenotypic variance from long-term coalescent dynamics. Ultimately, these findings establish a fundamental limit on the interpretation of quantitative genetic parameters in wild populations: heritable variance in relative fitness cannot be treated as an isolated ecological metric decoupled from its population-genomic context. The magnitude of variance derived from pedigrees must be evaluated against the consequent reduction in effective population size before it can be interpreted as the stable, transmissible Mendelian variance that drives long-term evolutionary adaptation.

\section{Appendix A: Derivation of the Unlinked Baseline}
To derive the baseline reduction in effective population size generated by unlinked heritable fitness variance, we track the cumulative variance in allele frequency change driven by the selected genetic background, independent of continuous spatial linkage.

In a neutral Wright-Fisher population with a constant diploid census size $N$, the variance in allele frequency change per generation due to random genetic drift for a neutral allele at frequency $p$ is the standard gametic sampling variance:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p^{\text{drift}}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}

When variance in reproductive success is heritable ($V_A > 0$), the neutral allele experiences selective interference from the genetic background upon which it resides. In a given generation, a diploid population of $N$ individuals has relative fitnesses $w_i$ with expectation $\mathbb{E}[w_i] = 1$ and variance $\text{Var}(w_i) = V_W$. The frequency of the neutral allele in the subsequent generation is approximately:

\begin{equation}
    p' \approx p + \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w_i - 1)z_i
\end{equation}
where $z_i \in \{0, \frac{1}{2}, 1\}$ denotes the allele copy number in individual $i$ under Hardy-Weinberg proportions. Because the neutral locus is unlinked from all selected sites, relative fitness $w_i$ and the neutral genotype $z_i$ are uncorrelated in expectation ($\mathbb{E}[(w_i-1)z_i] = 0$). The variance in allele frequency change driven purely by this single generation of heritable fitness variation is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p^{\text{sel}}) =\frac{1}{4N^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{Var}((w_i - 1)z_i) \approx V_A \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}

This formulation (Robertson 1961) relies on a large-$N$ approximation, assumes Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium ($\text{Var}(z_i) = p(1-p)/2$), and isolates the additive genetic component $V_A$ from the total fitness variance, as contributions from dominance and epistasis do not transmit stably across generations to drive multi-generational allele frequency changes.

Because the neutral allele and the selected background are unlinked (segregating on non-homologous chromosomes), they assort independently during meiosis ($r = 0.5$). Consequently, the statistical association, or linkage disequilibrium, between the neutral allele and its initial selected background halves each generation. The directional change in the neutral allele frequency driven by this specific selective cohort decays geometrically:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_t^{\text{sel}} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t \Delta p^{\text{sel}}
\end{equation}

Integrating this effect over time yields the total cumulative change in the neutral allele frequency resulting from a single generation of heritable variance:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_{\text{total}}^{\text{sel}} = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t \Delta p^{\text{sel}} = 2 \Delta p^{\text{sel}}
\end{equation}

This series is a special case. Santiago and Caballero (1995; reviewed in Wang et al. 2016) give the general cumulative factor
\begin{equation}
    Q = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \left[\frac{G(1+r_{mf})}{2}\right]^i = \frac{2}{2 - G(1+r_{mf})},
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the fraction of additive variance remaining among selected parents and $r_{mf}$ is the correlation between the expected selective values of male and female parents. (We write $r_{mf}$ to keep it apart from the recombination fraction $r$.) The series above sets $G = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$, and both are assumptions. $G = 1$ requires that one generation of selection removes a negligible share of the additive variance it acts on, which holds when $V_A \ll 1$. $r_{mf} = 0$ requires that mates pair at random with respect to their breeding values for fitness; under random mating Santiago and Caballero give $r_{mf} = -1/(N-1)$. Each departure moves $Q$ in a known direction. Selection strong enough to deplete variance within a generation ($G < 1$) lowers $Q$ below 2 and weakens the reduction in $N_e$. Assortative mating for fitness ($r_{mf} > 0$) raises it.

\paragraph{How far the data sit from $G = 1$.} The measured posterior modes run from 0.003 to 0.50 (median 0.10). That is not uniformly $\ll 1$, so the size of the correction has to be stated rather than assumed away. Let a parent's expected family size be $1 + b\,a$, linear in its breeding value $a$ with mean zero. The additive variance among parents, weighted by their offspring, is then exactly $V_A - b^2V_A^2 + b\,\mu_3$, where $\mu_3$ is the third central moment of $a$. So
\begin{equation}
    G = 1 - b^2 V_A + b\,\mu_3/V_A. \label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
Three cases bracket the data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Symmetric breeding values, fitness additive on the relative scale} ($b = 1$, $\mu_3 = 0$). Here $G = 1 - V_A$, so $Q = 2/(1+V_A)$ (the same form as $Q(s)$ in Appendix D) and
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{N}{N_e} = 1 + \frac{4V_A}{(1+V_A)^2}. \label{eq:Gcorrected}
    \end{equation}
    This rises with $V_A$ but never exceeds 2 over the domain $V_A < 1$. Against $G = 1$, it shrinks the selective term by 17\% at the median measured $V_A$ and by 56\% at the largest. The additive form itself also strains at the top of the range: at $V_A = 0.5$, 8\% of a Gaussian distribution of $1 + a$ lies below zero.
    \item \emph{Fitness multiplicative in a Gaussian breeding value}, as in the log-linked conditional-Poisson component of the model Bonnet et al. (2022) fit. Exponential selection on a normal distribution shifts its mean and leaves its variance unchanged, so $G = 1$ exactly for the link-scale breeding value.
    \item \emph{Right-skewed breeding values}, $\mu_3 > bV_A^2$, give $G > 1$.
\end{enumerate}
We therefore keep $G = 1$ throughout and report Equation~\eqref{eq:Gcorrected} as the sensitivity bound. Neither choice rescues the diversity comparison. At $G = 1$, a hundredfold deficit requires a long-run $V_A$ of 12--25, depending on $\kappa$, which is beyond the model's domain. With $G = 1 - V_A$, no $V_A$ within the domain produces more than a twofold reduction (threefold under strict monogamy, Appendix B).

Assuming $V_A$ remains stationary under mutation-selection-drift equilibrium, a new influx of selective variance of magnitude $V_A p(1-p)/(2N)$ is generated each generation. The long-run footprint of each generation's selective variation resolves to $2\Delta p^{\text{sel}}$.

The total selective variance in allele frequency change is the variance of this cumulative sum. Because the selective association persists across multiple generations, the variance of the cumulative effect scales with the square of the cumulative sum:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Var}(\Delta p_{\text{total}}^{\text{sel}}) = \text{Var}(2 \Delta p^{\text{sel}}) = 4 V_A \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}
This establishes that the multi-generational persistence of associations across independently assorting chromosomes amplifies the effective variance four-fold.

Because the neutral allele is physically unlinked from the causal selected loci, the variance generated by selective interference and the variance generated by neutral demographic sampling arise from independent biological processes. Their contributions to the total variance in allele frequency are therefore additive. Equating the standard definition of effective gametic sampling variance to the sum of these independent variances yields:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e} = \text{Var}(\Delta p^{\text{drift}}) + \text{Var}(\Delta p_{\text{total}}^{\text{sel}})
\end{equation}

Substituting the expanded terms:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e} = \frac{p(1-p)}{2N} + 4 V_A \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}
\end{equation}

Dividing by the common neutral variance term $p(1-p)$ and multiplying by 2 yields the unlinked expectation for the effective population size:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{N_e} = \frac{1}{N} (1 + 4V_A) \Rightarrow N_e = \frac{N}{1 + 4V_A}
\end{equation}
This baseline establishes the minimum selective interference generated by heritable fitness variance, irrespective of spatial chromosomal architecture. The extension to separate sexes and structured mating systems is derived in Appendix B, where the additive variance $V_A$ is scaled by the mating system parameter $\kappa(\alpha)$, yielding $N_e/N = 1/(1+4\kappa V_A)$.

\section{Appendix B: Separate Sexes and the Mating System Scalar ($\kappa$)}

Appendix A treats every individual as both a carrier of fitness genes and a parent whose own breeding value sets its family size. With separate sexes and pair bonds this fails in two ways: a parent's family size can depend on its mate's breeding value, and the two sexes must be combined into a single effective size. Neither requires new theory. We use the expected long-term genetic contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva (1999), which regress an ancestor's lasting gene flow on its selective advantages separately by sex, and the two-sex combination of Santiago and Caballero (1995). What follows applies that machinery to the mating systems in our data.

\paragraph{Setup and the meaning of $V_A$.} The population is diploid with discrete generations, constant size $N$ and an equal sex ratio. Each individual carries an additive effect $a$ on relative lifetime breeding success, and $\mathrm{Var}(a) = V_A$ in both sexes. This is the quantity Bonnet et al. (2022) estimate: one animal model per population, both sexes in the data, sex fitted as a fixed effect, and a single additive variance on the observed scale. Social partners pair at random with respect to $a$.

\paragraph{The cumulative advantage of a gene copy.} Follow a neutral gene copy carried by an individual $X$ of sex $q$. Its expected number of transmitted copies is $1 + u_X$, where $u_X$ is the relative selective advantage of $X$. Its carriers in the next generation have expected breeding value $h_1$, and from then on that expectation halves each generation through Mendelian segregation with random mates (Appendix A). The cumulative expected advantage of the copy is
\begin{equation}
    L_X = u_X + \sum_{t \ge 1} \bar{m}\, h_t = u_X + 2\bar{m}\, h_1,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{m}$ is the sex-averaged coefficient with which a carrier's own breeding value enters its selective advantage. $L_X$ is, up to scale, the regression of the expected long-term contribution of $X$ on its selective advantages (Woolliams et al. 1999, Equation 6b). Under the assumption $G = 1$ of Appendix A, the variance contributed by selection to the drift of the copy's frequency is $\mathrm{Var}(L_X)\,p(1-p)/(2N)$. Santiago and Caballero (1995) combine the sexes through $1/N_e = 1/(4N_{e,m}) + 1/(4N_{e,f})$ (after Crow and Denniston 1988). With equal sex ratio this is a mean over the two sexes, not a sum:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N}{N_e} = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{Var}(L_f) + \mathrm{Var}(L_m)\right] \equiv 1 + 4\kappa V_A. \label{eq:kappa_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{No pair bond: $\kappa = 1$.} In polygynous and polygynandrous systems each parent's family size depends on its own breeding value alone, $u = a$ in both sexes. A carrier's offspring have expected breeding value $h_1 = a/2$, since mates are random, and $\bar{m} = 1$. So $L = a + 2 \cdot a/2 = 2a$ in both sexes, $\mathrm{Var}(L) = 4V_A$, and $\kappa = 1$. This is Appendix A unchanged: with both sexes expressing the same $V_A$, separating them changes nothing. It is also the $\beta_q = 2\alpha_q$ solution of Woolliams et al. (1999) for two categories with equal selection.

\paragraph{Social monogamy with extra-pair paternity.} In a socially monogamous system the unit of productivity is the nest, and both social partners contribute to it: a female's relative offspring number is $u_f = a_f + a_s$, where $a_s$ is her social mate's breeding value. Extra-pair paternity at rate $\alpha$ moves paternity but not productivity. A male sires a fraction $1-\alpha$ of his own nest's offspring and gains extra-pair offspring elsewhere,
\begin{equation}
    u_m = (1-\alpha)(a_m + a_{f'}) + \alpha\gamma\, a_m,
\end{equation}
where $a_{f'}$ is his social mate's breeding value and $\gamma$ is the dependence of extra-pair success on his own breeding value. Model A sets $\gamma = 0$ (extra-pair siring is a lottery). Model B sets $\gamma = 1$, so that extra-pair gains replace exactly the within-pair loss. Model B is the only version in which a male's breeding value enters his own breeding success with the same weight as a female's at every $\alpha$, as Bonnet et al.'s single-variance model assumes.

A carrier's own breeding value then enters its advantage with weight 1 in females and $m = 1 - \alpha + \alpha\gamma$ in males, so $\bar{m} = (1+m)/2$. Offspring of a female carrier are sired by her social mate with probability $1-\alpha$, and offspring of a male carrier come from his social mate with weight $1-\alpha$. To first order, therefore,
\begin{equation}
    h_1^{(f)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[a_f + (1-\alpha)a_s\right], \qquad h_1^{(m)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[a_m + (1-\alpha)a_{f'}\right].
\end{equation}
Substituting,
\begin{align}
    L_f &= (1+\bar{m})\,a_f + \left[1 + \bar{m}(1-\alpha)\right] a_s, \\
    L_m &= (m+\bar{m})\,a_m + (1+\bar{m})(1-\alpha)\,a_{f'},
\end{align}
and, with partners' breeding values independent ($r_{mf} = 0$), Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa_def} gives
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(\alpha) = \frac{(1+\bar{m})^2\left[1+(1-\alpha)^2\right] + \left[1+\bar{m}(1-\alpha)\right]^2 + (m+\bar{m})^2}{8}.
\end{equation}
For the two models:
\begin{align}
    \text{Model A } (\gamma = 0):&\quad \kappa_A(\alpha) = \frac{(4-\alpha)^2\left[1+(1-\alpha)^2\right] + (4-3\alpha+\alpha^2)^2 + (4-3\alpha)^2}{32}, \\
    \text{Model B } (\gamma = 1):&\quad \kappa_B(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Limits and what is not new.} Under strict monogamy ($\alpha = 0$) both models give $\kappa = 2$. This is the base model of Santiago and Caballero (1995) itself: $N/2$ pair-mated families sharing a family fitness whose variance is $C^2 = \mathrm{Var}(a_f + a_s) = 2V_A$, so that $N/N_e = 1 + Q^2 C^2 = 1 + 8V_A$. The factor of 2 arises because each nest pools two independent breeding values. It is not a second application of the same variance. As $\alpha$ rises, $\kappa_B$ falls monotonically to $9/8$ at $\alpha = 1$; it stays above 1 because the social male still contributes to nest productivity. $\kappa_A$ falls to $7/16$, because males then gain nothing from their own breeding value.

\paragraph{General form: $G$ and $r_{mf}$.} The results above set $G = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$, the two assumptions of Appendix A, and both carry into the mating-system scalar. Let social partners' breeding values correlate $r_{mf}$, and let each generation of selection retain a fraction $G$ of the association between a gene copy and its background. A carrier with expected breeding value $h$ then has expected advantage $\bar{c}\,h$, with $\bar{c} = (1+m)/2 + r_{mf}(2-\alpha)/2$. Its carrier offspring have expected breeding value $\theta h$, with $\theta = (G/2)\left[1 + (1-\alpha)r_{mf}\right]$. Summing the geometric series,
\begin{align}
    L_f &= (1+\Phi)\,a_f + \left[1 + \Phi(1-\alpha)\right] a_s, \\
    L_m &= (m+\Phi)\,a_m + (1+\Phi)(1-\alpha)\,a_{f'}, \qquad \Phi = \frac{\bar{c}\,G/2}{1-\theta},
\end{align}
and $\mathrm{Var}(x\,a + y\,b) = V_A(x^2 + y^2 + 2r_{mf}xy)$ for partners. $\Phi = \bar{m}$ at $G = 1$, $r_{mf} = 0$, which recovers the expressions above. Two limits reproduce Santiago and Caballero exactly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strict monogamy, $\alpha = 0$: $1 + \Phi = 2/[2 - G(1+r_{mf})] = Q$ and $N/N_e = 1 + Q^2C^2$ with $C^2 = 2V_A(1+r_{mf})$, their pair-family model.
    \item No pair bond: $\kappa = Q^2/4$.
\end{itemize}
For $G$, Equation~\eqref{eq:G} applies sex by sex, with $b$ the weight of a parent's own breeding value in its family size (1 in females and in non-monogamous males, $m$ in socially monogamous males). The two sexes are averaged, $G = (G_m + G_f)/2$ (Santiago and Caballero 1995). Under Model B $m = 1$, so $G = 1 - V_A$ in every mating system in the symmetric additive case. Strict monogamy then gives $N/N_e = 1 + 8V_A/(1+V_A)^2 \le 3$.

$\kappa$ and $r_{mf}$ are different quantities. $r_{mf}$ measures assortative pairing on breeding value and lengthens the persistence of an association. $\kappa$ arises from nest sharing between partners, which in Santiago and Caballero's framework enters through the family-level $C^2$. The mating system therefore enters through three measurable features: whether a pair bond shares productivity, the extra-pair rate $\alpha$, and assortment $r_{mf}$. We set $r_{mf} = 0$ for want of estimates; positive assortment would raise $\kappa$.

\paragraph{Own, partner and linked components.} The linked term of the main text needs $\kappa$ taken apart, because only some of the variance it counts can be linked to a focal gene copy. With $r_{mf} = 0$, $\mathrm{Var}(L_f) + \mathrm{Var}(L_m)$ separates into the carrier's own breeding value and its social partner's:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}, \quad
    \kappa_\text{own} = \frac{(1+\Phi)^2 + (m+\Phi)^2}{8}, \quad
    \kappa_\text{partner} = \frac{\left[1+\Phi(1-\alpha)\right]^2 + (1-\alpha)^2(1+\Phi)^2}{8}.
\label{eq:kappa_split}
\end{equation}
The partner's genes are never on the focal copy's haplotype, so the partner component is wholly unlinked. Of the own component, the share on the focal haplotype ($v_h$) moves to the linked term. There a selected locus whose association survives a generation with probability $\rho = (1-r)Z$ contributes $w_0 + \bar{m}\,\rho/(1-\rho)$ to the copy's cumulative advantage, where $w_0$ is the carrier's own weight (1 in females, $m$ in males) and later carriers average $\bar{m}$. Relative to the unweighted $1/(1-\rho)$, this gives
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_\text{cis}(\rho) = \frac{\left[1 + \bar{m}X\right]^2 + \left[m + \bar{m}X\right]^2}{2(1+X)^2}, \qquad X = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho},
\end{equation}
which falls from $(1+m^2)/2$ as $\rho \to 0$ to $\bar{m}^2$ under tight linkage. Without a pair bond, and under Model B, $m = 1$, so $\kappa_\text{own} = \kappa_\text{cis} = 1$ and $\kappa_\text{partner} = \kappa - 1$. The chromosome-level reduction of the main text (Equation~\eqref{total_red}) uses this split: $\kappa_\text{cis}$ on the linked term, and $1 + Q_u^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)$ for the unlinked background, with $Q_u = 2/(2-Z)$.

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The derivation is first order in $a$ (weak selection per lineage), sets $G = 1$ and $r_{mf} = 0$ in the primary results, assumes an equal sex ratio, and lets the social male's breeding value contribute to all of his nest's productivity whether or not he sires the offspring. Combining Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa_def} with Appendix A yields the mating-system-corrected unlinked expectation,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa V_A},
\end{equation}
which is Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} of the main text.

\section{Appendix C: Analytical Integration of the Chromosome-Wide Constraint}\label{app:chromosapp}

This appendix evaluates the chromosome-wide interference $\overline{Q^2}_\text{linked}$ used in the main text. The kernel it integrates is established; what follows is its integration along a genetic map. We give closed forms under both the Haldane and the linear mapping function, and show that the linear form is a lower bound.

\subsection*{C.1\quad The kernel}

Consider a neutral site at map position $y$ on a chromosome of $M$ Morgans, and a selected locus at $x$, a distance $\ell = \vert x-y\vert$ away. An association between them survives one generation with probability $(1-r(\ell))Z$: recombination breaks it with probability $r(\ell)$, and $Z = 1 - V_m/V_A$ is the fraction of selective variance that persists, in the notation of Santiago and Caballero (1998). The cumulative effect of one generation's association is the geometric sum
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left[(1 - r(\ell))\,Z\right]^t = \frac{1}{1-(1-r(\ell))Z}.
\label{eq:geom_sum}
\end{equation}
This is the cumulative factor of Santiago and Caballero (2016) and Buffalo and Kern (2024, their Equation 3). With $Z = 1 - s$ it becomes $1/[s + r(1-s)]$, the background-selection kernel of Hudson and Kaplan (1995) and Nordborg et al. (1996) (Buffalo and Kern 2024, Equation 4). Squaring it, summing over selected loci, and keeping only associations on the focal haplotype gives the exponential reduction of the main text (Buffalo and Kern 2024, Equation 2; their Supplementary Equations 28--31). With selected loci spread uniformly along the map (the infinitesimal model), the profile at $y$ is
\begin{equation}
    Q^2(y) = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^M f(\vert x-y\vert)\,dx = \frac{1}{M}\left[F(y) + F(M-y)\right], \qquad F(D) = \int_0^D f(\ell)\,d\ell,
\label{eq:C_profile}
\end{equation}
with $f(\ell) = [1-(1-r(\ell))Z]^{-2}$. The split at $y$ follows Santiago and Caballero (2016) and Buffalo and Kern (2024, Supplementary Equation 32).

\subsection*{C.2\quad Closed form under the Haldane mapping function}

Under Haldane's function, $r(\ell) = \frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2\ell})$, the survival probability is $\frac{Z}{2}(1+e^{-2\ell})$ and
\begin{equation}
    f_\text{H}(\ell) = \left[A - B e^{-2\ell}\right]^{-2}, \qquad A = 1 - \tfrac{Z}{2}, \quad B = \tfrac{Z}{2}.
\end{equation}
The substitution $w = e^{-2\ell}$ gives the arm integral in elementary functions,
\begin{equation}
    F_\text{H}(D) = \frac{1}{2}\left\{\frac{2D + \ln\!\left[(A - Be^{-2D})/(1-Z)\right]}{A^2} + \frac{1}{A}\left[\frac{1}{1-Z} - \frac{1}{A - Be^{-2D}}\right]\right\}.
\label{eq:C_arm}
\end{equation}
Averaging Equation~\eqref{eq:C_profile} over neutral sites, and using the symmetry of $f$ to reduce the double integral to one over pairwise distances, weights each distance by the number of pairs at that distance:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_\text{H} = \frac{1}{M^2}\int_0^M\!\!\int_0^M f_\text{H}(\vert x-y\vert)\,dx\,dy = \frac{2}{M^2}\int_0^M (M-\ell)\,f_\text{H}(\ell)\,d\ell = \frac{2}{M^2}\int_0^M F_\text{H}(D)\,dD.
\label{eq:C_haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
This is Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} of the main text. The last integral is also closed-form, with a dilogarithm $\mathrm{Li}_2$ from $\int_0^M \ln(A - Be^{-2D})\,dD = M\ln A + \frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{Li}_2(q e^{-2M}) - \mathrm{Li}_2(q)\right]$, $q = B/A$:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_\text{H} = \frac{1}{M^2}\left\{\frac{M^2 - M\ln(1-Z) + M\ln A + \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{Li}_2(qe^{-2M}) - \mathrm{Li}_2(q)\right]}{A^2} + \frac{1}{A}\left[\frac{M}{1-Z} - \frac{2M + \ln\!\left[(A-Be^{-2M})/(1-Z)\right]}{2A}\right]\right\}.
\label{eq:C_closed_haldane}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:C_closed_haldane} agrees with numerical quadrature of Equation~\eqref{eq:C_haldane_avg} to ten significant digits for $Z \in [0.5, 0.999]$ and $M \in [0.05, 50]$.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Limits, and the scales on which they are reached.} Without recombination every distance vanishes and $\overline{Q^2}_\text{H} \to (1-Z)^{-2}$, the no-recombination background-selection limit ($e^{-U/s}$ for $Z = 1-s$). That limit needs the whole chromosome to lie within the kernel's decay distance, $M \ll 1-Z$, not merely a short map: at $Z = 0.99$ and $M = 0.5$, $\overline{Q^2}_\text{H} = 387$, against $(1-Z)^{-2} = 10^4$. At the other end $f_\text{H}(\ell) \to A^{-2} = [2/(2-Z)]^2$, Santiago and Caballero's (1995) unlinked $Q^2$ with $G$ replaced by $Z$. But the approach is slow:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_\text{H} = \left(\frac{2}{2-Z}\right)^2 + \frac{2}{M}\int_0^\infty \left[f_\text{H}(\ell) - A^{-2}\right]d\ell + O(M^{-2}),
\end{equation}
and the integral is close to $1/(1-Z)$ (10.3 at $Z = 0.9$, 105 at $Z = 0.99$). The free-recombination value is therefore reached only when $M \gg 1/(1-Z)$ Morgans. At $Z = 0.99$ and $M = 10$, $\overline{Q^2}_\text{H} = 24.8$, against the limit 3.9. Real chromosomes lie between the two limits, in the regime $\overline{Q^2}_\text{H} \approx 2/[M(1-Z)]$, which is Santiago and Caballero's (1998) Equation 8 and the $e^{-2U/R}$ of Barton (1995).
% UNVERIFIED: SC 1998 equation number taken from Buffalo & Kern 2024 Supplementary Eqs 34-35; SC 1998 body not read.

\subsection*{C.3\quad Closed form under the linear recombination model}

The linear map $r(\ell) \approx \ell$ gives $f_\text{L}(\ell) = (1-Z+\ell Z)^{-2}$ and $F_\text{L}(D) = D/[(1-Z)(1-Z+DZ)]$. The mid-chromosome profile is Buffalo and Kern's (2024) Supplementary Equation 33; averaging Equation~\eqref{eq:C_profile} over all neutral positions gives
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_\text{L} = \frac{2}{M^2(1-Z)}\int_0^M \frac{y}{1-Z+yZ}\,dy = \frac{2}{MZ(1-Z)} - \frac{2}{M^2Z^2}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{MZ}{1-Z}\right).
\label{eq:closed_form}
\end{equation}
Its leading term is the Santiago and Caballero (1998) result above. Expanding the logarithm gives $\overline{Q^2}_\text{L} = (1-Z)^{-2} - \frac{2}{3}MZ(1-Z)^{-3} + O(M^2)$. The $O(M)$ term is the same under the Haldane map, so the two agree to first order in $M$. As $M \to \infty$, $\overline{Q^2}_\text{L} \to 0$ rather than to the unlinked value: the linear map lets recombination exceed $1/2$, and for $\ell > 1$ it gives a negative survival probability.

\subsection*{C.4\quad The linear form is a lower bound}

Because $e^{-2\ell} > 1 - 2\ell$ for all $\ell > 0$, the Haldane recombination fraction is always below the linear one, $r_\text{H}(\ell) < \ell$. For $Z > 0$ the linear map therefore underestimates the survival of every association, so $f_\text{L} < f_\text{H}$ pointwise and
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_\text{L} < \overline{Q^2}_\text{H} \qquad \text{for all } M > 0.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:closed_form} is a lower bound on interference and hence on the reduction in $N_e$. The gap grows with $M$ and with $1-Z$: $\overline{Q^2}_\text{L}/\overline{Q^2}_\text{H}$ is 0.99, 0.95 and 0.91 at $M = 0.1$, 1 and 3 Morgans for $Z = 0.99$, and 0.99, 0.85 and 0.68 for $Z = 0.9$. All predictions in the main text use the Haldane form, Equation~\eqref{eq:C_haldane_avg}, evaluated by quadrature, which agrees with Equation~\eqref{eq:C_closed_haldane}.

\section{Appendix D: Stationary Equilibrium and Implied Selection Coefficients}\label{app:msdbapp}

This appendix states the variance in fitness that recurrent deleterious mutation maintains at mutation--selection--drift balance, and inverts it to the selection coefficient a measured $V_A$ would require. Every result in it is established; we collect them in the notation of the main text and cite their sources. What is ours is the application to wild-vertebrate $V_A$ in total fitness. The comparison itself has a direct precedent: Charlesworth (2015) set observed additive variance in \emph{Drosophila} fitness components against the mutation--selection prediction and concluded that there is too much of it to be explained by mutation--selection balance (see also Johnson and Barton 2005).

\subsection*{D.1\quad Stationary variance}

Let each of $U_d$ new deleterious mutations per diploid genome per generation have heterozygous effect $s$ on relative fitness (fitnesses $1$, $1-s$, $1-2s$), so its average effect is $s$ and a site at frequency $q$ contributes $2q(1-q)s^2$ to $V_A$. A new mutation enters at $q = 1/(2N)$, so the influx of variance is $V_m = U_d s^2$ (Charlesworth 2015, Equation 1). Selection removes a rare allele's variance at rate $s$ and drift removes heterozygosity at rate $1/(2N_e)$, so a flux balance gives
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx \frac{V_m}{s + 1/(2N_e)} = \frac{2N_eU_ds^2}{1+2N_es}.
\label{eq:D_VA_interp}
\end{equation}
This interpolates between the mutation--drift limit $V_A = 2N_eV_m$ for $N_es \ll 1$ (Lynch and Hill 1986) and the deterministic rare-allele balance $V_A = U_ds$, $q = \mu/s$, for $N_es \gg 1$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Charlesworth 2012). It has the same form as the stochastic house-of-cards variance of Bürger et al. (1989).

Equation~\eqref{eq:D_VA_interp} is a heuristic, not the diffusion result. Drift does not change the expected allele frequency, and under irreversible mutation $\mathbb{E}[q]$ has no stationary value, so the balance has to be written for expected heterozygosity. The diffusion solution for a semidominant deleterious allele (Kimura 1969) gives, per site, $\mathbb{E}[H] = 8N_e\mu\left[1/\beta - 1/(e^\beta - 1)\right]$ with $\beta = 4N_es$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es}-1}\right].
\label{eq:D_VA_exact}
\end{equation}
% UNVERIFIED: equation number in Kimura (1969) not checked; formula confirmed by quadrature of the sojourn density and a Wright-Fisher simulation (2Ns = 2).
Both expressions share their two limits. Between them Equation~\eqref{eq:D_VA_interp} is low by up to 29\%, at $4N_es \approx 3$. Our data lie deep in the strong-selection limit ($2N_es^* = 9{,}400$ to $390{,}000$ where $N_e = \pi/4\mu$ is available), where the two agree to better than one part in $10^3$.

\subsection*{D.2\quad The implied selection coefficient}

In the strong-selection limit, Equation~\eqref{eq:D_VA_exact} inverts to
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{V_A}{U_d}.
\label{eq:sHoC}
\end{equation}
With $U_d = 4\mu L_\text{coding}$ (Methods) and the direct mutation-rate estimates, $U_d = 0.34$--$1.26$ across the 18 populations with an annotated genome. The posterior modes of $V_A$ then give $s^* = 0.006$--$0.78$, median 0.16, and $P(s^* > 0.01) > 0.89$ in 17 of the 18 posteriors (0.71 in the snow vole). The limit is self-consistent: $2N_es^*$ is $9{,}400$ or more in every population where $N_e$ can be computed. The weak-selection branch fails its own premise. $N_es \ll 1$ with $V_A \approx 2N_eU_ds^2$ requires $N_e \ll 2U_d/V_A$, about 10 for the median population.

Against a mean heterozygous effect of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ in vertebrate DFEs (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017), $s^*$ is one to two orders of magnitude too large. Read forward, $V_A = U_ds$ at $s = 10^{-3}$, $5 \times 10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$ falls short of the measured median $V_A$ by factors of 160, 32 and 16.
% UNVERIFIED: whether Huber et al. 2017 and Kim et al. 2017 report s as the homozygous effect (h = 0.5); if so the heterozygous mean is s/2, which widens the gap.

\paragraph{Persistence of associations.} With uniform $s$, the fraction of standing variance that persists per generation, $Z = 1 - V_m/V_A$, is $1 - s$ in the strong-selection limit ($1 - s - 1/(2N_e)$ with drift). The per-generation persistence $(1-s)(1-r)$ of an association between a neutral site and a deleterious allele is that of Nordborg et al. (1996) and Charlesworth (2012, Appendix). Under a distribution of effects, $V_m/V_A = \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s] = \bar{s}(1 + \mathrm{CV}_s^2)$, so $Z = 1 - \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s]$. That is well below $1 - \bar{s}$ for leptokurtic DFEs: $1 - 6\bar{s}$ for a gamma DFE of shape 0.2.

\subsection*{D.3\quad Distributions of fitness effects}

Let effects follow a density $\phi(t)$, with $t$ the heterozygous effect. In the strong-selection limit each class contributes $U_d\,t$, so
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d \int_0^\infty \phi(t)\,t\,dt = U_d\,\bar{t}, \qquad \bar{t}^* = \frac{V_A}{U_d},
\label{eq:VA_DFE}
\end{equation}
whatever the shape of the DFE. The inversion of Equation~\eqref{eq:sHoC} therefore applies to its arithmetic mean. Shape enters only through nearly neutral mutations ($4N_et \lesssim 1$), which contribute less than $U_dt$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:D_VA_exact} applied class by class). A more leptokurtic DFE with the same mean puts more mass there, which lowers the sustainable $V_A$ and widens the gap. Two cautions apply. DFEs inferred from site-frequency spectra do not constrain the strongly deleterious tail, so their mean is partly an extrapolation. And the variance-weighted mean $\mathbb{E}[t^2]/\mathbb{E}[t]$, not $\bar{t}$, governs $V_m/V_A$ and $Z$.

\paragraph{Forward prediction.} Summing Equation~\eqref{eq:D_VA_exact} over a discretised DFE gives the variance a given DFE can sustain:
\begin{equation}
    V_A^{\text{DFE}} = U_d \sum_i f_i\, \bar{t}_i\left[1 - \frac{4N_e\bar{t}_i}{e^{4N_e\bar{t}_i}-1}\right],
\label{eq:VA_forward}
\end{equation}
with $f_i$ the fraction of mutations in class $i$ and $\bar{t}_i$ its mean. With vertebrate-calibrated classes (Rettelbach et al. 2025) this yields $V_A^{\text{DFE}} \approx$ [XX].
% UNVERIFIED: Rettelbach et al. 2025 not located; possibly Rettelbach et al. 2019 (Genetics, collared flycatcher). Check before citing.

\paragraph{Unlinked interference at large $s$.} Strongly deleterious alleles are purged within a few generations, so their associations with unlinked neutral sites decay faster than by Mendelian segregation alone: $Q(t) = 2/(1+t)$ in place of 2. Following Charlesworth (2012, Appendix) and Santiago and Caballero (1998), the unlinked term becomes
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N}{N_e} = 1 + 4\kappa\,U_d\,\bar{t}_\text{eff}, \qquad \bar{t}_\text{eff} = \int_0^\infty \phi(t)\,\frac{t}{(1+t)^2}\,dt < \bar{t},
\label{eq:B_corrected}
\end{equation}
which replaces $4\kappa V_A = 4\kappa U_d\bar{t}$. This is the $Z = 1 - t$ counterpart of the $G = 1 - V_A$ sensitivity in Appendix A, and it acts in the same direction. At the implied $\bar{t}^*$, the factor $1/(1+\bar{t}^*)^2$ is 0.31--0.99 (median 0.74). The diversity predictions of the main text, which take $Q = 2$, therefore overstate the unlinked reduction at the selection coefficients the measured $V_A$ would require.
