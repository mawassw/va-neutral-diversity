\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The quantity and its timescale}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity throughout is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness $w$, $V_A(w)$, shortened to $V_A$. Equivalently, it is the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive success, standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). Each model we use writes this quantity in its own terms, so we first set out how it appears in each. For its effect on neutral diversity, under random mating it is the $C^2$ of Santiago and Caballero (1995), and with pair-mated families their $C^2$ is the variance of family fitness, $2V_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}). In the multiplicative model of Barton and Etheridge (2011), it is the variance of the breeding value $z$ for log-fitness, where expected fitness is proportional to $e^{z}$. The two models agree while $V_A$ is small (\autoref{sec:onemodel}). In the maintenance model it is the same variance, summed over sites segregating for semidominant deleterious alleles as $V_A = \sum_j 2q_j(1-q_j)s_j^2$, where $q_j$ is the deleterious allele's frequency at site $j$, set by MSDB, and $s_j$ its heterozygous effect on relative fitness (\autoref{sec:msdb}). Bonnet et al. (2022) back-transformed their estimates to the observed scale of relative fitness with the multivariate \textit{delta} method, $V_A = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive genetic covariance matrix of the model's latent predictors and $\mathbf{d}$ the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to them, so the estimates need no rescaling.

The same variance describes selection at two levels, which quantitative and population genetics name differently. On phenotypes it is directional selection: by the secondary theorem of selection (Robertson 1966; Price 1970), each generation it changes the mean breeding value of fitness by $V_A$, and of every trait that covaries genetically with fitness by that covariance. On alleles it says only that those raising fitness increase in frequency. At MSDB these are the alleles free of new deleterious mutations, so selection is purifying at every locus, and the change it makes in each breeding value is undone by new mutation (\autoref{sec:msdb}). Directional selection on a phenotype therefore implies no positive selection on any allele. We use \emph{directional} only for phenotypes, and \emph{purifying} and \emph{positive} for selection against an allele and toward its fixation. The two levels decide what each test assumes. The global reduction in effective size depends on the heritable variance in family size, and on the alleles only through the retention $Z$ and two requirements: that their effects be fixed, and that their frequencies change little while an association persists (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). MSDB meets both, and it also holds $V_A$ constant over the coalescent window, which is the stationarity that the diversity test assumes and the maintenance test examines. The maintenance test and the local reduction at linked sites depend on the alleles, which we take to be at MSDB (\autoref{sec:msdb}, \autoref{sec:linkage}).

Both tests require the long-term value of this variance, and each integrates it over a span far longer than a pedigree. Neutral diversity, $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$, where $\mu$ is the mutation rate per site, measures the coalescent effective size $N_{eC}$ and reflects genealogies spanning around $4N_e$ generations. Santiago and Caballero's result gives the per-generation variance effective size $N_{eV}$, and the two coincide only at long-term demographic and selective equilibrium. MSDB describes a stationary variance, carried by deleterious alleles that selection turns over in about $1/s$ generations (\autoref{sec:msdb}). In the models we build on, the two conditions are coupled. Santiago and Caballero (1995) assume an equilibrium level of $V_A$, and models of background selection take the variance itself from MSDB and infer its inputs from diversity (Buffalo and Kern 2024; Murphy et al. 2023). The effect of $V_A$ on diversity and its maintenance are then one calculation. We separate them, because the amount of $V_A$ is measured rather than derived, and whether MSDB could maintain it is tested rather than assumed. A pedigree estimate, however, covers the cohorts of one study, a few decades, in the environments they met. That scope is built into the estimator: $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, so the same $\mathbf{G}$ in another environment gives another $V_A$. We therefore write the long-term variance as
% UNVERIFIED: Murphy et al. 2023 (Murphy, Elyashiv, Amster and Sella, eLife?) not Crossref-checked and not in references.bib.
\begin{equation}
    \bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is unknown.

Biologically, $c$ is how representative the study period is of the population's evolutionary past. A value above one means the study's environments dampened the expression of heritable fitness differences, or the estimate is too low, and a value below one means they amplified it, or the estimate is inflated. What we can compute is the value of $c$ each model requires, the factor by which the pedigree estimate must be multiplied for the model to reproduce the data. A required value other than one means either that the study period is unrepresentative or that the model leaves out processes acting in the population, and we cannot tell which based on a single test. Maintenance sets an upper limit on $c$, which below one is the share of the estimated variance that recurrent mutation can maintain, while the rest must be maintained by other processes or simply does not persist. Diversity sets a lower limit, which above one is the factor by which the long-term variance must exceed the estimate to explain the missing diversity; if the long-term variance is smaller, the rest of the deficit has other causes. Because a larger $\bar{V}_A$ removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, the two limits move in opposite directions. A population has only one $c$, so if the lower limit lies above the upper one, no degree of representativeness reconciles them, and the gap measures what the models leave out. The quantities of interest are therefore the values of $c$ that each model requires, and their range across the tested species.

Throughout, $N_e/N$ denotes the reduction in effective size caused by heritable variance in fitness alone. The reference is a population of $N$ breeding adults whose family sizes follow a Poisson distribution, the neutral Wright--Fisher baseline, for which $N_e = N$. Both diversity models share this reference, and their $N_e$ is the per-generation $N_{eV}$. The demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$, estimated from variance in family size, the sex ratio and fluctuations in census size, is typically 0.1--0.5 (Frankham 1995), and counts every source of reproductive variance, heritable or not. The diversity ratio, $N_{eC}/N$, is usually orders of magnitude smaller (Buffalo 2021). Our $N_e/N$ predicts it only if heritable variance in fitness is the only departure from the Poisson baseline, the population is at long-term equilibrium, and the census counts breeding adults only. We assume a constant $N$ with an equal sex ratio and pairing at random with respect to breeding value. Appendices~A and B give the general forms, and Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation.

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{sec:msdb}
At MSDB, new deleterious mutations supply additive variance in fitness at the rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$ per generation, the mutational variance, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous effect on relative fitness. Each generation, selection removes a fraction $s$ of the standing variance $V_A$, and drift removes a fraction $1/(2N_e)$ (Charlesworth 2015). For a single effect $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant allele (Kimura 1969; Appendix~\ref{app:D_stationary}) gives the equilibrium variance
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right]. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
When selection is weak relative to drift ($N_es \ll 1$), MSDB reduces to the mutation--drift variance $2N_eU_ds^2$ (Lynch and Hill 1986). When it is strong,\footnote{We use strong and weak only for selection relative to drift. The rate $s$ at which selection removes a deleterious allele we call fast or slow purging, and we describe $V_A$ itself as small or large relative to one.} each deleterious allele is held near frequency $\mu/s$ and
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1) \label{eq:strong}
\end{equation}
(Mukai et al. 1972; Charlesworth 2012). Each new mutation lowers its carrier's fitness by $s$, so $U_d\,s$ is also the decline in mean breeding value for fitness that mutation causes each generation, and Equation~\eqref{eq:strong} states that selection removes as much as mutation adds. The bracketed term in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, so $U_d\,s$ is the most variance MSDB can maintain at any effective size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:strong} gives the deleterious effect that a long-term variance requires,
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}. \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
Charlesworth (2015) set the variance observed in fitness components of \emph{Drosophila} against this prediction (see also Johnson and Barton 2005). Here we apply it to total fitness, at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

Under a distribution of fitness effects (DFE), class $k$ receives a fraction $\varphi_k$ of new mutations and, when selection is strong, contributes $U_d\varphi_ks_k$. The total is $U_d\bar{s}$, so $s^*$ estimates the arithmetic mean $\bar{s}$ of the DFE, whatever its shape. Shape matters only through nearly neutral mutations ($4N_es \lesssim 1$), whose term in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} is below one, so at the same mean a DFE with more mutations of very small and of very large effect maintains less variance. We use the strong-selection limit throughout, for two reasons. Because $U_d\bar s$ is this maximum, setting weakly selected mutations aside can only overstate what MSDB maintains, and a finding that $\bar{V}_A$ is too large to be maintained is then conservative. And a variance carried mainly by weakly selected mutations is not self-consistent: it requires $N_e \ll U_d/(2V_A)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion}), an effective size of a few individuals at the measured variances (Methods). Inbreeding and population structure lower the effective size, but not to that scale.

\subsection{The global reduction in effective size}\label{sec:unlinked}
We first take a neutral site that assorts independently of every selected locus, under the assumptions of \autoref{sec:quantity}, and suppose that the association between the site and its selected background is eroded only by segregation. Follow a neutral gene copy carried by an ancestor whose breeding value for log-fitness exceeds the mean by $z - \bar{z}$, and count generations after the ancestor by $t$, with $t = 0$ the ancestor's generation. Each carrier of the copy receives half its breeding value from the parent that passed the copy on and half from a mate drawn at random, so the expected excess of the carrier in generation $t$ is $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$. Each generation, the number of copies is multiplied by its carrier's expected relative fitness, $1 + (z - \bar{z})/2^t$ for a small excess, so the ancestor's expected long-term contribution is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) \approx \prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\left[1 + \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2^t}\right] \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z}), \qquad Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2.
\end{equation}
$Q$ is Robertson's cumulative transmission factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}: the ancestor's advantage counted over every generation in which it persists.

Drift at a neutral site is set by how much long-term contributions vary among ancestors (Wray and Thompson 1990), and inherited fitness differences add $Q^2V_A$ to that variation beyond the Poisson expectation (Robertson 1961; Nei and Murata 1966; Santiago and Caballero 1995, Equation 16; Woolliams et al. 1993; Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}). Hence, for an unlinked site with $Q = 2$ and random mating,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4V_A}, \label{unlinked_baseline}
\end{equation}
the special case of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}.

Selection also erodes an ancestor's advantage, by removing the carriers of deleterious alleles. We write $Z$ for the retention: the fraction of a neutral site copy's association with its selected background that survives one generation of selection. With mates whose breeding values are correlated by $r_{mf}$, Santiago and Caballero's (1995) factor is
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}. \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian breeding value built from many loci of small effect, under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$ exactly, the case above (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). For a deleterious allele, by contrast, the association is lost with the carriers that selection removes (Nordborg et al. 1996; Charlesworth 2012, Appendix), so when selection is strong each effect class $k$ is retained at
\begin{equation}
    Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
whatever the size of $V_A$, and its unlinked factor is $Q = 2/(1 + s_k) < 2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}). Under MSDB, $Q = 2$ is therefore an upper bound (sweeping beneficial alleles would give $Z > 1$), and the process that maintains $V_A$ affects the reduction at unlinked sites only through $Z$. We compute the global reduction at $Z = 1$, its upper bound. The retention $Z_k$ is used in the local reduction of \autoref{sec:linkage}.

The reduction requires fixed additive effects: each selected allele changes fitness by an amount that does not depend on its own frequency. It does not hold under underdominance, stabilizing selection or frequency-dependent selection, where an allele's effect on fitness changes with its frequency. For fixed additive effects, two timescales that differ by orders of magnitude are involved. An ancestor's expected contribution is settled within a few generations, because the series for $Q$ is dominated by its first terms (Barton and Etheridge 2011), so the sign of a lineage's advantage needs to persist for only about two generations. Inherited advantages therefore increase drift at MSDB, while beneficial alleles sweep, and under fluctuating selection that is positively autocorrelated across generations. Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} gives the size of that increase only under two further conditions, however. The retention must be the same from one generation to the next (Santiago and Caballero 1995), and allele frequencies at the selected loci must change little during the generations an association persists (Barton 2000). The infinitesimal model on which Santiago and Caballero's general result rests meets the second condition for any kind of selection, and so does MSDB at unlinked sites, where each deleterious allele is held near its equilibrium frequency and an association lasts about two generations. When alleles sweep between extreme frequencies, the drift they cause depends on the rate of sweeps relative to recombination and can be much larger than the variance in fitness indicates (Barton 2000), so the formula understates it. Neutral diversity, in contrast, accumulates the drift these contributions cause over the coalescent window of \autoref{sec:quantity}. If $V_A$ changes over time, each generation contributes its own $N/N_e(t) = 1 + 4V_A(t)$, and diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$ (Wright 1938). Over $T$ generations,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t), \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
so diversity responds to $\bar{V}_A$, the target of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}, and, at a constant retention, to nothing else about the trajectory of $V_A$. If $N$ also varies, the same holds for its harmonic mean when $V_A(t)$ and $1/N(t)$ are uncorrelated.

Every neutral site shares this reduction, because a site is exposed to variance in family size through the individual that carries it, not through the haplotype on which it sits. We call the resulting effective size the global effective size. At MSDB it is also what background selection becomes at unlinked loci (Appendix~\ref{app:D_bgs}).

\subsection{Separate sexes and the mating system}\label{sec:mating}
Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} treats the population as hermaphrodite. With separate sexes, Santiago and Caballero (1995) combine the sexes as $1/N_e = 1/(4N_{e,m}) + 1/(4N_{e,f})$ (after Crow and Denniston 1988), which at an equal sex ratio averages the selective variance over the sexes rather than adding it. What sets a parent's family size, however, depends on the mating system. Under random mating, each offspring's two parents are drawn independently, and a parent's family size depends on its own breeding value alone. Under social monogamy the nest is the unit of productivity, so a parent's family size also depends on its social partner's breeding value. Santiago and Caballero (1995) treat both regimes, and under strict pair-mating the variance of family fitness doubles, to $C^2 = 2V_A$. In socially monogamous birds, extra-pair paternity moves a fraction $\alpha$ of a nest's offspring to other sires without changing how many offspring the nest raises. Applying the expected long-term contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva (1999) to this mating system (Appendix~B) gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}, \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is common to both sexes, as Bonnet et al. (2022) estimate it, and $\kappa(\alpha)$ is a mating-system scalar. Under random mating $\kappa = 1$. Under social monogamy, we take a male's extra-pair success to be proportional to his breeding value, so that it weighs on his breeding success as much as a female's does on hers, as a single variance for both sexes requires. Then
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2. \label{eq:kappaB}
\end{equation}
Without extra-pair paternity, social and genetic monogamy coincide: both partners share every offspring of the nest, and $\kappa(0) = 2$ recovers Santiago and Caballero's pair-mated families. As extra-pair paternity rises, a male's success depends more on his own breeding value and less on his partner's, and $\kappa$ drops below 2. Superb fairy-wrens approach the other extreme: most nestlings are sired by males from outside the group, who provide no care, while the social male raises the brood (Mulder et al. 1994; Hajduk et al. 2018). Even when every nestling is sired by an extra-pair male, $\kappa(1) = 9/8$ still exceeds 1, because the social male's breeding value still affects how many offspring the nest raises, so a female's family size still depends on her partner. If extra-pair siring is instead independent of male breeding value, $\kappa$ is lower; we use that alternative as a sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}).

\subsection{The multiplicative model and its bound}\label{sec:onemodel}
Equations~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} and \eqref{unlinked_mating}, which we call the small-variance approximation, treat each lineage's advantage as small, so they hold only while $V_A \ll 1$, because the product of per-generation fitnesses in $R(z)$ was approximated by a sum, which is accurate only for small advantages. Larger variances require setting a specific model of fitness, which the pedigree estimates already supply. The animal model of Bonnet et al. (2022) is multiplicative, with expected breeding success exponential in a Gaussian breeding value, so the reported $V_A$ is the variance of $z$ with $w \propto e^{z}$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}; de Villemereuil et al. 2016, Equations 15--16).

Barton and Etheridge (2011) solved this model for parents chosen in proportion to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. On the log scale a lineage's per-generation advantages add exactly, so an ancestor's expected long-term contribution is $R(z) = \exp[2(z - \bar{z}) - 2V_A]$, with $Q = 2$ now in an exponent. Its second moment across ancestors is $e^{4V_A}$, and taking the inbreeding effective size from that second moment (Wray and Thompson 1990) gives, under random mating, the $e^{4V_A}$ increase in the rate of coalescence found by Weissman and Barton (2012),
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
For small $V_A$ this approximates to $1 - 4V_A$, the linear approximation of Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline}, and as $V_A$ grows Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} gives the larger reduction. Under strict monogamy, with pairs formed at random and offspring number in proportion to $e^{z + z'}$, the effect doubles to $e^{-8V_A}$ (Weissman and Barton 2012), the counterpart of the pair-mated $C^2 = 2V_A$ of Santiago and Caballero (1995). Their model has neither separate sexes nor extra-pair paternity, and with both the reduction lies between two exponentials (Appendix~B, Equation~\eqref{eq:B_mult_pair}),
\begin{equation}
   e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa(\alpha) V_A}, \label{eq:ne_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_\text{max}$ is the largest value $\kappa$ takes in a mating system: 2 under social monogamy, reached without extra-pair paternity, and 1 under random mating. The two ends meet under strict monogamy. The right-hand end agrees with Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} while $V_A$ is small, and the left-hand end, set by the offspring of the carrier's own nest, is the largest reduction the model allows.

The multiplicative result rests on the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness: a 100-fold reduction is carried by ancestors more than four standard deviations from the mean (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}). Any limit on fitness cuts that tail off, whether a biotic limit on territory or trade-offs in clutch size or the bounded probability of breeding as in Bonnet et al.'s zero-inflation component, whose variance we treat as log-additive. We therefore treat $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$ as an upper bound on the reduction in neutral diversity that a given $V_A$ can produce, and call it the multiplicative bound. It is the case most favorable to explaining a loss of diversity. It also covers a variance that fluctuates: because the reduction accelerates with $V_A$, a fluctuating variance removes at least as much diversity as a constant one with the same mean (by Jensen's inequality). We note that an additive fitness map, $w = 1 + a$, is not a valid model because it gives negative fitness to a growing share of individuals as $V_A$ approaches one (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}).

\subsection{The variance needed to explain a loss of diversity}\label{sec:ceiling}
Inverting the multiplicative bound gives the smallest variance that could account for a given loss of neutral diversity. Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
be the diversity ratio of \autoref{sec:quantity}. At equilibrium, setting the bound equal to $\delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\delta} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}}. \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
Because the bound overestimates the reduction, a population whose $V_A$ is less than $V_{A,\delta}$ cannot owe its missing diversity to heritable variance in fitness at unlinked sites. $V_{A,\delta}$ grows only logarithmically as diversity decays. Each further tenfold loss of diversity requires only $\ln(10)/(4\kappa_\text{max}) \approx 0.58/\kappa_\text{max}$ more variance, and strict monogamy halves it. It depends only on $\delta$ and the mating system, so any population with a diversity estimate can be mapped against it. For a population that retains at least $1/(1 + 4\kappa)$ of its neutral expectation, about a fifth under random mating and a ninth under strict monogamy, the small-variance approximation also gives a ceiling in the strict sense, $V_{A,\max}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax}, Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}): a measured variance above it would remove more diversity than is observed. However, for the deficits of wild vertebrates it would require $V_A > 1$, outside the range in which it holds. A population with $\delta \ge 1$ fails the equilibrium assumptions and is excluded from our analysis (Methods).

\subsection{Local effective size at linked sites}\label{sec:linkage}
When a neutral site is physically linked to selected loci, recombination breaks associations only partially, so they last longer than the two generations of the global reduction and depress a local effective size that varies along the genome. The global reduction acts through the individual that carries the neutral allele, the local one through the chromosome segment that carries it. The local reduction needs two inputs: how much variance each chromosome carries, and how long its associations last. Background selection models take both from MSDB (\autoref{sec:quantity}). In our case, the pedigree estimate supplies the amount: chromosome $i$ carries $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is its share of the genome's functional sequence. How long an association lasts cannot be measured from a pedigree, so it is the only parameter that requires the MSDB assumption, through the retention $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), with classes weighted by the share of $V_A$ each carries at equilibrium, $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}; Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). The local effective size, unlike the global one, therefore depends on the process that maintains $V_A$. Because $Z_k$ does not depend on $V_A$ under the strong selection limit, using $\hat{V}_A$ changes the size of the local reduction but not its form, and when $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds what MSDB can maintain (i.e., $s^* > \bar{s}$), it gives the larger reduction. Otherwise, setting $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$ instead recovers the prediction of MSDB alone (\autoref{sec:conditions}). The expressions below are written for one class with retention $Z$.

Pedigree linkage maps give the length of each chromosome far better than the variation in recombination along it, so we treat the chromosome as the unit. The neutral site and the selected loci are placed uniformly along chromosome $i$, of map length $M_i$ Morgans, and the reduction is averaged over their positions. On a whole chromosome, pairs of sites lie up to $M_i$ apart, often more than 0.5 M, where a linear map, $r = \ell$, would give recombination fractions above $\tfrac12$. We therefore use Haldane's mapping function, $r(\ell) = \tfrac12(1 - e^{-2\ell})$, which approaches $\tfrac12$ at large distances. Additionally, it assumes no crossover interference, which raises recombination at a given map distance, so Haldane's function skews toward a larger local reduction, the direction most favorable to explaining a loss of diversity.

An association between a neutral site and a selected locus $\ell$ Morgans away survives one generation of recombination and selection with probability $\rho = (1 - r(\ell))Z = \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})$. This is the linked counterpart of the factor $\tfrac12$ in $Q$ (\autoref{sec:unlinked}): the weight with which the chromosome segment carrying the neutral allele keeps its selected background each generation. The segment's cumulative advantage is therefore $1/(1-\rho)$, which returns $Q = 2$ at $r = \tfrac12$ and $Z = 1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}). Squaring it and averaging over positions gives (Appendix~C)
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_i = \frac{2}{M_i^2}\int_0^{M_i} (M_i - \ell)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})}\right)^2 d\ell. \label{eq:haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
The factor inside the integral is the one familiar from background selection theory: at $Z = 1 - s$, it is $1/[s + r(1-s)]$ (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996; Santiago and Caballero 1998, 2016; Buffalo and Kern 2024). Buffalo and Kern (2024) evaluate it at each position along a fine-scale recombination map, whereas averaging over positions gives the expected reduction on a chromosome when only its map length is known. The local reduction decreases with map length and with the rate of purging, $1 - Z$. Appendix~C gives Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} in closed form (Appendix~\ref{app:C_closed}), its limits (Appendix~\ref{app:C_limits}), and the effect of clustering selected loci in regions of low recombination (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}).

A neutral allele is linked in cis only to the selected loci on its own haplotype, because associations with the homologous chromosome are broken at meiosis (Buffalo and Kern 2024), so of the variance $v_i$ the focal haplotype carries $v_h = v_i/2$. A social partner's breeding value affects the carrier's family size but is never on the focal haplotype, so only the carrier's own breeding value counts in cis. We write $\kappa_\text{cis}$ for its weight in the carrier's family size and $\kappa_\text{own}$ for its share of $\kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}$. Both are 1 under the mating model of \autoref{sec:mating}, under both random mating and social monogamy (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}). Treating selected loci as independent along the map, so that their effects multiply (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Santiago and Caballero 2016), the local reduction for chromosome $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right). \label{eq:ne_chrom}
\end{equation}

The rest of the genome, the homologous haplotype and the other chromosomes, assorts independently of the focal site and contributes the global reduction at the remaining variance, $\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h$; the partner's share is unaffected. Fitness is multiplicative across loci (\autoref{sec:onemodel}), so the two factors combine in one exponential (Buffalo and Kern 2024, Supplementary Equations 28--29; cf.\ Santiago and Caballero 1998):
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\left\{-\left[\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h + Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)\right]\right\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
where $Q = 2/(2 - Z)$ is Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} at the same retention as the local factor, and $Q^2$ is the limit of $\overline{Q^2}_i$ as $M_i \to \infty$. For the multiplicative bound, $\kappa_\text{max}$ replaces $\kappa$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega} gives the small-variance approximation).

The local factor is $\exp(-\kappa_\text{cis}\overline{Q^2}_iv_h)$ and the global factor $\exp[-Q^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)]$. The cis variance moves from the global factor to the local one, so nothing is counted twice, and on a freely recombining chromosome the two reduce to the global reduction $e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). By linked selection we mean the local factor only. Relative to the global reduction at the same retention, it is
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right], \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
which does not exceed one, because $\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ in every effect class and $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). With neutral sites spread uniformly over the physical genome, the genome-wide values are averages over the $n$ autosomes weighted by physical length $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i. \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}
Setting Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} equal to $\delta$ gives the variance that would account for a diversity ratio with linkage included. Because $\overline{\Omega}$ itself depends on $V_A$, it is found numerically (Methods). Since $\overline{\Omega} \le 1$, that variance is at most the unlinked value at the same retention, $\ln(1/\delta)/(Q^2\kappa)$, which is $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}) at $Z = 1$ with $\kappa_\text{max}$.

\subsection{The two tests as one condition}\label{sec:conditions}
On the scale of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}, the two limits on $c$ of \autoref{sec:quantity} take a closed form. MSDB maintains at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}), so the maintenance limit is
\begin{equation}
c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
Heritable variance in fitness accounts for a deficit at unlinked sites only if $\bar{V}_A$ reaches $V_{A,\delta}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}), so the diversity limit is
\begin{equation}
c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\delta}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
A single $c$ satisfies both only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, that is, if
\begin{equation}
\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat} contains no pedigree estimate of $V_A$. A variance maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation can reduce diversity at unlinked sites to no less than $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}$ of its neutral expectation, even under the multiplicative bound. Whether the two tests can be reconciled is therefore settled by the mutational parameters before any estimate of $V_A$ is fitted, and the pedigree estimates place each population relative to the two thresholds, at $c = 1$. With linkage, the linked variance of \autoref{sec:linkage} replaces $V_{A,\delta}$, and the condition is evaluated numerically (Methods).

\subsection{Direction of the approximations}\label{sec:approx}
Most of the approximations here are biased toward an optimistic prediction, thereby establishing a conservative threshold for each of the tests. On the diversity side, each approximation overestimates the reduction in effective size: the multiplicative bound in place of a reduction that places a limit on high fitness (\autoref{sec:onemodel}); $\kappa_\text{max}$ in place of $\kappa$ under social monogamy; $Q = 2$ in place of the smaller $Q$ of purged deleterious alleles (\autoref{sec:unlinked}); the attribution of all non-Poisson variance in family size to $V_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}); Haldane's mapping function in place of one with that accounts for crossover interference; and, in the local factor, the measured variance in place of what MSDB maintains, spread uniformly along the map for loci placed independently of recombination rate (\autoref{sec:linkage}). A finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too small to explain a deficit is therefore conservative. On the maintenance side, the strong-selection limit overestimates what MSDB maintains (\autoref{sec:msdb}), so $s^*$ is the smallest mean effect that could maintain $\bar{V}_A$, and a finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too large to be maintained is conservative too. The mutational target is the exception: a larger $U_d$ lowers $s^*$ in proportion, so we use two available estimates of the mutation rate in our analysis (Methods).
