\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The quantity and its timescale}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$: the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive success, standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). Without a pair bond it is the $C^2$ of Santiago and Caballero (1995); with pair-mated families their $C^2$ is the variance of family fitness, $2V_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}). Bonnet et al. (2022) back-transformed their estimates to this scale as $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive genetic covariance matrix of the model's latent predictors and $\mathbf{d}$ the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to them, so the estimates are used without rescaling.

Both comparisons need the long-term value of this variance. Neutral diversity measures the coalescent effective size, $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$, and so reflects genealogies spanning some $4N_e$ generations. Santiago and Caballero's result gives the variance effective size $N_{eV}$, the rate of drift per generation. The two coincide at long-term demographic and selective equilibrium, and that equality is what lets neutral diversity constrain $V_A$. Mutation--selection--drift balance likewise describes a variance that is constant over time. A pedigree estimate describes something narrower, and the difference is built into the estimator: $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, so the same $\mathbf{G}$ in another environment gives another $V_A(w)$. We therefore write the long-term variance as
\begin{equation}
\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is unknown. The two comparisons respond to $c$ in opposite directions, because a larger $\bar{V}_A$ removes more diversity and is harder to maintain. The diversity comparison also assumes that $\bar{V}_A$ is sustained over the coalescent window, and whether recurrent mutation can sustain it is exactly what the maintenance comparison tests. Therefore, what we are truly interested in is the distribution of $c$ and its range across the tested species.

Throughout, $N_e/N$ denotes the reduction caused by heritable variance in fitness alone, relative to $N$ breeding adults whose family sizes would otherwise be Poisson. It is neither the demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$, typically 0.1--0.5, nor the diversity ratio $\delta$ of \autoref{sec:ceiling}, although at equilibrium it is the model's prediction for $\delta$. We assume a constant $N$ with an equal sex ratio and pairing at random with respect to breeding value ($r_{mf} = 0$); Appendices~A and B give the general forms, and Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation.

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{msdb}
New deleterious mutations add additive variance at rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous effect on relative fitness. Selection removes that variance at rate $s$ per generation and drift at rate $1/(2N_e)$ (Charlesworth 2015). For a single effect $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant allele (Kimura 1969; Appendix~D) gives the equilibrium variance
\begin{equation}
V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right]. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
Where selection is weak relative to drift ($N_es \ll 1$), this is the mutation--drift variance $2N_eU_ds^2$ (Lynch and Hill 1986). Where it is strong,\footnote{We use strong and weak only for selection relative to drift. The rate $s$ at which selection removes a deleterious allele we call fast or slow purging, and we describe $V_A$ itself as small or large relative to one.} each deleterious allele is held near frequency $\mu/s$ and
\begin{equation}
V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1) \label{eq:HoC}
\end{equation}
(Mukai et al. 1972; Charlesworth 2012). The bracket in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, so $U_d\,s$ is the most variance the balance can maintain at any effective size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:HoC} gives the mean effect that a long-term variance requires,
\begin{equation}
s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}. \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
Charlesworth (2015) made this comparison for fitness components in \emph{Drosophila} (see also Johnson and Barton 2005); we apply it to total fitness, at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

Because every class of mutation contributes $U_d\,s$ when selection is strong, $s^*$ estimates the arithmetic mean $\bar{s}$ of the distribution of fitness effects (DFE), whatever its shape. Shape matters only through nearly neutral mutations ($4N_es \lesssim 1$), which contribute less than $U_d\,s$. A DFE with more mutations of very small and of very large effect at the same mean therefore maintains less variance, not more. The weak-selection branch would require an effective size of a few individuals (Appendix~D), so in outbred populations Equations~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} and \eqref{eq:HoC} coincide.

\subsection{The global reduction in effective size}\label{sec:unlinked}
Setting $Q = 2$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} gives the reduction at a neutral site that assorts independently of every selected locus. An ancestor whose breeding value exceeds the mean passes the excess to its offspring, half of it to its grand-offspring, and so on. Its expected long-term contribution is therefore, for a small excess, $R(z) \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$, where $z$ is the breeding value for log-fitness (\autoref{sec:onemodel}) and
\begin{equation}
Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2.
\end{equation}
Drift at a neutral site is set by how much long-term contributions vary among ancestors (Wray and Thompson 1990), and inherited fitness differences add $Q^2V_A$ to that variation beyond the Poisson expectation (Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}). Hence
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4V_A}, \label{unlinked_baseline}
\end{equation}
the result of Robertson (1961) and Santiago and Caballero (1995). Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} is exact under long-term equilibrium: it needs no assumption about how selection behaves from one generation to the next, only that the variance is the long-term equilibrium $\bar{V}_A$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}. It is approximate in another respect. It treats each lineage's advantage as small, so it holds while $V_A \ll 1$, and \autoref{sec:onemodel} gives the reduction beyond that.

Two timescales are involved, and they differ by orders of magnitude. An ancestor's expected contribution is settled within a few generations, because the series for $Q$ is dominated by its first terms (Barton and Etheridge 2011), so the equation needs the sign of a lineage's advantage to persist for only about two generations. It therefore holds under mutation--selection--drift balance, during directional sweeps, and under fluctuating selection that is positively autocorrelated across generations. Neutral diversity, in contrast, accumulates the drift these contributions cause over the $\sim 4N_e$ generations of a coalescence. If $V_A$ changes over time, each generation contributes its own $N/N_e(t) = 1 + 4V_A(t)$, and diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$ (Wright 1938). Over $T$ generations, with $4\kappa$ in place of 4 once the mating system is included (\autoref{sec:mating}),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t). \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
Because $N/N_e$ is linear in $V_A$, the harmonic mean passes the time average through unchanged. Diversity responds to $\bar{V}_A$, the target of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}, and to nothing else about the trajectory of $V_A$. If $N$ also varies, Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic} holds with $N$ replaced by its harmonic mean, provided that $V_A(t)$ is uncorrelated with $1/N(t)$ and the mating system does not change.

Segregation is not the only process that erodes an ancestor's advantage. Selection also removes the carriers of deleterious alleles. We write $Z$ for the retention: the fraction of a gene copy's association with its selected background that survives one generation of selection.\footnote{Santiago and Caballero (1995) write it $G$; it is $1 - \kappa$ in Buffalo and Kern (2024, Supplementary Equation 38). See Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}.} With retention $Z$, and mates whose breeding values are correlated by $r_{mf}$, Santiago and Caballero's (1995) factor is
\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}. \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian breeding value built from many loci of small effect, under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$ exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). For a deleterious allele, the association is lost with the carriers that selection removes (Nordborg et al. 1996; Charlesworth 2012, Appendix). When selection is strong, each effect class $k$ is therefore retained at
\begin{equation}
Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
whatever the size of $V_A$, and its unlinked factor is $Q = 2/(1 + s_k) < 2$ (Appendix~D). Under the maintenance model tested here, $Q = 2$ is an upper bound; rare beneficial alleles that are sweeping would give $Z > 1$. The process that maintains $V_A$ affects the reduction at unlinked sites only through $Z$.

Every neutral site shares the reduction of Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline}, because a site is exposed to variance in family size through the individual that carries it, not through the haplotype on which it sits. We call the resulting effective size the global effective size. Under mutation--selection--drift balance it is also what background selection becomes at unlinked loci (Appendix~D).

\subsection{Separate sexes and the mating system}\label{sec:mating}
Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} treats the population as hermaphrodite. With separate sexes, Santiago and Caballero (1995) combine the sexes as $1/N_e = 1/(4N_{e,m}) + 1/(4N_{e,f})$ (after Crow and Denniston 1988), which at an equal sex ratio averages the selective variance over the sexes rather than adding it. What sets a parent's family size depends on the mating system. Without a pair bond, each parent's own breeding value does. Under social monogamy the nest is the unit of productivity, so a parent's family size also depends on its social partner's breeding value. Extra-pair paternity at rate $\alpha$ moves paternity out of the nest without changing how many offspring the nest produces. Applying the expected long-term contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva (1999) to these mating systems (Appendix~B) gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}, \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is common to both sexes, as Bonnet et al. (2022) estimate it, and $\kappa(\alpha)$ is a mating-system scalar.\footnote{Not the $\kappa$ of Buffalo and Kern (2024, Supplementary Equations 19--20 and 38), which is $1 - Z$ here.} Without a pair bond $\kappa = 1$. Under social monogamy, when extra-pair gains are proportional to male breeding value and replace within-pair losses (Model B, used in all primary results),
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_B(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2, \label{eq:kappaB}
\end{equation}
which falls from 2 at $\alpha = 0$ to 9/8 at $\alpha = 1$. Model A, in which extra-pair siring is independent of male breeding value, serves as a sensitivity bound (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}). Under strict monogamy both models give $\kappa = 2$, the pair-mated model of Santiago and Caballero (1995): each nest pools two independent breeding values, so a given loss of diversity needs half the variance it needs without a pair bond. Both forms assume $Z = 1$.

\subsection{The multiplicative model and its bound}\label{sec:onemodel}
Equations~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} and \eqref{unlinked_mating} hold only while $V_A$ is small, and the pedigree estimate itself supplies the model for larger values. The animal model of Bonnet et al. (2022) is multiplicative. Breeding values are additive and Gaussian on a latent scale, expected breeding success is their exponential through a log link, and a separate logit link sets the probability of breeding at all. Expected relative fitness is $w = p\lambda$, with $p$ the probability of breeding (logit predictor $l_1$) and $\lambda$ the expected number of offspring given breeding (log predictor $l_2$), so $\ln w = \ln p + l_2$. Bonnet et al. report $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive covariance matrix of $l_1$ and $l_2$ and $\mathbf{d}$ holds the average derivatives of $w$ with respect to them. At mean relative fitness one these are, to first order, also the derivatives of $\ln w$, and for the log-linked component the observed and latent variances coincide exactly (de Villemereuil et al. 2016, Equations 15--16). The reported $V_A(w)$ is therefore the variance of the breeding value $z$ for log-fitness, $w \propto e^{z}$. The posterior draws available to us give only this combined variance, so all of it is treated as log-additive. Because $\ln p \le 0$, that overstates the reduction for large $V_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}). One model of fitness can therefore serve for both the estimate and the prediction.

Barton and Etheridge (2011) solved this model for parents chosen in proportion to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. The expected long-term contribution of an ancestor is $R(z) = \exp[2(z - \bar{z}) - 2V_A]$, so the factor $Q = 2$ now sits in an exponent. Its second moment across ancestors is $e^{4V_A}$ (its mean is one), and the probability of coalescence goes as the square of $R$. Taking the inbreeding effective size from the second moment of long-term contributions (Wray and Thompson 1990) gives, without a pair bond, the $e^{4V_A}$ increase in the rate of coalescence found by Weissman and Barton (2012),
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
For small $V_A$ this is $1 - 4V_A$, the same as Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline}, and as $V_A$ grows the multiplicative form gives the larger reduction. Under strict monogamy, with pairs formed at random and offspring in proportion to $e^{z + z'}$, Weissman and Barton (2012) showed that the effect doubles, to $e^{-8V_A}$, the counterpart of the pair-mated $C^2 = 2V_A$. Their model has no separate sexes or extra-pair paternity. With extra-pair paternity, a gene copy's long-term contribution combines offspring of its own nest with extra-pair offspring, and the reduction lies between two exponentials (Appendix~B, Equation~\eqref{eq:B_mult_pair}):
\begin{equation}
    e^{-4\kappa(0)V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa_B V_A}, \qquad \kappa(0) = 2. \label{eq:ne_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
The two ends meet under strict monogamy, at $e^{-8V_A}$. The right-hand end agrees with Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} while $V_A$ is small; the left-hand end, set by offspring of the carrier's own nest, is the largest reduction the model allows.

The multiplicative result rests on the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness. A 100-fold reduction is carried by ancestors about 4.3 standard deviations above the mean, roughly one in $10^5$, and a $10^4$-fold reduction by ancestors about 6.1 standard deviations out. Any ceiling on fitness cuts that tail off: limits on territory or clutch size, or the bounded probability of breeding in the zero-inflation component of Bonnet et al.'s model (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}). We therefore treat $e^{-4\kappa(0)V_A}$, with $\kappa(0) = 1$ without a pair bond and 2 with one, as an upper bound on the reduction that a given $V_A$ can produce, and call it the multiplicative bound. It is the case most favourable to explaining a loss of diversity. It also covers a variance that fluctuates: because the reduction accelerates with $V_A$, a fluctuating variance removes at least as much diversity as a constant one with the same mean (by Jensen's inequality). An additive fitness map, $w = 1 + a$, agrees with Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} for small $V_A$, but it gives negative fitness to a growing share of individuals as $V_A$ approaches one and is not used (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}).

\subsection{The ceiling on additive variance set by neutral diversity}\label{sec:ceiling}
Inverting the reduction gives the variance compatible with a given loss of neutral diversity. Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
be the diversity ratio: the coalescent effective size relative to the number of breeding adults. At the equilibrium of \autoref{sec:quantity}, setting the multiplicative bound equal to $\delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\text{max}}^{(\text{mult})} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa(0)}, \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
which grows only logarithmically as diversity falls. Because the bound overstates the reduction, Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult} is the smallest variance that could account for $\delta$: a population whose $V_A$ falls below it cannot owe its missing diversity to heritable variance in fitness at unlinked sites. Where diversity is only modestly reduced, $\delta \gtrsim 1/(1 + 4\kappa)$, inverting Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} gives the ceiling
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\text{max}} = \frac{1 - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta}, \label{eq:vamax}
\end{equation}
which strict monogamy halves relative to a system without a pair bond. For smaller $\delta$ it returns $V_{A,\max} > 1$, outside the range in which it holds. Both ceilings depend only on $\delta$ and the mating system, so any population with a diversity estimate can be read against them. Both attribute all variance in family size beyond the Poisson expectation to $V_A$; environmental or non-additive sources of reproductive variance would lower them. A population with $\delta \ge 1$ fails the equilibrium assumptions and is excluded rather than interpreted (Methods).

\subsection{Local effective size at linked sites}\label{sec:linkage}
At a neutral site linked to selected loci, recombination breaks associations only partially. They outlast the two generations of the global reduction and depress a local effective size that varies along the genome. How long they last depends on how long the selected alleles persist, so the local effective size, unlike the global one, depends on the process that maintains $V_A$, through the retention $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}). The expressions below are written for one class with retention $Z$. Under a DFE each is averaged over classes, weighted by the share of $V_A$ that each class carries at balance, $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$, where $\varphi_k$ is the fraction of new mutations in class $k$ (Appendix~D).

The variance is the measured one: chromosome $i$ carries $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is its share of the genome's functional sequence, rather than a share of the $U_d\bar{s}$ that balance would maintain. Because $Z_k$ does not depend on $V_A$, this choice changes the size of the linked reduction but not its form, and whenever $s^* > \bar{s}$ it gives the larger reduction. The calculation is therefore a hybrid. How long associations last, and how the variance is divided among classes, both presuppose mutation--selection balance; only the amount of variance departs from it, and that amount is one balance cannot supply. The self-consistent prediction of balance with background selection is the same expression at $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$, which is $c = c_\text{MSD}$ in \autoref{sec:conditions}.

An association between a neutral site and a selected locus $\ell$ Morgans away survives one generation of recombination and selection with probability $(1 - r(\ell))Z = \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})$, where $r(\ell) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - e^{-2\ell})$ is Haldane's mapping function. Whole chromosomes are often longer than 0.5 M, beyond which a linear map gives $r > 1/2$. Summing over generations, and averaging over selected loci and neutral sites spread uniformly along chromosome $i$, of map length $M_i$, gives (Appendix~C)
\begin{equation}
\overline{Q^2}_i = \frac{2}{M_i^2}\int_0^{M_i} (M_i - \ell)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})}\right)^2 d\ell. \label{eq:haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
The factor inside the integral is the one familiar from background selection: at $Z = 1 - s$, $1/[1 - (1-r)Z] = 1/[s + r(1-s)]$ (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996; Santiago and Caballero 1998, 2016; Buffalo and Kern 2024). Buffalo and Kern (2024) evaluate it at each position along a fine-scale recombination map. Averaging over the position of the neutral site instead gives the expected reduction on a chromosome when only its map length is known, as for the populations here. The same factor is the expected long-term contribution of a chromosome segment rather than of an individual. At an unlinked site an ancestor's advantage is carried forward with weight $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each generation; at a linked site the segment carrying the neutral allele keeps its selected background with probability $\rho = (1 - r)Z$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}), so the cumulative advantage is $1/(1-\rho)$, which returns $Q = 2$ at $r = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $Z = 1$. Interference therefore falls with map length and with the rate of purging, $1 - Z$. Appendix~C gives Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} in closed form (Appendix~\ref{app:C_closed}), its limits (Appendix~\ref{app:C_limits}), and the effect of clustering selected loci in regions of low recombination (Appendix~\ref{app:C_spatial}).

A neutral allele is linked in cis only to the selected loci on its own haplotype, because associations with the homologous chromosome are broken at meiosis (Buffalo and Kern 2024), so of the variance $v_i$ the focal haplotype carries $v_h = v_i/2$. Treating linked contributions as a Poisson process along the map (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Santiago and Caballero 2016), the linked reduction for chromosome $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right), \label{eq:ne_chrom}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_\text{cis}$ is the weight of the carrier's own breeding value in its family size. A social partner's genes are never in cis with the focal copy, and $\kappa_\text{cis} = 1$ without a pair bond and under Model B (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}).

The rest of the genome, meaning the homologous haplotype and the other chromosomes, assorts independently of the focal site and contributes the global reduction at variance $V_A - v_h$. Under social monogamy it also includes the partner's breeding value, so only the carrier's own share $\kappa_\text{own}$ of $\kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}$ loses $v_h$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}). Fitness is multiplicative across loci under the model of \autoref{sec:onemodel}, so the reduction is a product of per-locus factors, and the two terms combine in one exponential (Buffalo and Kern 2024, Supplementary Equations 28--29; cf.\ Santiago and Caballero 1998):
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\left\{-\left[\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h + Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)\right]\right\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
which under Model B, and without a pair bond, is $\exp\{-[\overline{Q^2}_i v_h + Q^2(\kappa V_A - v_h)]\}$. Here $Q = 2/(2 - Z)$ is Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} at the same retention as the linked term, and $Q^2$ is the limit of $\overline{Q^2}_i$ as $M_i \to \infty$. For small unlinked variance the second factor is $1/[1 + Q^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)]$, and for the upper bound $\kappa$ is replaced by $\kappa(0)$.

Equation~\eqref{total_red} splits the effective size at a neutral site into a local factor, $\exp(-\kappa_\text{cis}\overline{Q^2}_iv_h)$, and a global factor, $\exp[-Q^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)]$. Both arise from linkage disequilibrium between the neutral site and selected loci. They differ in recombination, $r = 1/2$ for the global factor and $r < 1/2$ for the local one, and in what they require: $V_A$ alone for the global factor, and also its maintenance for the local one. The cis variance moves from the global factor to the local one, so nothing is counted twice, and on a freely recombining chromosome the two reduce to the global reduction $e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). By linked selection we mean the local factor only. Relative to the global reduction at the same retention, it is
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right], \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
which does not exceed one, because $\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ in every effect class and $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$ in the primary model (Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). With neutral sites spread uniformly over the physical genome, the genome-wide values are averages over the $n$ autosomes weighted by physical length $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i. \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}
Setting Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} equal to $\delta$ gives the variance that would account for a diversity ratio with linkage included; because $\overline{\Omega}$ itself depends on $V_A$, it is found numerically (Methods). Since $\overline{\Omega} \le 1$, that variance is at most the unlinked value at the same retention, $\ln(1/\delta)/(Q^2\kappa)$, which at $Z = 1$ and with $\kappa(0)$ is Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}.

\subsection{The two comparisons as one condition}\label{sec:conditions}
On the scale of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}, each comparison becomes a bound on $c$. Mutation--selection--drift balance maintains at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{msdb}), so it requires
\begin{equation}
c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
Heritable variance in fitness accounts for a deficit at unlinked sites only if $\bar{V}_A$ reaches the smallest variance that could produce it, Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}, so the diversity comparison requires
\begin{equation}
c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\text{max}}^{(\text{mult})}(\delta)}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
A single $c$ satisfies both only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, that is, if
\begin{equation}
\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa(0)} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa(0)U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat} contains no pedigree estimate. A variance maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation can reduce diversity at unlinked sites to no less than $e^{-4\kappa(0)U_d\bar{s}}$ of its neutral expectation, even under the multiplicative bound. Whether the two comparisons can be reconciled is therefore settled by the mutational parameters before any estimate of $V_A$ is consulted, and the pedigree estimates locate each population relative to the two thresholds, at $c = 1$. With linkage, the linked variance of \autoref{sec:linkage} replaces Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}, and the condition is evaluated numerically (Methods).

\subsection{Direction of the approximations}\label{sec:approx}
On the diversity side, each principal approximation overstates the reduction in effective size: the multiplicative bound in place of a reduction that a ceiling on fitness would truncate (\autoref{sec:onemodel}); $\kappa(0)$ in place of $\kappa$ under social monogamy; $Q = 2$ in place of the smaller $Q$ of purged deleterious alleles (\autoref{sec:unlinked}); the attribution of all non-Poisson variance in family size to $V_A$ (\autoref{sec:ceiling}); and, in the linked term, the measured variance in place of what balance maintains, spread uniformly along the map for loci placed independently of recombination rate (\autoref{sec:linkage}). A finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too small to explain a deficit is therefore conservative. On the maintenance side, $U_d\,s$ is the most variance the balance maintains at any $N_e$, and a DFE with more mutations of very small and very large effect maintains less (\autoref{msdb}). So $s^*$ is the smallest mean effect that could maintain $\bar{V}_A$, and a finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too large to be maintained is conservative too. The mutational target is the exception: a larger $U_d$ lowers $s^*$ in proportion, so we carry both available estimates of the mutation rate (Methods). Apart from $\delta$ itself, no input to either comparison comes from diversity data (Methods).
