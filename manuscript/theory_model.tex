\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The quantity and its timescale}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$: the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive contribution, standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). Where each parent's own breeding value sets its family size it is the mean-standardized variance $C^2$ of Santiago and Caballero (1995); with pair-mated families their $C^2$ is the variance of family fitness, $2V_A$ (Appendix~B). Bonnet et al. (2022) estimate $V_A$ in these units. They fit a zero-inflated over-dispersed Poisson animal model to lifetime breeding success and back-transform the latent-scale additive genetic covariance matrix $\mathbf{G}$ as $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$ (Introduction). Their estimates therefore enter every model below without rescaling.

Both comparisons require the long-term value of this variance. Santiago and Caballero's derivation gives the variance effective size, $N_{eV}$, which sets the per-generation rate of drift. Nucleotide diversity measures the coalescent effective size, $N_{eC}$, with $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$. Under long-term demographic and selective equilibrium the two coincide ($N_{eV} = N_{eC} = N_e$), and this equality is what allows neutral diversity to constrain $V_A$. Diversity reflects genealogies with a total pairwise branch length of $4N_e$ generations, and mutation--selection--drift balance describes a stationary variance. Neither refers to the variance of a single generation.

A pedigree estimate refers to a different quantity, and the difference follows from the estimator. $V_A(w)$ is specific to the sampled cohorts and environment: $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, so the same $\mathbf{G}$ in a different environment gives a different $V_A(w)$. We therefore write the long-term variance as
\begin{equation}
\bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is unknown. The two comparisons respond to $c$ in opposite directions, since a larger $\bar{V}_A$ removes more diversity and is harder to maintain. The diversity comparison also assumes that $\bar{V}_A$ is sustained over the coalescent window, and whether recurrent mutation can sustain a variance of that size is what the mutation--selection--drift comparison tests.

Two ratios called $N_e/N$ appear in this literature, and neither is the one in our model equations. The demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$, set by family-size variance of every origin and by sex ratio, is typically 0.1--0.5. The diversity ratio $\delta = \pi/(4\mu N)$ (\autoref{sec:ceiling}) is typically several orders of magnitude smaller. In every equation below, $N_e/N$ denotes the reduction caused by heritable variance in fitness alone, relative to $N$ breeding adults whose family sizes are otherwise Poisson; at equilibrium it is the model's prediction for $\delta$.

For the rest of the paper we assume a constant number $N$ of breeding adults with an equal sex ratio, Poisson family sizes in the absence of selection, and random pairing with respect to breeding value ($r_{mf} = 0$; Appendices~A and B give the general forms). Notation is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:notation}.

\subsection{One model for the estimate and the prediction}\label{sec:onemodel}
The pedigree estimate and the diversity prediction can be derived from a single model of how breeding values map to fitness. Bonnet et al.'s (2022) animal model is multiplicative. Breeding values are additive and Gaussian on the latent scale, and expected breeding success is modeled as exponential through a log link, with a separate logit link for the probability of breeding at all (zero-inflation). For a log link, the additive variance on the observed scale equals the latent variance when mean relative fitness is one (de Villemereuil et al. 2016, Equations 15--16). The reported $V_A(w)$ is therefore the variance of the breeding value $z$ for log-fitness, $w \propto e^{z}$.

The same $z$ determines how long a gene copy's descendants persist. Barton and Etheridge (2011) modeled parents chosen with probability proportional to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. They show that the expected long-term contribution of an ancestor with log-fitness $z$, which they call its reproductive value, is $R(z) = \exp[2(z - \bar{z}) - 2V_A]$. Crucially, they find that its variance across ancestors is $e^{4V_A}$ with the probability of coalescence scaling with the square of $R$. Because the selected parents keep a Gaussian distribution of $z$ with unchanged variance, an ancestor's advantage is retained in full from one generation to the next ($Z = 1$, \autoref{sec:unlinked}) when $z$ is Gaussian and infinitesimal (Appendix~A.4). Taking the inbreeding effective size from the second moment of long-term contributions (Wray and Thompson 1990) gives, without a pair bond,
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} expands to $1 - 4V_A + O(V_A^2)$, the first-order result of Robertson (1961) and Santiago and Caballero (1995) derived in \autoref{sec:unlinked}. The two forms are one model at two orders, not competing models.

Table~\ref{tab:onemodel} sets out the chain. The first-order form holds for any fitness map but only while $V_A \ll 1$. The multiplicative form holds at every order, but only if the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness is real. A reduction of 100-fold rests on ancestors about 4.3 standard deviations above the mean, roughly one in $10^5$, and one of $10^4$-fold rests on ancestors about 6 standard deviations out. Any ceiling on fitness truncates that tail: limits on territory or clutch size, or the bounded probability of breeding in Bonnet et al.'s zero-inflation component. Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} is therefore an upper bound on the reduction that a given $V_A$ can produce. That makes it the generous case when asking whether heritable fitness variance can explain a loss of diversity. An additive map, $w = 1 + a$, would also reduce to the first-order form, but it gives negative fitness to a growing share of individuals as $V_A$ approaches one, so it is not used (Appendix~A.4).

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{msdb}
We first ask how large a variance recurrent mutation can maintain. The classical account is a balance between recurrent deleterious mutation and purifying selection. New mutations add variance at rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous effect on relative fitness (Charlesworth 2015). Selection removes this variance at rate $s$ per generation and drift at rate $1/(2N_e)$. For a uniform $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant deleterious allele (Kimura 1969) gives the stationary variance
\begin{equation}
V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right] \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
(Appendix~D). It runs from the mutation--drift limit $V_A = 2N_eU_ds^2$ for $N_es \ll 1$ (Lynch and Hill 1986) to the deterministic balance
\begin{equation}
V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1), \label{eq:HoC}
\end{equation}
in which each deleterious allele is held at frequency $q \approx \mu/s$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Charlesworth 2012). The bracket in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, so $U_d\,s$ is the largest variance the balance maintains at any effective size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:HoC} gives the selection coefficient that a long-term variance requires,
\begin{equation}
s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}. \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
Charlesworth (2015) made this comparison for fitness components in \emph{Drosophila} (see also Johnson and Barton 2005); here it is applied to total fitness, with $\bar{V}_A$ evaluated at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

The literature calls selection strong or weak in three senses, and we keep them apart. The first is selection against drift. For $4N_es \gg 1$ deleterious alleles are purged before they can fix, the balance is the deterministic one of Equation~\eqref{eq:HoC}, and background-selection theory applies. For $4N_es \lesssim 1$ alleles drift to fixation, and the variance falls below $U_ds$ by the variance those fixations remove (Santiago and Caballero 2016; Buffalo and Kern 2024, Supplementary Equation 43), which is Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD}. Only in this sense do we write strong or weak selection. The second is the rate of purging, $s$ per generation, which sets how long an association with a selected allele lasts; we call it fast or slow purging. The third is the size of a lineage's selective advantage, $V_A$ relative to one, which sets the order of the reduction in effective size (\autoref{sec:unlinked}); we call it first order or all orders.

In the strong-selection limit $s^*$ estimates the arithmetic mean $\bar{s}$ of the distribution of fitness effects (DFE), whatever the DFE's shape, because each class of mutation contributes $U_d\,s$ and so $V_A = U_d\,\bar{s}$. Shape enters only through nearly neutral mutations ($4N_es \lesssim 1$), which contribute less than $U_d\,s$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD}); a more leptokurtic DFE with the same mean therefore maintains less variance, not more. Site-frequency spectra constrain the strongly deleterious tail poorly, so a DFE mean inferred from them is partly an extrapolation. The weak-selection branch requires $2N_es \ll 1$, and with $V_A \approx 2N_eU_ds^2$ this implies $N_e \ll U_d/(2V_A)$: for $U_d$ of order one and $V_A$ of order 0.1, an effective size of a few individuals. No outbred population lies on that branch, so the strong-selection limit applies, and there Equations~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} and \eqref{eq:HoC} coincide.

The same balance sets how long a gene copy's association with its selected background lasts. We write $Z$ for the retention, the fraction of that association that survives one generation of selection. It is the $G$ of Santiago and Caballero (1995), defined there through the variance remaining among selected parents, and the $1 - \kappa$ of Buffalo and Kern (2024, Supplementary Equation 38), whose $\kappa$ is a rate of loss and unrelated to the mating-system scalar of \autoref{sec:mating}. A per-generation retention presupposes a stationary selected background, which mutation--selection--drift balance provides (Barton 1998, 2000). Its value depends on the architecture of $V_A$ (Appendix~A.4). For a Gaussian, infinitesimal breeding value under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$. For a deleterious allele of effect $s$, the association is lost with the carriers that selection removes (Nordborg et al. 1996; Charlesworth 2012, Appendix), so in the strong-selection limit an effect class $k$ is retained at
\begin{equation}
Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
whatever the size of $V_A$. At balance class $k$ contributes $U_d\varphi_ks_k$ to $V_A$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:HoC}), where $\varphi_k$ is the fraction of new mutations in that class, so it carries the share $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ of the variance. Every interference factor below that depends on $Z$ is the $\nu$-weighted average of its value over classes, and for uniform effects it is evaluated at the single $Z = 1 - s$. The aggregate rate at which the total variance decays, $V_m/V_A = \mathbb{E}[s^2]/\mathbb{E}[s] = \bar{s}(1 + \mathrm{CV}_s^2)$ (Santiago and Caballero 1998), equals $s$ only for uniform effects and is $6\bar{s}$ for a gamma DFE of shape 0.2 (Appendix~D). It is the $\nu$-weighted mean of $1 - Z_k$, but interference is nonlinear in $Z$, so the aggregate cannot stand in for the classes. Weak selection lowers the retention further (Buffalo and Kern 2024, Supplementary Equation 47).

\subsection{The genome-wide reduction in effective size}\label{sec:unlinked}
We next ask how much diversity a given $\bar{V}_A$ removes at a neutral site that assorts independently of all selected loci. Segregation halves the inherited breeding value each generation, and the cumulative transmission factor is
\begin{equation}
Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2.
\end{equation}
This is the case $Z = 1$, $r_{mf} = 0$ of Santiago and Caballero's (1995)
\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}, \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
with $Z$ the retention of \autoref{msdb} and $r_{mf}$ the correlation between mates' breeding values. $Z = 1$ is exact for a Gaussian, infinitesimal breeding value under the multiplicative model of \autoref{sec:onemodel}. Deleterious alleles at mutation--selection balance are purged as well as segregated, and give $Q = 2/(1+s) < 2$ for each effect class (Charlesworth 2012, Appendix; Appendix~D), so under the maintenance model tested here $Q = 2$ is an upper bound. Rare beneficial alleles that are sweeping would have $Z > 1$ (Appendix~A.4). Combining this selective contribution with Poisson sampling drift (Appendix~A) gives the unlinked reduction of Santiago and Caballero (1995), Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} at $Q = 2$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4V_A}, \label{unlinked_baseline}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of breeding adults and the demographic background is Poisson.

Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} is exact under long-term equilibrium, to first order in the selective advantage of a lineage, and the quantity it requires is the long-term equilibrium variance $\bar{V}_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}). If $V_A$ is not stationary, the equation holds generation by generation and diversity records its time average (\autoref{sec:mating}). $Q$ measures how long an ancestor's advantage persists in its descendants. An ancestor whose breeding value for log-fitness exceeds the mean by $z - \bar{z}$ passes that advantage to its offspring, half of it to its grand-offspring, and so on, so its expected long-term contribution is, to first order, $R(z) \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$, the leading term of $R(z)$ in \autoref{sec:onemodel}. Drift at a neutral site is set by the variance of long-term contributions among ancestors (Wray and Thompson 1990), and Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} states that inherited fitness differences add $Q^2V_A$ to that variance beyond the Poisson expectation. Because the series is dominated by its first terms, an ancestor's expected contribution is settled within a few generations (Barton and Etheridge 2011), and the equation requires the sign of a lineage's advantage to persist for only about two of them. Neutral diversity, in contrast, accumulates the drift these contributions cause over the $\sim 4N_e$ generations of a coalescence, which is why it responds to $\bar{V}_A$. At $Q = 2$ it makes no assumption about the haplotype structure of the selected background, and so applies under mutation--selection--drift balance, during directional sweeps, and under fluctuating selection that is positively autocorrelated across generations. The process that maintains $V_A$ enters only through $Z$.

We call the effective size of Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} the global effective size. Every neutral site shares it, because a site is exposed to the variance in family size through the individual that carries it, not through the haplotype on which it sits. Under mutation--selection--drift balance it is also the unlinked limit of background selection. Summing the single-locus effect of background selection (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996) over loci at $r = 1/2$ counts the associations of a neutral site with selected alleles in cis, which carry half the additive variance; the other half sits on the homologous copies, which at unlinked loci are reshuffled exactly as often and contribute equally (Appendix~D). The sum over cis copies therefore recovers half of Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline}, and the trans copies supply the rest.

The equation also has a domain. It treats each lineage's advantage as small, so it requires $V_A \ll 1$, and outside that range it is an extrapolation rather than a prediction. Beyond first order the multiplicative model gives Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult}, of which Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} is the expansion: the two agree while $V_A$ is small and separate as it grows, the multiplicative form giving the larger reduction. Asking what variance would produce a given reduction is therefore a question for Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult}, read as an upper bound on the reduction (\autoref{sec:onemodel}).

\subsection{Separate sexes and the mating system}\label{sec:mating}
Equations~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} and \eqref{unlinked_baseline} treat the population as hermaphrodite. With separate sexes, half of every genome passes through each sex, and Santiago and Caballero (1995) combine the sexes as $1/N_e = 1/(4N_{e,m}) + 1/(4N_{e,f})$ (after Crow and Denniston 1988). At an equal sex ratio this averages the selective variance over the sexes rather than adding it. What sets a parent's family size depends on the mating system. Without a pair bond, each parent's own breeding value does. Under social monogamy the unit of productivity is the nest, so a parent's family size also depends on its social partner's breeding value. Extra-pair paternity at rate $\alpha$ moves paternity away from the nest without changing the number of offspring the nest produces. Applying the expected long-term contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva (1999) to these mating systems (Appendix~B) gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}, \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is common to both sexes, as estimated by Bonnet et al. (2022), and $\kappa(\alpha)$ is a mating-system scalar.\footnote{Not the $\kappa$ of Buffalo and Kern (2024, Supplementary Equations 19--20 and 38), which is the per-generation rate at which a selected background's variance is lost, $1 - Z$ here.} Without a pair bond $\kappa = 1$, and Equation~\eqref{unlinked_baseline} is unchanged. Under social monogamy, when extra-pair gains are proportional to male breeding value and replace within-pair losses (Model B, used in all primary results),
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_B(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2, \label{eq:kappaB}
\end{equation}
which falls monotonically from 2 at $\alpha = 0$ to 9/8 at $\alpha = 1$. A model in which extra-pair siring is independent of male breeding value (Model A) is given in Appendix~B and used as a sensitivity bound. Under strict monogamy both models give $\kappa = 2$, the pair-mated model of Santiago and Caballero (1995): each nest pools two independent breeding values, so the variance of family fitness is $2V_A$, and a given reduction in diversity requires half the variance needed without a pair bond. Both forms assume $Z = 1$.

Under the multiplicative model the mating system enters the exponent. Without a pair bond Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} holds as written. Under social monogamy the long-term contribution of a gene copy mixes two lognormal terms, one for offspring of its own nest and one for extra-pair offspring, and the exact reduction is bracketed (Appendix~A.3):
\begin{equation}
    e^{-4\kappa(0)V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa_B V_A}, \qquad \kappa(0) = 2. \label{eq:ne_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
The two ends coincide under strict monogamy. The right-hand end matches Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} to first order, and the left-hand end is the largest reduction the model allows, because the within-nest term compounds fastest. Where an upper bound on the reduction is needed we use $e^{-4\kappa(0)V_A}$, the mating-system scalar without extra-pair paternity: 2 with a pair bond and 1 without.

The mating-system forms also fix what diversity records when $V_A$ changes over time. Each generation contributes $1/N_e(t)$, and neutral diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$ over the coalescent window (Wright 1938). At first order, $1/N_e(t) = [1 + 4\kappa V_A(t)]/N$, and over $T$ generations
\begin{equation}
\frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t). \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
Because the first-order reduction is linear in $V_A$, the harmonic mean passes the time average through without further approximation: diversity responds to $\bar{V}_A$ and to nothing else about the trajectory of $V_A$. This is why $\bar{V}_A$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:c} is the target of the diversity comparison. Under the multiplicative form $N/N_e(t) = e^{4\kappa V_A(t)}$ is convex in $V_A$, so by Jensen's inequality a fluctuating variance removes at least as much diversity as a constant variance equal to its mean. If $N$ also varies, Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic} holds with $N$ replaced by its harmonic mean provided that $V_A(t)$ is uncorrelated with $1/N(t)$; it also assumes a constant mating system, and so a constant $\kappa$.

\subsection{The ceiling on additive variance set by neutral diversity}\label{sec:ceiling}
Inverting Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} gives the largest $V_A$ compatible with a given loss of neutral diversity. Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
be the diversity ratio, the coalescent effective size relative to the number of breeding adults. Under the equilibrium of \autoref{sec:quantity} ($N_{eC} = N_{eV}$), setting Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} equal to $\delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\text{max}} = \frac{1 - \delta}{4\kappa(\alpha)\,\delta}. \label{eq:vamax}
\end{equation}
The ceiling depends only on $\delta$ and $\kappa$, and we present it as a curve against which any population with a diversity estimate can be read; strict monogamy halves it relative to a system without a pair bond. The derivation attributes all overdispersion of family size beyond the Poisson expectation to $V_A$; environmental or non-additive sources of reproductive variance would lower the ceiling.

Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax} inherits the domain of Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating}: it is a valid ceiling only while it returns $V_{A,\text{max}} \ll 1$, roughly for $\delta \gtrsim 1/(1 + 4\kappa)$. For smaller $\delta$ the ceiling comes from the multiplicative model. Inverting Equations~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} and \eqref{eq:ne_mult_pair} gives, at all orders,
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\text{max}}^{(\text{mult})} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa(0)}, \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
which grows only logarithmically as diversity falls. Because the multiplicative reduction is an upper bound, Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult} is the smallest variance that could account for a diversity ratio $\delta$: a population whose $V_A$ falls below it cannot owe its missing diversity to heritable variance in fitness at unlinked sites. A population with $\delta \ge 1$ gives $V_{A,\text{max}} \le 0$, which indicates that the equilibrium assumptions fail for that population; such populations are excluded rather than interpreted (Methods).

\subsection{Local effective size at linked sites}\label{sec:linkage}
The global effective size leaves out one source of drift. At a neutral site linked to selected loci, associations do not halve each generation: recombination breaks them only partially, and how long they last depends on how long the selected background itself persists. The resulting reduction varies along the genome, and we call it the local effective size. Its persistence is the retention $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), so unlike the global effective size it depends on the process that maintains $V_A$. We write the expressions below for a single class with retention $Z$; under a DFE each is averaged over classes with weights $\nu_k$ (\autoref{msdb}). The variance itself is the measured one: chromosome $i$ carries $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is its share of the genome's functional sequence, not a share of the $U_d\bar{s}$ that balance would maintain. Because $Z_k$ does not depend on $V_A$, this choice changes the magnitude of the linked reduction and not its kernel. Whenever $s^* > \bar{s}$ (\autoref{msdb}), the measured variance exceeds what balance maintains, and this choice gives the larger reduction. The calculation is therefore a hybrid. The kernel, the retentions $Z_k = 1 - s_k$ and the weights $\nu_k$ all presuppose mutation--selection balance: deleterious alleles held at $q \approx \mu/s$ and purged at rate $s$. Only the amount of variance departs from it, and that amount is one balance cannot supply. The self-consistent prediction of balance with background selection is the same expression at $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$, which is $c = c_\text{MSD}$ in \autoref{sec:conditions}.

Map distance $\ell$ (in Morgans) is converted to recombination frequency with the Haldane function, $r(\ell) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - e^{-2\ell})$, because whole chromosomes often exceed 0.5 M, beyond which a linear map gives $r > 1/2$. An association survives one generation of recombination and selection with probability $(1 - r(\ell))Z = \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})$. Summing over generations, and averaging over selected loci and neutral sites spread uniformly along chromosome $i$, of map length $M_i$, gives (Appendix~C)
\begin{equation}
\overline{Q^2}_i = \frac{2}{M_i^2}\int_0^{M_i} (M_i - \ell)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})}\right)^2 d\ell. \label{eq:haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
The kernel is the background-selection kernel: with $Z = 1 - s$ it is $1/[s + r(1-s)]$ (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996; Santiago and Caballero 1998, 2016; Buffalo and Kern 2024). In the terms of \autoref{sec:unlinked}, the kernel is the expected long-term contribution of a chromosome segment rather than of an individual. At an unlinked site an ancestor's advantage is carried forward with weight $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each generation, because its descendants inherit half of its breeding value. At a linked site the segment carrying the neutral allele keeps its selected background with probability $\rho = (1 - r)Z$ per generation (Appendix~B), so the cumulative advantage is $1/(1-\rho)$, which returns $Q = 2$ when $r = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $Z = 1$. Linkage strengthens the reduction because it lengthens the persistence of a segment's excess contribution. Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} has a closed form, and the linear-map form is a lower bound on it (Appendix~C). Real chromosomes lie between the limits of complete linkage ($M_i \ll 1 - Z$) and free recombination ($M_i \gg 1/(1-Z)$), near $\overline{Q^2}_i \approx 2/[M_i(1-Z)]$ (Santiago and Caballero 1998; Appendix~C and Table~\ref{tab:linked_limits}), so interference decreases with map length and with the rate of purging, $1 - Z$. With one obligate crossover per bivalent, avian microchromosomes have map lengths of about 0.5 M or more and do not approach complete linkage. When selected loci are placed independently of local recombination rate, spreading the variance uniformly along the map maximizes the expected reduction; clustering in regions of low recombination can reverse this ordering (Appendix~C).

A neutral allele is linked in cis only to selected loci on its own haplotype, because associations with the homologous chromosome are broken at meiosis (Buffalo and Kern 2024). Of the variance $v_i$ on chromosome $i$, the focal haplotype carries $v_h = v_i/2$. Treating linked contributions as a Poisson process along the map (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Santiago and Caballero 2016), the linked reduction for chromosome $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\left(-\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h\right). \label{eq:ne_chrom}
\end{equation}
The linked term carries only $\kappa_\text{cis}$, the weight of the carrier's own breeding value in its family size, because a social partner's genes are never in cis with the focal copy (Appendix~B). $\kappa_\text{cis} = 1$ without a pair bond and under Model B.

The rest of the genome, the homologous haplotype and all other chromosomes, assorts independently of the focal site and contributes the unlinked reduction at variance $V_A - v_h$. Under social monogamy this background also includes the partner's breeding value, so only the own component $\kappa_\text{own}$ of $\kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}$ loses $v_h$ (Appendix~B). Buffalo and Kern (2024) omitted the associations with the homologous haplotype; here that variance is assigned to the unlinked background. The two terms act on independent segments of the genealogy and combine multiplicatively. Under the multiplicative model of \autoref{sec:onemodel} fitness is multiplicative across loci as well, and both terms are exponential (Buffalo and Kern 2024, Supplementary Equation 29):
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\left\{-\left[\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h + Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)\right]\right\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
which under Model B, and without a pair bond, is $\exp\{-[\overline{Q^2}_i v_h + Q^2(\kappa V_A - v_h)]\}$. Here $Q$ is the unlinked factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} evaluated at the same retention $Z$ as the linked term ($r_{mf} = 0$), $Q = 2/(2-Z)$. It is the $M_i \to \infty$ limit of $\overline{Q^2}_i$ (Appendix~C) and returns 2 at $Z = 1$. To first order in the unlinked variance, the second factor is the hyperbolic reduction $1/[1 + Q^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)]$. Where an upper bound on the reduction is required, $\kappa$ is replaced by $\kappa(0)$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult_pair}).

Equation~\eqref{total_red} partitions the effective size at a neutral site into a local factor, $\exp(-\kappa_\text{cis}\overline{Q^2}_iv_h)$, and a global factor, $\exp[-Q^2(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h)]$. The partition conserves variance: the cis variance $v_h$ moves from the global term to the local one, and the trans variance (the homologous haplotype, the other chromosomes and, under social monogamy, the partner) stays in the global term, so nothing is counted twice. As $M_i \to \infty$, $\overline{Q^2}_i \to Q^2$, and in the primary model ($\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$) the two factors recombine into the global reduction $e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}$: a freely recombining chromosome is indistinguishable from the rest of the genome (Appendix~C). Both factors arise from associations, that is linkage disequilibrium, between the neutral site and selected loci. They differ in the recombination fraction, $r = 1/2$ for the global factor, whose associations last about two generations, against $r < 1/2$ for the local one, whose associations last up to $1/[1 - (1-r)Z]$ generations; and in what they require, $V_A$ alone for the global factor and also its maintenance for the local one. In what follows, linked selection refers to the local factor only.

The reduction attributable to linkage, relative to the unlinked reduction at the same retention, $e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}$, is
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right]. \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
Variance assigned to the linked term no longer contributes to the unlinked one, and the exponent is the difference between the two. Under a DFE both exponents are $\nu$-weighted sums over effect classes. Because $\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ on every chromosome and in every class and $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$ in the primary model, $\Omega_i \le 1$ follows directly (Appendix~C). With neutral sites spread uniformly over the physical genome, the genome-wide values are the length-weighted averages over the $n$ autosomes,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i, \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}
where $L_i$ is the physical length of autosome $i$ and $L_\text{total} = \sum_i L_i$. Setting Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} equal to $\delta$ gives the variance that would account for a diversity ratio with linkage included. It is implicit, because $\overline{\Omega}$ depends on $V_A$ through $v_h$, and is found numerically (Methods). Because $\overline{\Omega} \le 1$, it does not exceed the unlinked value at the same retention, $\ln(1/\delta)/(Q^2\kappa)$. At $Z = 1$, with $\kappa$ replaced by $\kappa(0)$ as for the upper bound, that value is Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}.

\subsection{The two comparisons as conditions on the long-term variance}\label{sec:conditions}
The two comparisons can now be stated on the common scale of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}. Mutation--selection--drift balance maintains at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{msdb}), so it requires $\bar{V}_A \le U_d\,\bar{s}$, or
\begin{equation}
c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
Heritable variance in fitness accounts for an observed deficit at unlinked sites only if $\bar{V}_A$ reaches at least the smallest variance that could produce it, Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult}, so the diversity comparison requires
\begin{equation}
c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\text{max}}^{(\text{mult})}(\delta)}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
A single $c$ satisfies both only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, that is,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa(0)} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa(0)U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat} contains no pedigree estimate. It is a property of the model and the mutational parameters: a variance maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation can reduce diversity at unlinked sites to no less than $e^{-4\kappa(0)U_d\bar{s}}$ of its neutral expectation, even under the bound most generous to the reduction. Whether the two comparisons can be reconciled is therefore decided before any estimate of $V_A$ is consulted; the pedigree estimates locate each population relative to the two thresholds, at $c = 1$. Replacing Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax_mult} by the linked value of \autoref{sec:linkage} gives the corresponding condition with linkage, which we evaluate numerically (Methods).

\subsection{Direction of the approximations}\label{sec:approx}
The principal approximations above have known directions, and on each side they act in the same direction. On the diversity side each of the following overstates the reduction in effective size: the multiplicative form at all orders, which takes the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness as real although zero-inflation and any ceiling on fitness truncate it (\autoref{sec:onemodel}); $\kappa(0)$ in place of $\kappa$ under social monogamy (\autoref{sec:mating}); full retention, $Q = 2$, rather than the $Q < 2$ of purged deleterious alleles (\autoref{sec:unlinked}); the attribution of all non-Poisson variance in family size to $V_A$ (\autoref{sec:ceiling}); the uniform spread of variance along the map, for loci placed independently of recombination rate (\autoref{sec:linkage}; Appendix~C); and the measured variance, rather than the variance balance would maintain, in the linked term (\autoref{sec:linkage}). The predicted reduction is therefore an upper bound, and a finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too small to explain a deficit is conservative. On the maintenance side, $U_d\,s$ is the largest variance the balance maintains at any $N_e$, and a more leptokurtic DFE at the same mean maintains less (\autoref{msdb}), so $s^*$ is the smallest mean effect that could maintain $\bar{V}_A$, and a finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too large to be maintained is likewise conservative. The mutational target is the exception: a larger $U_d$ lowers $s^*$ in proportion, and we therefore carry both available estimates of the mutation rate (Methods). Apart from $\delta$ itself, no input to either comparison is taken from diversity data (Methods).
