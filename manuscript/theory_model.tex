\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The long-term additive genetic variance in fitness}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity throughout this work is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, written $V_A$ hereafter and defined as the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive success standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). \claude{Its square root is the standard deviation of the relative fitness that individuals' breeding values predict, so at $V_A = 0.1$ an individual one standard deviation above the mean expects about a third more offspring than average.} Santiago and Caballero's family-fitness variance $C^2$ equals $2V_A$ with pair families and $V_A$ under random mating (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}), while Barton and Etheridge's log-fitness variance \claude{equals $V_A$ under the log link of the animal model} (\autoref{sec:onemodel}). At MSDB under linkage equilibrium, $V_A = \sum_j 2q_j(1-q_j)s_j^2$, summed over sites segregating for a semidominant deleterious allele at frequency $q_j$ with heterozygous effect $s_j$ on relative fitness (\autoref{sec:msdb}). Bonnet et al. estimated $V_A$ on the observed scale of relative fitness by back-transforming their latent parameters via the multivariate \textit{delta} method, $V_A = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive genetic covariance matrix of the latent predictors and $\mathbf{d}$ is the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to them. \claude{Consequently, these estimates are the variance that both the drift and the MSDB models require, without rescaling.}

The same variance describes selection at two distinct levels. At the phenotypic level, it \claude{measures} directional selection, which increases the mean breeding value for fitness by $V_A$ each generation (Fisher's \claude{fundamental theorem}). At the allelic level, however, if the population is at MSDB, \claude{all selection on alleles is purifying, and the variance, the gain in mean fitness it produces each generation and the mutational loss that offsets that gain all equal $U_d\bar{s}$ (}\autoref{sec:msdb}\claude{)}. Consequently, directional selection on the fitness phenotype does not imply positive selection on any specific allele. To maintain this distinction, we use \emph{directional} \claude{only} for phenotypes, and \emph{purifying} and \emph{positive} for selection against an allele and toward its fixation, respectively.

Throughout this study, $N_e/N$ denotes the reduction in effective size caused \claude{only} by heritable variance in fitness. The reference is \claude{a neutral population} of $N$ breeding adults with Poisson-distributed family sizes, \claude{in which} $N_e = N$. In this framework, $N_e$ \claude{is the inbreeding effective size, set by the probability that two lineages coalesce in the preceding generation (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_R}\claude{), and therefore the coalescent effective size $N_{eC}$ that neutral diversity measures, $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$, with per-site mutation rate $\mu$. The inbreeding, variance and coalescent effective sizes coincide in any population whose size and pattern of reproduction remain constant, not only under Wright--Fisher sampling}~\cite{caballero1994}\claude{, and they differ when the population changes in size}~\cite{crow1988}\claude{. Accordingly, the variance effective size converges to our $N_e$ over many generations when $N$ and $V_A$ are constant, whereas the variance effective size of a single generation omits the persistence of an ancestor's advantage across generations, which produces the factor $Q^2$ (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_R}\claude{).} \claude{The short-term demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$, which compounds all sources of reproductive variance, is typically 0.1--0.5}~\cite{frankham1995}\claude{, whereas} the diversity ratio $N_{eC}/N$ is usually orders of magnitude smaller~\cite{buffalo2021,lewin2026}. Our $N_e/N$ calculation predicts this diversity ratio only if heritable fitness variance is the sole departure from the Poisson baseline, the population is at long-term equilibrium, and \claude{$N$ is the harmonic mean number of breeding adults, over the genealogy (Equation}~\eqref{eq:harmonic}\claude{), of the population that $\pi$ samples}. We write $N$ for breeding adults (to which $Q$ and $Z$ also refer) and $N_c$ for a total adult census\claude{, and we distinguish the diversity ratio $\delta = N_{eC}/N$, a parameter, from its estimate $\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$, which substitutes a contemporary total census for $N$ (}\autoref{sec:pi_methods}\claude{)}; Methods treats the component of the empirical estimates that reflects pre-breeding survival. \claude{The ratio $\delta$ is the fraction of its neutral expectation that a population's diversity retains, and we call $1/\hat{\delta}$ its deficit, so a 1,000-fold deficit leaves a thousandth of the expected diversity.}

\claude{For a single variance to satisfy both tests, its average over the roughly $4N_e$ generations that the genealogy spans must equal the stationary variance of MSDB} (\autoref{sec:msdb})\claude{. A pedigree estimate is instead conditional on the cohorts and environment of a single study, because the derivative vector $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population; the same $\mathbf{G}$ would yield a different $V_A$ in another environment.} To account for this mismatch, we write the required theoretical variance as
\begin{equation}
    \bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is an unknown scaling factor.

Biologically, $c$ \claude{measures} how representative the study period \claude{and the study population are of the evolutionary past of the population whose genealogy $\pi$ records. Because the lineages sampled for $\pi$ move among localities over the coalescent window, whereas a pedigree samples one, $c$ includes spatial as well as temporal departures of the pedigree estimate from the long-term variance.} Analytically, $c$ is the factor by which the empirical estimate must be multiplied to reproduce the observed data, allowing us to evaluate the discrepancy each model demands. Throughout, we assume a constant $N$ with an equal sex ratio and random pairing with respect to breeding value; Appendices~A and B provide general forms, and Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists all notation.

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{sec:msdb}
At MSDB, the influx of deleterious mutations generates additive genetic variance in fitness at a rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$ per generation, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate\claude{, the mean number of new deleterious mutations in a zygote,} and $s$ is the heterozygous effect on relative fitness. Each generation, selection removes a fraction $s$ of the standing variance $V_A$, while drift removes a fraction $1/(2N_e)$~\cite{burger1989,charlesworth2015}. For a single effect $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant allele~\cite{kimura1969} (Appendix~\ref{app:D_stationary}) gives the equilibrium variance
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right]. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
When selection is weak relative to drift ($N_es \ll 1$), MSDB reduces to \claude{$2N_eU_ds^2$, the variance at mutation--drift balance}~\cite{lynch1986}. When selection is strong relative to drift, each deleterious allele is held near its deterministic mutation--selection balance frequency, yielding~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2012}
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1). \label{eq:strong}
\end{equation}
\claude{Intuitively, a mutation adds variance in proportion to $s^2$ for the roughly $1/s$ generations before selection removes it, so it contributes in proportion to $s$, and only the number of new mutations and their mean effect matter.} \claude{Selection that is strong relative to drift ($N_es \gg 1$) is compatible with the small variance ($V_A \ll 1$) and small effects ($s \ll 1$) that the models of the reduction in $N_e$ assume, because both hold in a large enough population.} Because the bracketed term in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, \claude{$U_d\,s$ is the most variance MSDB can maintain at any effective size.} Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:strong} yields the deleterious effect required to sustain a given long-term variance,
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}, \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
which we evaluate at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

Under a distribution of fitness effects (DFE), class $k$ receives a fraction $\varphi_k$ of new mutations and, provided $N_es_k \gg 1$, contributes $U_d\varphi_ks_k$ to the standing variance. Summed across all classes, the total maintained variance is $U_d\bar{s}$. Consequently, \claude{because each mutation contributes in proportion to its effect,} $s^*$ recovers the arithmetic mean $\bar{s}$ of the DFE regardless of its underlying shape, while nearly neutral mutations ($N_es_k \lesssim 1$) contribute less (Appendix~\ref{app:D_dfe}). We assume this strong-selection limit throughout, \claude{which reduces} MSDB to mutation--selection balance. Because $N_es \ll 1$ implies $V_A = 2N_eU_ds^2 \ll U_d/(2N_e)$, a variance maintained primarily by weakly selected mutations would instead require $N_e \ll U_d/(2V_A)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion}; Results).

\subsection{Effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:unlinked}
We first consider a neutral site that assorts independently of all selected loci, \claude{under} the assumptions of \autoref{sec:quantity}, and assume the association between this site and its selected background is eroded \claude{only} by segregation. \claude{We follow a neutral gene copy from an ancestor} ($t = 0$) whose breeding value for log-fitness deviates from the population mean by $z - \bar{z}$. Because each subsequent carrier receives half of its breeding value from the parent transmitting the copy and half from a randomly drawn mate, the carrier's expected breeding value deviation in generation $t$ halves each generation to $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$. \claude{For small $V_A$, the carrier's expected relative fitness is} $1 + (z - \bar{z})/2^t$. Because the number of gene copies is multiplied by its carrier's relative fitness each generation, the ancestor's expected long-term contribution is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) \approx \prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\left[1 + \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2^t}\right] \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z}), \qquad Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2,
\end{equation}
yielding Robertson's cumulative transmission factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. \claude{$R$ is the share of the population's genes, many generations later, that descends from this ancestor, relative to the share of an average ancestor.} \claude{The pedigree estimate $\hat{V}_A$ is the variance of the $t = 0$ factor alone, the ancestor's own relative fitness. The later terms follow from Mendelian inheritance, under the same selection over the few generations that the series spans (stated below).} Intuitively, $Q = 2$ implies that an ancestor's descendants collectively add \claude{as much to its long-term contribution as its own breeding success}.

Drift at a neutral site is governed by the variance in long-term contributions among ancestors~\cite{wray1990}. Relative to the Poisson baseline \claude{of} \autoref{sec:quantity}, the effective population size ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}, \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
a relation derived from the probability of pairwise lineage coalescence (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}). \claude{Two lineages are more likely to meet in an ancestor that left a large share of descendants, so the more unequal the shares, the faster lineages coalesce; with equal shares the ratio is one.} \claude{Every reduction at unlinked sites in this study rests on this ratio, because the theories of drift under selection that we use all compute $R$ and differ only in how it depends on the breeding values of an ancestor and its mates (Table}~\ref{tab:models}\claude{).} Assuming small $V_A$, the linear approximation $R \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$ yields a variance of $Q^2V_A$. \claude{This term is the variance in long-term contributions that inherited fitness differences add to the Poisson baseline}~\cite{robertson1961,nei1966,woolliams1993}; \cite[Equation~16]{santiago1995} (Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}). Consequently, for an unlinked site under random mating ($Q = 2$), Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} simplifies to $N_e/N = 1/(1 + 4V_A)$. 

\claude{In the gene-centered perspective of Barton and Etheridge}~\cite{barton2011}\claude{, $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$ is the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the neutral copy and its carrier's selected background, and the two perspectives coincide at an unlinked site; only the gene-centered perspective extends to the other processes that break the association, selection (below) and recombination (}\autoref{sec:linkage}\claude{).}

Selection further erodes an ancestor's advantage by removing carriers of deleterious alleles. To quantify this, we define the retention factor $Z$ as the fraction of a neutral site's association with its selected background that survives one generation of selection. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} treat this retention factor as a constant parameter across generations. Assuming mates have breeding values correlated by $r_{mf}$, their cumulative transmission factor is
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}. \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian breeding value composed of many loci of small effect under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$ exactly\claude{, because selection among parents with normally distributed breeding values shifts their mean and leaves their spread unchanged, so none of an ancestor's advantage is lost to selection. For this reason} the model of Barton and Etheridge (\autoref{sec:onemodel}) requires no free retention factor (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). Conversely, because the association of a deleterious allele is lost alongside the carriers that selection removes~\cite{nordborg1996}; \cite[Appendix]{charlesworth2012}, each effect class $k$ is retained under strong selection at
\begin{equation}
    Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
independent of the magnitude of $V_A$. For an unlinked site under random mating, this yields $Q = 2/(1 + s_k) < 2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}). Intuitively, while purging erodes the association by a fraction $s_k$ each generation, independent assortment at an unlinked site already halves the association per generation. For small $s_k$, \claude{independent assortment removes the association far faster than purging does, so $Q$ changes little}. Consequently, under MSDB, $Q = 2$ \claude{is an upper bound}, and the \claude{process that maintains} $V_A$ affects the unlinked $N_e$ reduction \claude{only} through $Z$. We therefore compute this unlinked reduction assuming $Z = 1$, reserving the retention factor $Z_k$ for the local reduction at linked sites (\autoref{sec:linkage}). Because this unlinked effect acts through the individual carrying the site rather than the haplotype on which it resides, every independently assorting neutral site in the genome experiences it equally. We refer to this shared baseline as the global effective size. \claude{Under} MSDB, this global reduction converges to the limit of background selection at unlinked loci (Appendix~\ref{app:D_bgs}).

This reduction \claude{requires fixed additive effects, so that an allele's effect on fitness does not depend on its own frequency}. This condition excludes underdominance, stabilizing selection, and frequency-dependent selection. Within this additive regime, an ancestor's expected contribution \claude{is settled within about ten generations, and mostly within the first two, because} the initial terms of the $Q$ series dominate~\cite{barton2011}. Consequently, the \claude{advantage needs to keep its sign for only a few generations to amplify drift}. This condition is met under MSDB, during beneficial sweeps, and under fluctuating selection with positive intergenerational autocorrelation. Conversely, selection that reverses direction every generation \claude{cuts this advantage short, because} the terms of the series alternate in sign, \claude{giving} $Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(-\tfrac{1}{2})^t = 2/3$. However, applying Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} to quantify this drift requires two additional conditions: the retention factor must remain constant across generations~\cite{santiago1995}, and allele frequencies at the selected loci must change \claude{little} while LD persists~\cite{barton2000}. \claude{The infinitesimal model meets the second condition}, as does MSDB at unlinked sites, where deleterious alleles remain near their equilibrium frequencies. In contrast, alleles \claude{that sweep between extreme frequencies violate it and generate} more drift than the concurrent fitness variance alone would predict~\cite{barton2000}.

\claude{These conditions apply only to the few generations over which a single ancestor's contribution is settled, not to the roughly $4N_e$ generations of the genealogy (}\autoref{sec:quantity}\claude{), over which $V_A$ may change.} Because each generation contributes an inverse effective size of $N/N_e(t) = 1 + 4V_A(t)$, long-term diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$~\cite{wright1938}. Over $T$ generations,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t). \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
\claude{Because this expression is linear in $V_A(t)$, under the small-variance approximation neutral diversity depends only on the arithmetic mean $\bar{V}_A$, the quantity defined in Equation}~\eqref{eq:c}\claude{, and, for a constant $Z$, not on how $V_A$ varied over time.} \claude{Under the multiplicative model, fluctuations remove more diversity than a constant variance with the same mean (}\autoref{sec:onemodel}\claude{).} If the census size $N(t)$ also fluctuates, this relationship holds with $N$ replaced by its harmonic mean provided $V_A(t)$ and $1/N(t)$ are uncorrelated. If they covary, however, the long-term variance required by Equation~\eqref{eq:c} becomes the mean of $V_A(t)$ weighted by $1/N(t)$, because generations with small population sizes \claude{contribute most to both} the harmonic mean and the drift induced by heritable fitness variance.

\subsection{Large variances and the multiplicative bound}\label{sec:onemodel}
\claude{The small-variance approximation $N_e/N = 1/(1 + 4V_A)$ holds only provided $V_A \ll 1$, as does its extension to other mating systems (}\autoref{sec:mating}\claude{).} \claude{The restriction arises because the linear relationship approximates the product of fitnesses across generations, $R(z)$, by a sum, $1+Q(z-\bar{z})$, and discards their compounding. The pedigree estimates $\hat{V}_A$ are mostly small enough for this approximation. However, inverting it to find the long-term variance $\bar{V}_A$ that the observed diversity deficits ($N_{eC}/N \ll 1$) require gives $\bar{V}_A \gg 1$, where the approximation breaks down and overestimates the required variance.}

Evaluating large variances therefore requires a non-linear model of fitness. The generalized linear mixed models used by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} already assume a multiplicative architecture, modeling expected breeding success as exponential in a Gaussian latent breeding value $z$ ($w \propto e^{z}$). \claude{Under a log link, the additive variance in relative fitness on the observed scale equals the latent variance, $V_A = \sigma_z^2$ with $\sigma_z^2 = \mathrm{Var}(z)$, because the average derivative of expected fitness with respect to $z$ is mean fitness}~\cite[Equations~15--16]{devillemereuil2016}\claude{. The total genetic variance in relative fitness, $e^{\sigma_z^2} - 1$, is larger, and the excess is non-additive on the observed scale. The pedigree estimate $\hat V_A$ is therefore the variance of $z$ that the multiplicative model requires, exactly for the log-link component of the animal model and approximately once its zero-inflation component is included.}

Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} solved this multiplicative model \claude{exactly} for parents drawn in proportion to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. Because a lineage's per-generation selective advantages sum exactly on the log scale without truncation, the long-term contribution $R$ is exponential rather than linear in $z$ ($R \propto e^{Qz}$). Consequently, $Q = 2$ \claude{appears} in the exponent, and Equation~\eqref{eq:ratio} evaluates to $\exp[-\mathrm{Var}(\ln R)]$. Under random mating, heritable variance inflates the coalescence rate by a factor of $e^{4V_A}$~\cite{barton2011,weissman2012} (\claude{with $\sigma_z^2 = V_A$;} Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
In the limit of small $V_A$, both Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} and the small-variance approximation converge to $1 - 4V_A$. \claude{The forms we use are therefore nested rather than competing. The small-variance approximation is the limit for small $V_A$ of every smooth mapping from breeding value to fitness, the multiplicative model is exact for the architecture above, which the animal model assumes, and the multiplicative bound (below) is the largest reduction that the multiplicative model allows. Where the multiplicative model and its limit disagree, the difference measures how much a prediction depends on how loci combine to determine fitness.} As $V_A$ increases, however, exponential compounding in Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} produces a \claude{much} stronger reduction in $N_e$. Under strict monogamy, where pairs form at random and family productivity scales with $e^{z + z'}$, the exponent doubles to $e^{-8V_A}$~\cite{weissman2012}, \claude{the} multiplicative counterpart \claude{of} Santiago and Caballero's pair-mated families. \claude{We write both reductions as $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$, where $\kappa_\text{max}$, the variance of long-term log-contributions in units of its random-mating value, is 2 under social monogamy and 1 under random mating; extra-pair paternity gives a smaller reduction (}\autoref{sec:mating}\claude{).}

\claude{The exponential reduction depends on the unbounded upper tail of the Gaussian distribution for log-fitness. A 100-fold reduction in $N_e$, for instance, comes mostly from ancestors more than four standard deviations above the mean (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_tail}\claude{). In natural populations, ecological and physiological constraints, such as territorial saturation, clutch-size trade-offs or the bounded probability of breeding in Bonnet et al.'s zero-inflation component, truncate this tail and weaken the resulting drift.} We therefore treat $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$ as the \claude{largest} reduction in neutral diversity that a stationary variance $V_A$ can \claude{cause, and call it} the \emph{multiplicative bound}. Unlike the linear regime of Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}, however, the convexity of the exponential coalescence rate (\claude{$N/N_e(t) = e^{4\kappa_\text{max} V_A(t)}$}) implies by Jensen's inequality that temporal fluctuations in $V_A(t)$ around a mean $\bar{V}_A$ remove even more diversity than a stationary variance of the same mean. \claude{The least variance that accounts for a deficit (}\autoref{sec:conditions}\claude{) then constrains $(4\kappa_\text{max})^{-1}\ln$ of the time average of $e^{4\kappa_\text{max} V_A(t)}$, which exceeds $\bar{V}_A$ appreciably only if the variance reaches, in some generations, values well above its mean. Moreover, a finite population realizes the bound only while $8\kappa_\text{max}V_A \lesssim \ln N$. Above that variance, the ancestors that produce the reduction are rarer than one per generation (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_tail}\claude{), so the bound describes a population of $N$ breeding adults only down to $N_e \approx \sqrt{N}$, below which coalescence comes in bursts from single exceptional lineages.}

\subsection{The two equilibrium tests as limits on the long-term variance}\label{sec:conditions}
Inverting the multiplicative bound yields the minimum long-term variance required to account for an observed deficit in neutral diversity. Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
denote the diversity ratio defined in \autoref{sec:quantity}\claude{, which we evaluate at its estimate $\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$ (}\autoref{sec:pi_methods}\claude{)}. At long-term equilibrium, setting the multiplicative bound equal to $\delta$ gives the minimum required variance:
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\delta} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}}. \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
Because the multiplicative bound maximizes the loss of diversity per unit of variance, a population with $\bar{V}_A < V_{A,\delta}$ cannot owe its diversity deficit solely to heritable fitness variance at unlinked sites. \claude{The variance} $V_{A,\delta}$ grows only logarithmically with the deficit, increasing by $\ln(10)/(4\kappa_\text{max}) \approx 0.58/\kappa_\text{max}$ for each additional tenfold loss of diversity. Explaining a 100-fold deficit under random mating, for instance, requires $\bar{V}_A \ge 1.15$, while a 10,000-fold deficit requires $\bar{V}_A \ge 2.3$. \claude{Inverting the small-variance approximation instead gives a ceiling that holds only in populations retaining at least about a fifth of their neutral expectation under random mating and a ninth under strict monogamy (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_first}\claude{).}

Expressed in terms of the scaling factor $c = \bar{V}_A/\hat{V}_A$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:c}), each equilibrium model imposes a one-sided threshold on $c$. Because MSDB can maintain at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}), a population passes the maintenance test only if
\begin{equation}
    c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
When \claude{$c_\text{MSD} < 1$}, this ratio \claude{is the largest} fraction of the pedigree estimate that recurrent deleterious mutation can sustain. Conversely, heritable fitness variance accounts for the unlinked diversity deficit only if $\bar{V}_A \ge V_{A,\delta}$, \claude{so} a population passes the diversity test only if
\begin{equation}
    c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\delta}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
When \claude{$c_\text{div} > 1$}, this ratio gives the minimum factor by which the long-term variance must exceed the contemporary pedigree estimate to explain the missing diversity. \claude{The two thresholds also size each equilibrium at the pedigree estimate. The first, $c_\text{MSD}$, is the share of $\hat{V}_A$ that recurrent mutation maintains, and the reciprocal of the second, $1/c_\text{div} = \hat{V}_A/V_{A,\delta}$, is the share of the deficit that $\hat{V}_A$ accounts for under the multiplicative bound, measured as $\ln[1/(N_e/N)_\text{pred}]/\ln(1/\delta)$ on a logarithmic scale because successive reductions in effective size multiply.} Because both thresholds \claude{are} equilibrium requirements, any required value of $c \ne 1$ implies a departure from stationarity. Table~\ref{tab:c} summarizes how the two verdicts combine when evaluated at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

\claude{A} single long-term scaling factor $c$ can satisfy both tests if and only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, which requires
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{or equivalently,} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat}\claude{ does not involve the pedigree estimate $\hat{V}_A$.} Even under the multiplicative bound, fitness variance maintained at MSDB cannot reduce neutral diversity at unlinked sites below a fraction $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}$ of its neutral expectation. \claude{That fraction is close to one because each mutation contributes only in proportion to its effect.} At a typical vertebrate value of $U_d\bar{s} = 0.01$, for example, compatibility under random mating requires $\delta \ge 0.96$, \claude{a loss of at most $4\%$ of diversity}. \claude{Condition}~\eqref{eq:compat}\claude{ therefore restates, for a population with a measured mutation rate, DFE and diversity, the known result that variance maintained by recurrent mutation removes little neutral diversity at unlinked sites}~\cite{charlesworth2012}\claude{. The pedigree estimate adds only where each population lies relative to the two thresholds, and hence the two shares defined with them. If $\hat{V}_A$ overstated the long-term variance by a factor $1/c_\text{MSD}$, the maintenance test would be passed and only the known result would remain.}

\subsection{Extensions: mating system and linkage}\label{sec:extensions}\label{sec:mating}\label{sec:linkage}
\claude{Under social monogamy the nest is the unit of productivity, so a parent's family size depends on its social partner's breeding value as well as its own, and extra-pair paternity reassigns a fraction $\alpha$ of a nest's offspring to outside sires without changing how many the nest raises. Expressing each parent's long-term contribution as a regression on its own and its partner's breeding values}~\cite{woolliams1999}\claude{, and averaging over the sexes}~\cite{santiago1995}\claude{, gives (Appendix}~\ref{app:B_kappa}\claude{)}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}. \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
\claude{Here $V_A$ is the variance common to both sexes that Bonnet et al.~estimate, and the mating-system scalar $\kappa(\alpha)$ is the variance in long-term contributions averaged over the sexes, in units of its random-mating value $4V_A$, so it is the factor by which the mating system amplifies the drift that a given $V_A$ causes.} Under random mating, $\kappa = 1$. \claude{Under social monogamy, with a male's extra-pair siring success proportional to his breeding value (Model~B, Appendix}~\ref{app:B_kappa}\claude{),}
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2. \label{eq:kappaB}
\end{equation}
\claude{Without} extra-pair paternity ($\alpha = 0$), social partners share every offspring equally\claude{, and with it each other's advantage, which doubles the variance of long-term contributions}; $\kappa = 2$ recovers Santiago and Caballero's result for pair-mated families. As extra-pair paternity increases, a male's reproductive success \claude{depends less on his social partner's breeding value and more on his own.} Consequently, $\kappa$ decreases monotonically, reaching $9/8$ at $\alpha = 1$\claude{, which stays above the random-mating value because a female's family size still depends on her social partner. Extra-pair siring independent of breeding value (Model~A) gives lower values (Appendix}~\ref{app:B_limits}\claude{). Under the multiplicative model the reduction lies between two exponentials (Appendix}~\ref{app:B_mult}\claude{),}
\begin{equation}
   e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa(\alpha) V_A}, \label{eq:ne_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
\claude{and the multiplicative bound takes the left-hand side, with $\kappa_\text{max} = 2$ whatever the rate of extra-pair paternity. Because $\kappa_\text{max}$ is at most 2, the mating system changes the variance that a given deficit requires at most twofold.}

When a neutral site is physically linked to selected loci, recombination separates them less frequently than independent assortment separates unlinked sites. Consequently, unless purifying selection purges the deleterious allele first, these associations outlast \claude{the halving per generation at unlinked sites ($Q = 2$) and lower a local effective size that varies along the genome}. \claude{This local reduction requires two inputs that a pedigree does not supply, each chromosome's share of the variance and how long its associations persist.} Standard models of background selection derive both inputs from MSDB~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024,marsh2026}. Here, by contrast, the pedigree estimate supplies the total variance and the genome annotation \claude{sets} each chromosome's share, $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is the proportion of functional sequence on chromosome $i$. \claude{Only the persistence requires MSDB, through the retention factor $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation}~\eqref{eq:Z}\claude{) weighted by its equilibrium share of $V_A$, $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (Appendix}~\ref{app:A_Z}\claude{). The linked term is therefore a hybrid, in which the measured variance sets the size of the local reduction and the DFE sets how fast it decays, so it predicts a larger reduction than MSDB whenever $\hat{V}_A > U_d\bar{s}$ and recovers the MSDB prediction at $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$ (}\autoref{sec:conditions}\claude{).} For clarity, the expressions below are written for a single effect class with retention factor $Z$.

Because pedigree linkage maps resolve whole-chromosome genetic lengths far more reliably than fine-scale recombination landscapes, we treat the chromosome as our focal unit. We place the neutral site and selected loci uniformly along chromosome $i$, of genetic length $M_i$ Morgans, and average the resulting reduction across all pairwise positions. \claude{Linkage disequilibrium between a neutral site and a selected locus separated by $\ell$ Morgans is retained across a single generation of recombination and selection in the proportion $\rho = (1 - r(\ell))Z$, the linked counterpart to the unlinked segregation factor of $\tfrac12$, where $r(\ell)$ is the recombination fraction under Haldane's mapping function.} The \claude{segment's cumulative transmission factor, which replaces $Q$ in its long-term contribution, is therefore} $1/(1-\rho)$, which recovers $Q = 2$ at the unlinked, neutral-retention limit ($r = \tfrac12, Z = 1$). \claude{This factor is the expected number of generations that a neutral copy stays associated with a selected allele $\ell$ Morgans away, until recombination separates them or selection removes the allele, and for an unlinked allele it is the two generations of $Q$. Its square, averaged over where the neutral site and the selected alleles lie on chromosome $i$, is $\overline{Q^2}_i$, which has a closed form under Haldane's map (Equation}~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg}\claude{, Appendix}~\ref{app:chromosapp}\claude{).}

\claude{At linked sites the ancestor-centered and gene-centered perspectives part. A neutral copy stays associated only with the selected alleles on its own haplotype, which carries half of the chromosome's variance ($v_h = v_i/2$), and only its carrier's own breeding value acts on them, with weight $\kappa_\text{cis}$ in the carrier's family size. The homologous haplotype, the social partner and the other chromosomes assort independently of the copy, so they contribute the residual variance $\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h$ at unlinked sites, where $\kappa_\text{own}$ is the carrier's own share of $\kappa$ (Appendix}~\ref{app:B_split}\claude{; under random mating and Model~B, $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own} = 1$).} Assuming selected loci are distributed independently along the genetic map so that their effects multiply~\cite{hudson1995,santiago2016}, and that fitness is multiplicative across loci (\autoref{sec:onemodel}), the local and global reductions combine additively in the exponent~\cite[Supplementary Equations~28--29]{buffaloKern2024}; \cite[cf.][]{santiago1998}:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\Big\{-\Big[\underbrace{\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h}_{\text{local factor (\textit{cis})}} + \underbrace{Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)}_{\text{global factor (rest of the genome)}}\Big]\Big\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
where $Q = 2/(2 - Z)$ evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} at the same retention factor as the local term. Across a DFE, $\overline{Q^2}_i$ and $Q^2$ are replaced by their averages over the variance weights $\nu_k$. To evaluate the multiplicative bound, $\kappa_\text{max}$ replaces $\kappa$ (see Appendix~\ref{app:omega} for the corresponding small-variance approximation). Because the \emph{cis} haplotype variance $v_h$ is subtracted from the global term and transferred to the local term, no variance is double-counted. Normalizing Equation~\eqref{total_red} by the unlinked reduction at the same retention factor isolates \claude{the linked factor, the additional reduction that linkage causes:}
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right]. \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
\claude{Intuitively, $\Omega_i$ is the share of its diversity that a neutral site on chromosome $i$ keeps once linkage is added, and it is below one because selected alleles on the site's own haplotype stay associated with it for longer than unlinked alleles do, so their variance counts for more. Because linkage can only lengthen an association ($\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ for every effect class) and the carrier's breeding value weights the haplotype's variance equally at linked and unlinked sites ($\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$), the haplotype's variance never counts for less than it would at an unlinked site, so $\Omega_i \le 1$ (Appendix}~\ref{app:omega}\claude{).} Assuming neutral sites are distributed uniformly across the physical genome, the genome-wide effective size reduction is the average over all $n$ autosomes weighted by their physical lengths $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i. \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}
\claude{With linkage, the least variance that accounts for $\delta$ is the root of Equation}~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} \claude{set equal to $\delta$, found numerically because $\overline{\Omega}$ itself depends on $V_A$, and the compatibility of the two tests is evaluated in the same way (Methods). Because $\overline{\Omega} \le 1$, this root lies below the unlinked threshold at the same retention factor, $\ln(1/\delta)/(Q^2\kappa)$, which equals $V_{A,\delta}$ at $Z = 1$ and $\kappa = \kappa_\text{max}$.}

\subsection{Approximations biased toward passing both tests}\label{sec:approx}
\claude{We chose approximations biased toward passing each test, so a failed test, and the result that no single variance passes both ($c_\text{div} > 1 > c_\text{MSD}$), does not depend on them.}

On the diversity side, our approximations maximize the reduction in effective size generated by a given $V_A$, thereby minimizing the required threshold $V_{A,\delta}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unbounded multiplicative fitness:} \claude{The multiplicative bound places no ceiling on family size} (\autoref{sec:onemodel}).
    \item \textbf{Maximum mating-system amplification:} \claude{The bound uses $\kappa_\text{max} = 2$ under social monogamy, not the smaller $\kappa(\alpha)$} (\autoref{sec:mating}).
    \item \textbf{Complete retention at unlinked sites:} \claude{The global reduction uses $Z = 1$, so $Q = 2$ rather than $2/(1+s_k)$} (\autoref{sec:unlinked}).
    \item \claude{\textbf{Pre-breeding survival treated as fecundity:} $Q = 2$ applies to all of $\hat{V}_A$, although survival to breeding age has $Q = 1$ (}\autoref{sec:meth_va}\claude{).}
    \item \textbf{Absence of crossover interference:} \claude{Haldane's map omits interference, which raises recombination at intermediate distances and weakens linked selection.}
    \item \textbf{Empirical scaling of linked variance:} \claude{The local factor carries all of $\hat{V}_A$, not the smaller $U_d\bar{s}$ that MSDB maintains} (\autoref{sec:linkage}).
\end{itemize}

On the maintenance side, our approximation maximizes the variance that recurrent mutation can sustain, thereby maximizing $c_\text{MSD}$ and yielding the smallest possible required selection coefficient $s^*$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strong-selection limit:} \claude{$U_d\bar{s}$ is the most variance MSDB maintains at any effective size} (\autoref{sec:msdb}).
\end{itemize}

\claude{Four} factors could act in the opposite direction. First, if breeding values \claude{are strongly right-skewed rather than Gaussian, so that fitness variance is concentrated in a few extreme lineages,} the retention factor $Z$ can exceed one\claude{, because selection then widens the spread of breeding values among parents}, amplifying drift beyond the Gaussian model (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). Second, \claude{the linked model places selected and neutral sites uniformly along each chromosome, whereas clustering of functional loci in low-recombination regions strengthens linked selection (Appendix~C).} \claude{Third, under the multiplicative bound a variance that fluctuates over the genealogy removes more diversity than a constant variance with the same mean (}\autoref{sec:onemodel}\claude{), so a long-term mean below $V_{A,\delta}$ could account for a deficit if the variance reached, in some generations, values well above any pedigree estimate.} Finally, on the maintenance side, the required deleterious effect $s^* = \bar{V}_A/U_d$ scales inversely with the genome-wide deleterious mutation rate $U_d$. Because an underestimate of $U_d$ would inflate $s^*$, we evaluate all maintenance tests across two mutation-rate estimates and a broad range of functional target sizes derived from the genome annotations (Methods).
