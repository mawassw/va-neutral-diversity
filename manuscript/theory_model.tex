\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The long-term additive genetic variance in fitness}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, written $V_A$ hereafter, the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive success standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). Its square root is the standard deviation of the relative fitness that individuals' breeding values predict, so at $V_A = 0.1$ an individual one standard deviation above the mean expects about a third more offspring than average. Bonnet et al. estimated $V_A$ on this observed scale, by back-transforming the latent parameters of their animal model (\autoref{sec:meth_va}), so the estimates require no rescaling.

For a single variance to satisfy both tests, its average over the roughly $2N_e$ generations that the genealogy spans must equal the stationary variance of MSDB (\autoref{sec:msdb}). A pedigree estimate instead describes the cohorts and environment of a single study, and its back-transformation depends on the state of the population during the study, such as the proportion of individuals that never breed, so the same latent genetic variance would give a different $V_A$ in another environment. We therefore write the required long-term variance as
\begin{equation}
    \bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is an unknown scaling factor, which measures how representative the study is of the population and period that neutral diversity records. Because the lineages sampled for diversity move among localities, whereas a pedigree samples one, $c$ includes spatial as well as temporal departures from the long-term variance. Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists all notation.

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{sec:msdb}
At MSDB, deleterious mutations add additive genetic variance in fitness at a rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$ per generation, where $U_d$, the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate, is the mean number of new deleterious mutations in a zygote, and $s$ is the heterozygous effect on relative fitness. Under linkage equilibrium, the standing variance is $V_A = \sum_j 2q_j(1-q_j)s_j^2$ over sites segregating for a semidominant deleterious allele at frequency $q_j$, and each generation selection removes a fraction $s$ of this variance and drift a fraction $1/(2N_e)$~\cite{burger1989,charlesworth2015}. For a single effect $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant allele~\cite{kimura1969} (Appendix~\ref{app:D_stationary}) gives the equilibrium variance
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right]. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
When selection is strong relative to drift, each deleterious allele is held near its deterministic mutation--selection balance frequency, yielding~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2012}
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1). \label{eq:strong}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, a mutation adds variance in proportion to $s^2$ for the roughly $1/s$ generations before selection removes it, so it contributes in proportion to $s$, and only the number of new mutations and their mean effect matter. Because the bracketed term in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, $U_d\,s$ is the most variance MSDB can maintain at any effective size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:strong} yields the deleterious effect required to sustain a given long-term variance,
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}, \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
which we evaluate at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

Under a distribution of fitness effects (DFE), a deleterious class $k$, with heterozygous effect $s_k$, receives a fraction $\varphi_k$ of new mutations and, provided $N_es_k \gg 1$, contributes $U_d\varphi_ks_k$ to the standing variance. The classes together therefore maintain $U_d\bar{s}$, where $\bar{s}$ is the arithmetic mean of the DFE, so $s^*$ estimates that mean whatever the shape of the DFE, and nearly neutral mutations ($N_es_k \lesssim 1$) contribute less (Appendix~\ref{app:D_dfe}). We assume this strong-selection limit throughout, which reduces MSDB to mutation--selection balance (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion} treats weak selection).

The variance $V_A$ describes selection at two levels. On the phenotype, it measures directional selection, which raises the mean breeding value for fitness by $V_A$ each generation (Fisher's fundamental theorem). At MSDB, however, all selection on alleles is purifying, and the variance, the gain in mean fitness it produces each generation and the mutational loss that offsets that gain all equal $U_d\bar{s}$. We therefore use \emph{directional} only for phenotypes, and \emph{purifying} and \emph{positive} for selection against an allele and toward its fixation.

\subsection{Effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:unlinked}
Consider a neutral site that assorts independently of all selected loci, in a population of a constant number $N$ of breeding adults with an equal sex ratio and pairing at random with respect to breeding value (Appendices~A and B give general forms), and suppose that only segregation erodes its association with its selected background. We follow a neutral gene copy from an ancestor ($t = 0$) whose breeding value for log-fitness deviates from the population mean by $z - \bar{z}$. Because each subsequent carrier receives half of its breeding value from the parent transmitting the copy and half from a randomly drawn mate, the carrier's expected deviation halves each generation, to $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$ in generation $t$. For small $V_A$, the carrier's expected relative fitness is $1 + (z - \bar{z})/2^t$, and because the number of gene copies is multiplied by its carrier's relative fitness each generation, the ancestor's expected long-term contribution is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) \approx \prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\left[1 + \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2^t}\right] \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z}), \qquad Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2,
\end{equation}
yielding Robertson's cumulative transmission factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. $R$ is the share of the population's genes, many generations later, that descends from this ancestor, relative to the share of an average ancestor. The pedigree estimate $\hat{V}_A$ is the variance of the $t = 0$ factor alone, the ancestor's own relative fitness, and the later terms follow from Mendelian inheritance under the same selection over the few generations that the series spans (see below). Intuitively, $Q = 2$ means that an ancestor's descendants double its initial heritable advantage, provided selection remains roughly constant over the few generations in which that advantage decays. 

Drift at a neutral site is set by the variance in long-term contributions among ancestors~\cite{wray1990}. We express the reduction that heritable variance in fitness causes as $N_e/N$, the effective size relative to that of a neutral population of $N$ breeding adults with Poisson-distributed family sizes, in which $N_e = N$. In terms of an ancestor's expected long-term contribution $R(z)$, this ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}, \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
which follows from the probability that two lineages coalesce (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}). Two lineages are more likely to meet in an ancestor that left a large share of descendants, so the more unequal the shares, the faster lineages coalesce. Every reduction at unlinked sites here rests on this ratio, because the theories of drift under selection we use all compute $R$ and differ in how it depends on the breeding values of an ancestor and its mates (Table~\ref{tab:models}). For small $V_A$, the linear approximation $R \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$ gives $Q^2V_A$, the variance in long-term contributions that inherited fitness differences add to the Poisson baseline~\cite{robertson1961,nei1966,woolliams1993}; \cite[Equation~16]{santiago1995} (Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}), so for an unlinked site under random mating ($Q = 2$), Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} simplifies to $N_e/N = 1/(1 + 4V_A)$, which we call the \emph{small-variance approximation}.

Because Equation~\eqref{eq:ratio} comes from the asymptotic rate at which two lineages coalesce, $N_e$ is the asymptotic inbreeding effective size—which, in the Kingman coalescent limit, is the coalescent effective size $N_{eC}$ measured by neutral diversity, $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$, with per-site mutation rate $\mu$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R} relates it to the variance effective size). 

In the gene-centered perspective of Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011}, $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$ is the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the neutral copy and its carrier's selected background, and the two perspectives coincide at an unlinked site; only the gene-centered perspective extends to the other processes that break the association, selection (below) and recombination (\autoref{sec:linkage}).

Selection further erodes an ancestor's advantage by removing carriers of deleterious alleles. The retention factor $Z$ is the fraction of a neutral site's association with its selected background that survives one generation of selection, which Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} treat as constant across generations. With mates' breeding values correlated by $r_{mf}$, their cumulative transmission factor is 
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}. \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian breeding value composed of many loci of small effect under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$ exactly, because selection shifts the mean without changing the variance, so none of an ancestor's advantage is lost (Appendix~\autoref{app:A_Z}). The association with a deleterious allele, by contrast, is lost with the carriers that selection removes~\cite{nordborg1996,charlesworth2012}, so under strong selection each heterozygous effect class $k$ is retained at 
\begin{equation}
    Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
regardless of $V_A$, and an unlinked site under random mating has $Q = 2/(1 + s_k) < 2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}). Because purging erodes the association by a fraction $s_k$ each generation whereas independent assortment halves it, purging changes $Q$ little for small $s_k$. Under MSDB, $Q = 2$ is therefore an upper bound, and the process maintaining $V_A$ enters this unlinked framework only through $Z$; we thus compute the unlinked reduction at $Z = 1$ (giving an upper bound on its magnitude) and reserve $Z_k$ for linked sites (\autoref{sec:linkage}).

Because this unlinked reduction acts through the individual carrying the site rather than the haplotype on which it resides, every independently assorting neutral site experiences it equally\claude{, and at linked sites linkage adds to it (}\autoref{sec:linkage}\claude{).} Under MSDB, it converges to the limit of background selection at unlinked loci (Appendix~\ref{app:D_bgs}). Like $N$, the factors $Q$ and $Z$ refer to breeding adults (\autoref{sec:meth_va}). This model of the reduction in $N_e$ requires fixed additive effects, so that an allele's effect on fitness does not depend on its own frequency, which excludes underdominance, stabilizing selection and frequency-dependent selection. It also requires that an ancestor's advantage keep its sign over the few generations in which its contribution is settled. This is satisfied under MSDB, sweeps of positively selected alleles and positively autocorrelated fluctuating selection (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}). Additionally, the retention factor must remain constant across generations~\cite{santiago1995}, and allele frequencies at the selected loci \claude{must} change little while LD persists~\cite{barton2000}. The infinitesimal model and MSDB at unlinked sites meet this last condition, whereas alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies violate it and generate more drift than the concurrent variance in fitness predicts.

These conditions concern only the few generations over which an ancestor's contribution is settled, not the roughly $4N_e$ generations of the genealogy, over which $V_A$ may change. Because each generation contributes an inverse effective size $N/N_e(t) = 1 + 4V_A(t)$, long-term diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$~\cite{wright1938}, which over $T$ generations is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t). \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
Under the small-variance approximation, neutral diversity therefore depends only on the arithmetic mean $\bar{V}_A$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:c} and, for a constant $Z$, not on how $V_A$ varied over time. In contrast, under the multiplicative fitness model, temporal variation of $V_A$ does matter (\autoref{sec:onemodel}). A fluctuating $N(t)$ is treated in Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}.

\subsection{Large variances and the multiplicative bound}\label{sec:onemodel}
The small-variance approximation and its extension to other mating systems (\autoref{sec:mating}) require $V_A \ll 1$, because they replace the product of fitnesses across generations in $R(z)$ by a sum and so discard their compounding. Most pedigree estimates meet this condition, but inverting the approximation for the deficits observed ($N_{eC}/N \ll 1$) gives $\bar{V}_A \gg 1$, where it overstates the variance required.

Large variances therefore require a non-linear model of fitness, and the generalized linear mixed models of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} already assume one, with expected breeding success exponential in a Gaussian latent breeding value $z$ ($w \propto e^{z}$). Under a log link, the additive variance in relative fitness on the observed scale equals the latent variance, $V_A = \sigma_z^2 \equiv \mathrm{Var}(z)$, because the average derivative of expected fitness with respect to $z$ is mean fitness~\cite[Equations~15--16]{devillemereuil2016}, whereas the total genetic variance in relative fitness, $e^{\sigma_z^2} - 1$, is larger by a part that is non-additive on the observed scale. The pedigree estimate $\hat V_A$ is therefore the variance of $z$ that the multiplicative model requires, exactly for the log-link component of the animal model and approximately once its zero-inflation component is included.

Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} solved this multiplicative model exactly for parents drawn in proportion to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. Because a lineage's per-generation selective advantages sum exactly on the log scale without truncation, the long-term contribution $R$ is exponential rather than linear in $z$ ($R \propto e^{Qz}$), with $Q = 2$ in the exponent under the free-recombination limit, and Equation~\eqref{eq:ratio} evaluates to $\exp[-\mathrm{Var}(\ln R)]$. Under random mating, heritable variance therefore inflates the coalescence rate by a factor of $e^{4V_A}$~\cite{barton2011,weissman2012} (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}) and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
In the limit of small $V_A$, both Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} and the small-variance approximation converge to $1 - 4V_A$, but as $V_A$ increases, exponential compounding produces a much stronger reduction in $N_e$. The forms we use are therefore nested rather than competing. The small-variance approximation is the limit for small $V_A$ of every continuous mapping from breeding value to fitness, the multiplicative model is exact for the architecture above, which the animal model assumes, and the multiplicative bound (see below) is the largest reduction that the multiplicative model allows. Where the multiplicative model and its limit disagree, the difference measures how much a prediction depends on how loci combine to determine fitness. Under strict monogamy, where pairs form at random and family productivity scales with $e^{z + z'}$ for partners with breeding values $z$ and $z'$, the exponent doubles to $e^{-8V_A}$~\cite{weissman2012}. We write both reductions as $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$, where $\kappa_\text{max}$, the variance of long-term log-contributions in units of its random-mating value, is 2 under social monogamy and 1 under random mating; extra-pair paternity gives a smaller reduction (\autoref{sec:mating}).

The exponential reduction depends on the unbounded upper tail of the Gaussian distribution for log-fitness. A 100-fold reduction in $N_e$, for instance, comes mostly from ancestors more than four standard deviations above the mean (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}). In natural populations, ecological and physiological constraints, such as territorial saturation, clutch-size trade-offs or the bounded probability of breeding in Bonnet et al.'s zero-inflation component, truncate this tail and weaken the resulting drift. We therefore treat $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$ as the largest reduction in neutral diversity that a stationary variance $V_A$ can cause, and call it the \emph{multiplicative bound}. Unlike the linear form of Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}, however, the convexity of the exponential coalescence rate ($N/N_e(t) = e^{4\kappa_\text{max} V_A(t)}$) implies by Jensen's inequality that fluctuations around a mean $\bar{V}_A$ remove more diversity than a constant variance with that mean. Consequently, the minimum variance that accounts for a deficit (\autoref{sec:conditions}) constrains not $\bar{V}_A$ itself but the constant variance that would produce the same average coalescence rate, $(4\kappa_\text{max})^{-1}\ln \overline{e^{4\kappa_\text{max} V_A(t)}}$, where the bar denotes the average over generations. This constant variance exceeds $\bar{V}_A$ appreciably only if $V_A$ reaches, in some generations, values well above its mean. A population of $N$ breeding adults, moreover, realizes the bound only down to $N_e \approx \sqrt{N}$, because beyond that point the ancestors that produce the reduction are rarer than one per generation (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}).

\subsection{The two equilibrium tests as limits on the long-term variance}\label{sec:conditions}
Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
denote the diversity ratio, the fraction of its neutral expectation that a population's diversity retains. Our $N_e/N$ predicts $\delta$ only if heritable variance in fitness is the sole departure from the Poisson baseline, the population is at long-term equilibrium, and $N$ is the harmonic mean number of breeding adults, over the genealogy (Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}), of the population that $\pi$ samples. We distinguish this parameter from its estimate, $\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$, in which $\pi_\text{obs}$ is the observed diversity and $N_c$, a contemporary census of all adults, stands in for $N$ (\autoref{sec:pi_methods}), and we call $1/\hat{\delta}$ the \emph{deficit} (a 1,000-fold deficit leaves a thousandth of the expected diversity). The diversity ratio is usually orders of magnitude smaller~\cite{buffalo2021,lewin2026} than the demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$ of a single generation, which compounds all sources of reproductive variance and is typically 0.1--0.5~\cite{frankham1995}. Inverting the multiplicative bound at $\delta$ gives the minimum long-term variance that accounts for the deficit:
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\delta} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}}. \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
Because the multiplicative bound maximizes the reduction in $N_e$ per unit of variance, a population with $\bar{V}_A < V_{A,\delta}$ cannot owe its diversity deficit solely to heritable fitness variance at unlinked sites. The variance $V_{A,\delta}$ grows only logarithmically with the deficit, by $\ln(10)/(4\kappa_\text{max}) \approx 0.58/\kappa_\text{max}$ for each further tenfold loss of diversity, so under random mating a 100-fold deficit requires $\bar{V}_A \ge 1.15$ and a 10,000-fold deficit $\bar{V}_A \ge 2.3$ \claude{(Figure}~\ref{fig:S_required}\claude{).} Inverting the small-variance approximation instead gives a ceiling that holds only in populations retaining at least about a fifth of their neutral expectation under random mating and a ninth under strict monogamy (Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}).

In terms of $c = \bar{V}_A/\hat{V}_A$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:c}), each equilibrium imposes a one-sided threshold. Because MSDB maintains at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}), a population passes the maintenance test only if
\begin{equation}
    c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
When $c_\text{MSD} < 1$, this ratio is the largest fraction of the pedigree estimate that recurrent deleterious mutation can sustain. Conversely, heritable fitness variance accounts for the unlinked diversity deficit only if $\bar{V}_A \ge V_{A,\delta}$, so a population passes the diversity test only if
\begin{equation}
    c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\delta}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
When $c_\text{div} > 1$, it is the smallest factor by which the long-term variance must exceed the pedigree estimate to explain the missing diversity. Its reciprocal, $1/c_\text{div} = \hat{V}_A/V_{A,\delta}$, is the share of the deficit that $\hat{V}_A$ accounts for under the multiplicative bound, measured on a logarithmic scale (the logarithm of the predicted reduction, $1/(N_e/N)_\text{pred}$, relative to that of the deficit, $1/\delta$) because successive reductions in effective size multiply. Because both thresholds are equilibrium requirements, any required value of $c \ne 1$ implies a departure from stationarity. A single long-term scaling factor $c$ can satisfy both tests if and only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, which requires
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{or equivalently,} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat} does not involve the pedigree estimate. Because $U_d\bar{s}$ is the mean fitness lost to new deleterious mutations each generation, the bound $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}$ ties the maximum possible unlinked diversity loss directly to the single-generation mutational load on fitness. At a typical vertebrate value of $U_d\bar{s} = 0.01$, unlinked MSDB can reduce neutral diversity to at most $0.96$ of its neutral expectation under random mating (a $4\%$ loss); conversely, explaining even a $100$-fold deficit ($\delta = 0.01$) would require $U_d\bar{s} \ge 1.15$ under random mating—an implausible mutational decline of more than $100\%$ per generation. The condition therefore restates, for a population with a measured mutation rate, DFE, and diversity, the known result that variance maintained by recurrent mutation removes little neutral diversity at unlinked sites~\cite{charlesworth2012}. The pedigree estimate adds only where each population lies relative to the two thresholds, and so the two shares defined with them. Even if the true long-term variance were smaller than $\hat{V}_A$ by the factor $c_\text{MSD}$—small enough to be maintained at MSDB—it would still leave almost all of the diversity deficit unexplained. 

\subsection{Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}
\subsubsection{Mating system}\label{sec:mating}
Under social monogamy the nest is the unit of productivity, so a parent's family size depends on its social partner's breeding value as well as its own, and extra-pair paternity reassigns a fraction $\alpha$ of a nest's offspring to outside sires without changing how many the nest raises. For small $V_A$ the reduction becomes (Appendix~\ref{app:B_kappa})
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}, \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the variance common to both sexes that Bonnet et al.~estimate, and the mating-system scalar $\kappa(\alpha)$, the variance in long-term contributions averaged over the sexes in units of its random-mating value $4V_A$, is the factor by which the mating system amplifies the drift that a given $V_A$ causes ($\kappa = 1$ under random mating). Without extra-pair paternity ($\alpha = 0$), social partners share every offspring, and with it each other's advantage, which doubles the variance of long-term contributions ($\kappa = 2$, Santiago and Caballero's result for pair-mated families). As extra-pair paternity increases, a male's reproductive success depends less on his social partner's breeding value and more on his own, so when his extra-pair success is proportional to his breeding value (Model~B in \autoref{app:matingapp}), $\kappa$ decreases monotonically to $9/8$ at $\alpha = 1$, still above the random-mating value because a female's family size still depends on her social partner (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}). Under the multiplicative model the reduction lies between $e^{-4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}$ and $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_mult}), and the multiplicative bound takes the latter, with $\kappa_\text{max} = 2$ whatever the rate of extra-pair paternity, so the mating system changes the variance that a given deficit requires at most twofold.

\subsubsection{Linkage}\label{sec:linkage}
Recombination separates a neutral site from linked selected loci less often than independent assortment separates unlinked sites, so unless purging removes the deleterious allele first, their associations outlast the halving per generation at unlinked sites and reduce \claude{effective size further, by an amount} that varies along the genome. This \claude{reduction at linked sites} requires two inputs that a pedigree does not supply, each chromosome's share of the variance and how long its associations persist, which standard models of background selection derive from MSDB~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024,marsh2026}. Here, by contrast, the pedigree estimate supplies the total variance and the genome annotation sets each chromosome's share, $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is the proportion of functional sequence on chromosome $i$. Only the persistence requires MSDB, through the retention factor $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}) weighted by its equilibrium share of $V_A$, $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). The linked term is therefore a hybrid, in which the measured variance sets the size of the \claude{reduction at linked sites} and the DFE sets how fast it decays, so it predicts a larger reduction than MSDB whenever $\hat{V}_A > U_d\bar{s}$ and recovers the MSDB prediction at $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$. The expressions below are for a single effect class with retention factor $Z$.

We treat the chromosome as the unit of the linked reduction, because pedigree linkage maps give its genetic length reliably but resolve its fine-scale recombination landscape poorly. We place the neutral site and selected loci uniformly along chromosome $i$, of genetic length $M_i$ Morgans, averaging the reduction over all pairwise positions. LD between a neutral site and a selected locus $\ell$ Morgans apart is retained across a generation of recombination and selection in the proportion $\rho = (1 - r(\ell))Z$, where $r(\ell)$ is the recombination fraction under Haldane's mapping function. Hence the segment's cumulative transmission factor, which replaces $Q$, is $1/(1-\rho)$, which recovers $Q = 2$ at the unlinked, neutral-retention limit ($r = \tfrac12$, $Z = 1$). This factor is the expected number of generations that a neutral copy stays associated with a selected allele $\ell$ Morgans away, until recombination separates them or selection removes the allele. Its square, averaged over where the neutral site and the selected alleles lie on chromosome $i$, is $\overline{Q^2}_i$, which has a closed form under Haldane's map (Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg}, Appendix~\ref{app:chromosapp}).

At linked sites the ancestor-centered and gene-centered perspectives part, because a neutral copy stays associated only with the selected alleles on its own haplotype, which carries half of the chromosome's variance ($v_h = v_i/2$), whereas the homologous haplotype, the social partner and the other chromosomes assort independently of it and contribute at unlinked sites. If fitness is multiplicative across loci (\autoref{sec:onemodel}), the two contributions combine additively in the exponent (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}):
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\Big\{-\Big[\underbrace{\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h}_{\text{linked term (\textit{cis})}} + \underbrace{Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)}_{\text{unlinked term (rest of the genome)}}\Big]\Big\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_\text{cis}$ and $\kappa_\text{own}$ weight the haplotype's variance by the carrier's own breeding value (both 1 under random mating and Model~B; Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}), and $Q = 2/(2 - Z)$ evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} at the same retention factor as the \claude{linked} term. Normalizing Equation~\eqref{total_red} by the unlinked reduction at the same retention factor isolates the linked factor, the additional reduction in $N_e$ that linkage across chromosomes causes:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right]. \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, $\Omega_i$ is the share of its diversity that a neutral site on chromosome $i$ keeps once linkage is added. It cannot exceed one, because linkage can only lengthen an association ($\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ for every effect class) and the carrier's breeding value weights the haplotype's variance equally at linked and unlinked sites ($\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$; Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). With neutral sites uniform across the physical genome, the genome-wide reduction is the average over the $n$ autosomes weighted by their physical lengths $L_i$, which sum to $L_\text{total}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i. \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}
\subsection{Approximations biased toward passing both tests}\label{sec:approx}
We chose approximations biased toward passing each test, so a failed test, and the result that no single variance passes both ($c_\text{div} > 1 > c_\text{MSD}$), does not depend on them. \claude{On the diversity side, unbounded multiplicative fitness, the largest mating-system scalar $\kappa_\text{max}$, complete retention ($Z = 1$) at unlinked sites, the transmission factor of fecundity applied to pre-breeding survival, a map without crossover interference and the full $\hat{V}_A$ in the linked term each maximize the reduction in effective size that a given $V_A$ causes, and so minimize $V_{A,\delta}$. On the maintenance side, the strong-selection limit and a target in which every site, synonymous sites included, is deleterious maximize the variance that recurrent mutation maintains, and so maximize $c_\text{MSD}$ (Supplementary Methods justifies each approximation).} Four factors could act in the opposite direction. First, if breeding values are strongly right-skewed rather than Gaussian, so that fitness variance is concentrated in a few extreme lineages, selection inflates their variance  among parents and $Z$ can exceed one, amplifying drift beyond the Gaussian model (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). Second, the linked model places selected and neutral sites uniformly along each chromosome, whereas clustering of functional loci in low-recombination regions strengthens linked selection (Appendix~C). Third, under the multiplicative bound a fluctuating variance removes more diversity than its mean (\autoref{sec:onemodel}). Finally, an underestimate of $U_d$ would inflate $s^* = \bar{V}_A/U_d$, so we evaluate all maintenance tests across two mutation-rate estimates and a broad range of functional target sizes derived from the genome annotations (Methods).
