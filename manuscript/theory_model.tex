\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The long-term additive variance in fitness}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity throughout is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness $w$, $V_A(w)$, shortened to $V_A$. Equivalently, it is the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive success, standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). It is the $C^2$ of Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} under random mating, whereas with pair-mated families their $C^2$ is the variance of family fitness, $2V_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}); the variance of the breeding value $z$ for log-fitness in the multiplicative model of Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} (\autoref{sec:onemodel}); and, at MSDB, $V_A = \sum_j 2q_j(1-q_j)s_j^2$, summed over sites segregating for a semidominant deleterious allele at frequency $q_j$ with heterozygous effect $s_j$ on relative fitness (\autoref{sec:msdb}). Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} back-transformed their estimates to the observed scale of relative fitness with the multivariate \textit{delta} method, $V_A = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive genetic covariance matrix of the model's latent predictors and $\mathbf{d}$ the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to them, so the estimates need no rescaling.

The same variance describes selection at two levels. On phenotypes it is directional selection, which changes the mean breeding value of fitness by $V_A$ each generation (Introduction). On alleles, at MSDB, it is purifying selection at every locus, so directional selection on a phenotype implies no positive selection on any allele. We use \emph{directional} only for phenotypes, and \emph{purifying} and \emph{positive} for selection against an allele and toward its fixation. The global reduction in effective size depends on the alleles only through the retention factor $Z$ and two conditions on their effects and frequencies (\autoref{sec:unlinked}). The maintenance test and the local reduction at linked sites depend on the alleles directly, and we take them to be at MSDB (\autoref{sec:msdb}, \autoref{sec:linkage}).

Both tests require the long-term value of $V_A$. Neutral diversity, $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$, where $\mu$ is the mutation rate per site, measures the coalescent effective size $N_{eC}$ and reflects genealogies spanning around $4N_e$ generations. Santiago and Caballero's result gives the per-generation variance effective size $N_{eV}$, and the two coincide only at long-term demographic and selective equilibrium. MSDB describes a stationary variance (\autoref{sec:msdb}). A pedigree estimate, however, covers the cohorts of one study, a few decades, in the environments they met. That scope is built into the estimator: $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, so the same $\mathbf{G}$ in another environment gives another $V_A$. We therefore write the long-term variance as
\begin{equation}
    \bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is unknown.

Biologically, $c$ is how representative the study period is of the population's evolutionary past. $c >1 $ means the study's environments dampened the expression of heritable fitness differences, or the estimate is too low, and $c <1 $ means they amplified it, or the estimate is inflated. What we can compute is the value of $c$ each model requires, the factor by which the pedigree estimate must be multiplied for the model to reproduce the data. A required value other than one means either that the study period is unrepresentative or that the model leaves out processes acting in the population, and we cannot tell which based on a single test. Maintenance sets an upper limit on $c$, and diversity a lower one (\autoref{sec:conditions}). Because a larger $\bar{V}_A$ removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, the two limits move in opposite directions. A population has only one $c$, so if the lower limit lies above the upper limit, no degree of representativeness reconciles them, and the gap between them measures what the models leave out.

Throughout, $N_e/N$ denotes the reduction in effective size caused by heritable variance in fitness alone. The reference is a population of $N$ breeding adults whose family sizes follow a Poisson distribution, the neutral Wright--Fisher baseline, for which $N_e = N$, and $N_e$ is the long-term or asymptotic $N_{eV}$. The demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$, estimated from variance in family size, the sex ratio and fluctuations in census size, is typically 0.1--0.5~\cite{frankham1995}, and counts every source of reproductive variance, heritable or not. The diversity ratio, $N_{eC}/N$, is usually orders of magnitude smaller~\cite{buffalo2021}. Our $N_e/N$ predicts it only if heritable variance in fitness is the only departure from the Poisson baseline, the population is at long-term equilibrium, and the census counts breeding adults only. Bonnet et al.'s estimates are variances among all individuals recorded in each pedigree, most of which never breed (Methods), whereas $N$, $Q$ and $Z$ refer to the breeding adults that contribute offspring. Mortality before breeding that does not depend on breeding value leaves the additive variance in expected relative fitness unchanged; heritable survival before breeding we treat with the rest of the variance and return to in the Discussion. We assume a constant $N$ with an equal sex ratio and pairing at random with respect to breeding value. Appendices~A and B give the general forms, and Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation.

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{sec:msdb}
At MSDB, new deleterious mutations supply additive variance in fitness at the rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$ per generation, the mutational variance, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous effect on relative fitness. Each generation, selection removes a fraction $s$ of the standing variance $V_A$, and drift removes a fraction $1/(2N_e)$~\cite{burger1989,charlesworth2015}. For a single effect $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant allele~\cite{kimura1969} (Appendix~\ref{app:D_stationary}) gives the equilibrium variance
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right]. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
When selection is weak relative to drift ($N_es \ll 1$), MSDB reduces to the mutation--drift variance $2N_eU_ds^2$~\cite{lynch1986}. When it is strong,\footnote{We use strong and weak only for selection relative to drift. The rate $s$ at which selection removes a deleterious allele we call fast or slow purging, and we describe $V_A$ itself as small or large relative to one.} each deleterious allele is held near frequency $\mu/s$ and
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1) \label{eq:strong}
\end{equation}
~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2012}. The bracketed term in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, so $U_d\,s$ is the most variance MSDB can maintain at any effective size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:strong} gives the deleterious effect that a long-term variance requires,
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}, \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
which we evaluate at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

Under a distribution of fitness effects (DFE), class $k$ receives a fraction $\varphi_k$ of new mutations and, when selection is strong, contributes $U_d\varphi_ks_k$. The total is $U_d\bar{s}$, so $s^*$ estimates the arithmetic mean $\bar{s}$ of the DFE, whatever its shape; nearly neutral mutations contribute less (Appendix~\ref{app:D_dfe}). We use the strong-selection limit throughout. Because $U_d\bar s$ is the maximum, setting weakly selected mutations aside can only overstate what MSDB maintains, and a finding that $\bar{V}_A$ is too large to be maintained is then conservative. A variance carried mainly by weakly selected mutations would instead require $N_e \ll U_d/(2V_A)$, an effective size of a few individuals at the values of $\hat{V}_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion}; Results).

\subsection{Effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:unlinked}
We first take a neutral site that assorts independently of every selected locus, under the assumptions of \autoref{sec:quantity}, and suppose that the association between the site and its selected background is eroded only by segregation. Follow a neutral gene copy carried by an ancestor whose breeding value for log-fitness exceeds the mean by $z - \bar{z}$, and count generations after the ancestor by $t$, with $t = 0$ the ancestor's generation. Each carrier of the copy receives half its breeding value from the parent that passed the copy on and half from a mate drawn at random, so the expected excess of the carrier in generation $t$ is $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$. Each generation, the number of copies is multiplied by its carrier's expected relative fitness, $1 + (z - \bar{z})/2^t$ for a small excess, so the ancestor's expected long-term contribution is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) \approx \prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\left[1 + \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2^t}\right] \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z}), \qquad Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2,
\end{equation}
Robertson's cumulative transmission factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}.

Drift at a neutral site is set by how much long-term contributions vary among ancestors~\cite{wray1990}, and inherited fitness differences add $Q^2V_A$ to that variation beyond the Poisson expectation~\cite{robertson1961,nei1966,woolliams1993}; \cite[Equation~16]{santiago1995} (Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}). For an unlinked site with $Q = 2$ and random mating, Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} is therefore $N_e/N = 1/(1 + 4V_A)$. From the gene's point of view rather than the ancestor's, $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$ is the association between the copy and the selected background of its carrier in generation $t$, which is the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the neutral site and the selected loci, summed over loci with weights equal to their effects on fitness. $Q$ is that association summed over the generations it persists~\cite{barton2011}. The two perspectives coincide at an unlinked site, and only the gene-centered perspective extends to what else breaks the association: selection (below) and recombination (\autoref{sec:linkage}).

Selection also erodes an ancestor's advantage, by removing the carriers of deleterious alleles. We write $Z$ for the retention factor: the fraction of a neutral site copy's association with its selected background that survives one generation of selection. With mates whose breeding values are correlated by $r_{mf}$, Santiago and Caballero's~\cite{santiago1995} factor is
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}. \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian breeding value built from many loci of small effect, under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$ exactly, the case above (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). For a deleterious allele the association is lost with the carriers that selection removes~\cite{nordborg1996}; \cite[Appendix]{charlesworth2012}, so when selection is strong each effect class $k$ is retained at
\begin{equation}
    Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
whatever the size of $V_A$, and its unlinked factor is $Q = 2/(1 + s_k) < 2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}). Intuitively, purging erodes the association by a fraction $s_k$ each generation, but at an unlinked site recombination already halves it each generation, so for small $s_k$ purging changes $Q$ little. Under MSDB, $Q = 2$ is therefore an upper bound, and the process that maintains $V_A$ affects the reduction at unlinked sites only through $Z$. We compute the global reduction at $Z = 1$; the retention factor $Z_k$ is used in the local reduction of \autoref{sec:linkage}.

The reduction requires fixed additive effects: each selected allele changes fitness by an amount that does not depend on its own frequency, which excludes underdominance, stabilizing selection and frequency-dependent selection. Within that regime, an ancestor's expected contribution is settled within about ten generations~\cite{barton2011}, and mostly within the first two, because the series for $Q$ is dominated by its first terms, so the sign of a lineage's advantage must persist for only about two generations. Inherited advantages therefore increase drift at MSDB, while beneficial alleles sweep, and under fluctuating selection that is positively autocorrelated across generations. Selection that reverses direction every generation instead cuts the advantage short, because the terms of the series alternate in sign and $Q = \sum_{t}(-\tfrac{1}{2})^t = 2/3$. Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} gives the size of that increase only if the retention factor is the same from one generation to the next~\cite{santiago1995} and allele frequencies at the selected loci change little while the LD persists~\cite{barton2000}. The infinitesimal model meets the second condition for any kind of selection, and so does MSDB at unlinked sites, where each deleterious allele is held near its equilibrium frequency; alleles that sweep between extreme frequencies do not, and cause more drift than the variance in fitness indicates~\cite{barton2000}. Neutral diversity accumulates this drift over the coalescent window of \autoref{sec:quantity}. If $V_A$ changes over time, each generation contributes its own $N/N_e(t) = 1 + 4V_A(t)$, and diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$~\cite{wright1938}. Over $T$ generations,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t), \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
so diversity responds to $\bar{V}_A$, the target of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}, and, at a constant $Z$, to nothing else about the trajectory of $V_A$. If $N$ also varies, the same holds for its harmonic mean when $V_A(t)$ and $1/N(t)$ are uncorrelated. When they covary, the long-term variance of Equation~\eqref{eq:c} is the mean of $V_A(t)$ weighted by $1/N(t)$, because the generations in which the population is small dominate both the harmonic mean and the drift that heritable variance adds.

Every neutral site shares this reduction, because it acts through the individual that carries the site, not through the haplotype on which the site sits. We call the resulting effective size the global effective size. At MSDB it is also what background selection becomes at unlinked loci (Appendix~\ref{app:D_bgs}).

\subsection{Separate sexes, social monogamy and extra-pair paternity}\label{sec:mating}
The reduction above treats the population as hermaphrodite. With separate sexes, Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} average the selective variance over the sexes rather than adding it (Appendix~\ref{app:B_kappa}). What sets a parent's family size, however, depends on the mating system. Under random mating, each offspring's two parents are drawn independently, and a parent's family size depends on its own breeding value alone. Under social monogamy, the nest is the unit of productivity, so a parent's family size also depends on its social partner's breeding value. In socially monogamous birds, extra-pair paternity moves a fraction $\alpha$ of a nest's offspring to other sires without changing how many offspring the nest raises. Applying the expected long-term contributions of Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva~\cite{woolliams1999} to this mating system (Appendix~B) gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}, \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is common to both sexes, as Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} estimate it, and $\kappa(\alpha)$ is a mating-system scalar. Under random mating, $\kappa = 1$. Under social monogamy, we take a male's extra-pair success to be proportional to his breeding value, so that it weighs on his breeding success as much as a female's does on hers, as a single variance for both sexes requires. Then
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2. \label{eq:kappaB}
\end{equation}
Without extra-pair paternity, both partners share every offspring of the nest, and $\kappa(0) = 2$ recovers Santiago and Caballero's pair-mated families. As extra-pair paternity rises, a male's success depends more on his own breeding value and less on his partner's, and $\kappa$ falls, to $9/8$ at $\alpha = 1$. If extra-pair siring is instead independent of male breeding value, $\kappa$ is lower; we use that alternative as a sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}, Figure~\ref{fig:S_kappa}).

\subsection{Large variances and the multiplicative bound}\label{sec:onemodel}
Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} and its random-mating case, which we call the small-variance approximation, hold only while $V_A \ll 1$, because the product of per-generation fitnesses in $R(z)$ was approximated by a sum. Larger variances require a specific model of fitness, which the pedigree estimates already supply. The animal model of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} is multiplicative, with expected breeding success exponential in a Gaussian breeding value, so the reported $V_A$ is approximately the variance of $z$ with $w \propto e^{z}$, exactly so for a pure log link~\cite[Equations~15--16]{devillemereuil2016}.

Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} solved this model for parents chosen in proportion to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. On the log scale a lineage's per-generation advantages add exactly, so $Q = 2$ appears in an exponent, and under random mating heritable variance increases the rate of coalescence by $e^{4V_A}$~\cite{barton2011,weissman2012} (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
For small $V_A$, both this and the small-variance approximation are $1 - 4V_A$, and as $V_A$ grows Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} gives the larger reduction. Under strict monogamy, with pairs formed at random and offspring number in proportion to $e^{z + z'}$, the effect doubles to $e^{-8V_A}$~\cite{weissman2012}, the counterpart of Santiago and Caballero's pair-mated families. Their model has neither separate sexes nor extra-pair paternity, and with both the reduction lies between two exponentials (Appendix~\ref{app:B_mult}),
\begin{equation}
   e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa(\alpha) V_A}, \label{eq:ne_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_\text{max}$ is the largest value $\kappa$ takes in a mating system: 2 under social monogamy, the value of $\kappa(\alpha)$ at $\alpha = 0$, and 1 under random mating.

The multiplicative result rests on the Gaussian upper tail of log-fitness: a 100-fold reduction is carried by ancestors more than four standard deviations from the mean (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}). Any limit on fitness cuts that tail off, whether a biotic limit on territory, trade-offs in clutch size, or the bounded probability of breeding in Bonnet et al.'s zero-inflation component, whose variance we treat as log-additive. We therefore treat $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$ as an upper bound on the reduction in neutral diversity that a given $V_A$ can produce, and call it the multiplicative bound. It also covers a variance that fluctuates: because the reduction accelerates with $V_A$, a fluctuating variance removes at least as much diversity as a constant one with the same mean (by Jensen's inequality).

\subsection{Local effective size at linked sites}\label{sec:linkage}
When a neutral site is physically linked to selected loci, recombination separates them less often than segregation separates unlinked sites, so unless selection removes the selected allele first, their associations last longer than the two generations of the global reduction and depress a local effective size that varies along the genome. The unit that keeps the association is then the chromosome segment rather than the individual, and this changes what a pedigree can supply. A pedigree measures variance among individuals, which is all the global reduction requires. The local reduction requires two inputs defined on chromosome segments: how much variance each chromosome carries, and how long its associations last. Models of background selection take both from MSDB~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Here the pedigree estimate supplies the total and the genome annotation each chromosome's share: chromosome $i$ carries $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is its share of the genome's functional sequence. How long an association lasts cannot be measured from a pedigree, so it is the only parameter that requires the MSDB assumption, through the retention factor $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}), with classes weighted by the share of $V_A$ each carries at equilibrium, $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). The local effective size, unlike the global one, therefore depends on the process that maintains $V_A$. Because $Z_k$ does not depend on $V_A$ when selection is strong, using $\hat{V}_A$ changes the size of the local reduction but not its form, and when $\hat{V}_A$ exceeds what MSDB can maintain ($s^* > \bar{s}$) it gives the larger reduction. Setting $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$ instead recovers the prediction of MSDB alone (\autoref{sec:conditions}). The expressions below are written for a single class with retention factor $Z$.
% UNVERIFIED: Murphy et al. 2023 (Murphy, Elyashiv, Amster and Sella, eLife?) not Crossref-checked and not in references.bib.

Pedigree linkage maps give the length of each chromosome far better than the variation in recombination along it, so we treat the chromosome as the unit. The neutral site and the selected loci are placed uniformly along chromosome $i$, of map length $M_i$ Morgans, and the reduction is averaged over their positions. On a whole chromosome, pairs of sites lie up to $M_i$ apart, often more than 0.5 M, where a linear map, $r = \ell$, would give recombination fractions above $\tfrac12$. We therefore use Haldane's mapping function, $r(\ell) = \tfrac12(1 - e^{-2\ell})$, which approaches $\tfrac12$ at large distances.

LD between a neutral site and a selected locus $\ell$ Morgans away is retained over one generation of recombination and selection in the proportion $\rho = (1 - r(\ell))Z = \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})$, the linked counterpart of the factor $\tfrac12$ in $Q$. The segment's cumulative advantage is therefore $1/(1-\rho)$, which returns $Q = 2$ at $r = \tfrac12$ and $Z = 1$. Squaring it and averaging over positions gives (Appendix~\ref{app:C_kernel})
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_i = \frac{2}{M_i^2}\int_0^{M_i} (M_i - \ell)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})}\right)^2 d\ell. \label{eq:haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
The factor inside the integral is the one familiar from background selection theory: at $Z = 1 - s$, it is $1/[s + r(1-s)]$~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,santiago1998,santiago2016,buffaloKern2024}. Buffalo and Kern~\cite{buffaloKern2024} evaluate it at each position along a fine-scale recombination map, whereas averaging over positions gives the expected reduction on a chromosome when only its map length is known. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1998} and Buffalo and Kern~\cite[Supplementary Equations~32--33]{buffaloKern2024} give such averages on a linear map; we use Haldane's map and also average over the position of the neutral site. Appendix~C gives Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} in closed form, its limits, and the effect of clustering selected loci in regions of low recombination.

This is where the two perspectives of \autoref{sec:unlinked} diverge, because the homologous haplotype and a social partner's breeding value count in the individual's contribution but do not travel with the gene copy. A neutral allele is linked in cis only to the selected loci on its own haplotype, because associations with the homologous chromosome are broken at meiosis~\cite{buffaloKern2024}, so of the variance $v_i$ the focal haplotype carries $v_h = v_i/2$, and only the carrier's own breeding value, not its partner's, counts in cis. We write $\kappa_\text{cis}$ for its weight in the carrier's family size and $\kappa_\text{own}$ for its share of $\kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}$; both are 1 under the mating model of \autoref{sec:mating} (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}). The rest of the genome, the homologous haplotype and the other chromosomes, assorts independently of the focal site and contributes the global reduction at the remaining variance, $\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h$. Treating selected loci as independent along the map, so that their effects multiply~\cite{hudson1995,santiago2016}, and fitness as multiplicative across loci (\autoref{sec:onemodel}), the local and global factors combine in a single exponential~\cite[Supplementary Equations~28--29]{buffaloKern2024}; \cite[cf.][]{santiago1998}:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\Big\{-\Big[\underbrace{\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h}_{\text{local factor (cis)}} + \underbrace{Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)}_{\text{global factor (rest of the genome)}}\Big]\Big\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
where $Q = 2/(2 - Z)$ is Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} at the same retention factor as the local factor, and $Q^2$ is the limit of $\overline{Q^2}_i$ as $M_i \to \infty$. For the multiplicative bound, $\kappa_\text{max}$ replaces $\kappa$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega} gives the small-variance approximation).

The cis variance moves from the global factor to the local factor, so nothing is counted twice, and on a freely recombining chromosome Equation~\eqref{total_red} reduces to the global reduction $e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}$. By linked selection we mean the local factor only. Relative to the global reduction at the same retention factor, it is
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right], \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
which does not exceed one, because $\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ in every effect class and $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). With neutral sites spread uniformly over the physical genome, the genome-wide values are averages over the $n$ autosomes weighted by physical length $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i. \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The two tests as limits on the long-term variance}\label{sec:conditions}
Inverting the multiplicative bound gives the smallest variance that could account for a given loss of neutral diversity. Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
be the diversity ratio of \autoref{sec:quantity}. At equilibrium, setting the bound equal to $\delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\delta} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}}. \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
Because the bound overestimates the reduction, a population whose $V_A$ is less than $V_{A,\delta}$ cannot owe its missing diversity to heritable variance in fitness at unlinked sites. $V_{A,\delta}$ grows only logarithmically as diversity decays. Each further tenfold loss of diversity requires only $\ln(10)/(4\kappa_\text{max}) \approx 0.58/\kappa_\text{max}$ more variance. A 100-fold deficit, for example, requires $V_A \ge 1.15$ under random mating, and a 10,000-fold deficit 2.3. For a population that retains at least $1/(1 + 4\kappa)$ of its neutral expectation, about a fifth under random mating and a ninth under strict monogamy, the small-variance approximation also gives a ceiling in the strict sense, $V_{A,\max}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax}, Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}), above which a measured variance would remove more diversity than is observed; for the deficits of wild vertebrates it lies above one, outside the range in which it holds. With linkage, the variance that accounts for $\delta$ is the root of Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} set equal to $\delta$, found numerically because $\overline{\Omega}$ itself depends on $V_A$ (Methods). Since $\overline{\Omega} \le 1$, it is at most the unlinked value at the same retention factor, $\ln(1/\delta)/(Q^2\kappa)$, which is $V_{A,\delta}$ at $Z = 1$ with $\kappa_\text{max}$.

On the scale of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}, the two tests set limits on $c$. MSDB maintains at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}), so the maintenance test is passed only for
\begin{equation}
c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A},
\end{equation}
which below one is the share of the estimated variance that recurrent mutation can maintain. Heritable variance in fitness accounts for a deficit at unlinked sites only if $\bar{V}_A$ reaches $V_{A,\delta}$, so the diversity test is passed only for
\begin{equation}
c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\delta}}{\hat{V}_A},
\end{equation}
which above one is the factor by which the long-term variance must exceed the estimate. Both tests are equilibrium statements, so the required values of $c$ also diagnose departures from equilibrium. Table~\ref{tab:c} sets out how the two verdicts combine at the pedigree estimate.

A single $c$ passes both tests only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, that is, if
\begin{equation}
\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat} contains no pedigree estimate of $V_A$. A variance maintained by recurrent deleterious mutation can reduce diversity at unlinked sites to no less than $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}$ of its neutral expectation, even under the multiplicative bound. At $U_d\bar{s} = 0.01$, for example, the condition requires $\delta \ge 0.96$ under random mating. Whether the two tests can be reconciled is therefore settled by the mutational parameters before any estimate of $V_A$ is fitted, and the pedigree estimates place each population relative to the two thresholds, at $c = 1$. With linkage, the linked variance replaces $V_{A,\delta}$, and the condition is evaluated numerically (Methods).

\subsection{Approximations biased toward passing both tests}\label{sec:approx}
Most of the approximations here are biased toward passing, so a failed test is conservative. On the diversity side, each approximation overestimates the reduction in effective size: the multiplicative bound in place of a reduction that places a limit on high fitness (\autoref{sec:onemodel}); $\kappa_\text{max}$ in place of $\kappa$ under social monogamy; $Q = 2$ in place of the smaller $Q$ of purged deleterious alleles (\autoref{sec:unlinked}); the attribution of all non-Poisson variance in family size to $V_A$ (\autoref{sec:quantity}); Haldane's mapping function in place of one that accounts for crossover interference, which raises recombination at a given map distance; and, in the local factor, $\hat{V}_A$ in place of $U_d\bar{s}$, spread uniformly along the map for loci placed independently of recombination rate (\autoref{sec:linkage}). A finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too small to explain a deficit is therefore conservative. A variance concentrated in a few lineages, as in a right-skewed distribution of breeding values, would go the other way, raising the retention factor above one and the reduction with it (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). On the maintenance side, the strong-selection limit overestimates what MSDB maintains (\autoref{sec:msdb}), so $s^*$ is the smallest mean effect that could maintain $\bar{V}_A$, and a finding that $\hat{V}_A$ is too large to be maintained is conservative too. The mutational target is the exception: a larger $U_d$ lowers $s^*$ in proportion, so we use two available estimates of the mutation rate and a range of target sizes built from the genome annotations (Methods).
