\section{Theory and Model}
\subsection{The long-term additive genetic variance in fitness}\label{sec:quantity}
The central quantity throughout this work is the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, written $V_A$ hereafter and defined as the variance of breeding values for lifetime reproductive success standardized to mean fitness ($\bar{w} = 1$). Santiago and Caballero's family-fitness variance $C^2$ equals $2V_A$ with pair families and $V_A$ under random mating (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}), while Barton and Etheridge's log-fitness variance is approximately $V_A$ (since $\mathrm{Var}_A(\ln w)\approx V_A(w)$ for small $V_A$; \autoref{sec:onemodel}). At MSDB under linkage equilibrium, $V_A = \sum_j 2q_j(1-q_j)s_j^2$, summed over sites segregating for a semidominant deleterious allele at frequency $q_j$ with heterozygous effect $s_j$ on relative fitness (\autoref{sec:msdb}). Bonnet et al. explicitly estimated $V_A$ on the observed scale of relative fitness by back-transforming their latent parameters via the multivariate \textit{delta} method, $V_A = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the additive genetic covariance matrix of the latent predictors and $\mathbf{d}$ is the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to them. Consequently, these empirical estimates map directly to the variance parameter required by both the drift and MSDB frameworks without rescaling. 

The same variance describes selection at two distinct levels. At the phenotypic level, it represents directional selection, which increases the mean breeding value for fitness by $V_A$ each generation (Fisher's Fundamental Theorem). At the allelic level, however, if the population is at MSDB, this variance is driven entirely by purifying selection across loci. Consequently, directional selection on the fitness phenotype does not imply positive selection on any specific allele. To maintain this distinction, we use \emph{directional} exclusively for phenotypes, and \emph{purifying} and \emph{positive} for selection against an allele and toward its fixation, respectively.

Throughout this study, $N_e/N$ denotes the reduction in effective size caused exclusively by heritable variance in fitness. The reference is \edit{a neutral population} of $N$ breeding adults with Poisson-distributed family sizes, \edit{in which} $N_e = N$. In this framework, $N_e$ \edit{is the inbreeding effective size, set by the probability that two lineages coalesce in the preceding generation (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}), and therefore the coalescent effective size $N_{eC}$ that neutral diversity measures, $\pi = 4N_{eC}\mu$, with per-site mutation rate $\mu$. The inbreeding, variance and coalescent effective sizes coincide in any population whose size and pattern of reproduction remain constant, not only under Wright--Fisher sampling~\cite{caballero1994}, and they differ when the population changes in size~\cite{crow1988}. Accordingly, the variance effective size converges to our $N_e$ over many generations when $N$ and $V_A$ are constant, whereas the variance effective size of a single generation omits the persistence of an ancestor's advantage across generations, which produces the factor $Q^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}).} While the short-term demographic ratio $N_{eV}/N$ typically falls in the range 0.1--0.5~\cite{frankham1995} by compounding all sources of reproductive variance, the diversity ratio $N_{eC}/N$ is usually orders of magnitude smaller~\cite{buffalo2021,lewin2026}. Our $N_e/N$ calculation predicts this diversity ratio only if heritable fitness variance is the sole departure from the Poisson baseline, the population is at long-term equilibrium, and \edit{$N$ is the harmonic mean number of breeding adults, over the genealogy (Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}), of the population that $\pi$ samples}. We write $N$ for breeding adults (to which $Q$ and $Z$ also refer) and $N_c$ for a total adult census\edit{, and we distinguish the diversity ratio $\delta = N_{eC}/N$, a parameter, from its estimate $\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$, which substitutes a contemporary total census for $N$ (\autoref{sec:pi_methods})}; Methods treats the component of the empirical estimates that reflects pre-breeding survival.

Testing this equilibrium requires bridging distinct theoretical timescales. Because $N_{eC}$ reflects genealogies spanning roughly $4N_e$ generations, the diversity deficit is governed by the long-term historical average of $V_A$. MSDB, conversely, describes a stationary variance at equilibrium (\autoref{sec:msdb}). Thus, for a single variance to satisfy both frameworks, the long-term historical average must equal this stationary equilibrium. A pedigree estimate, however, covers only the cohorts of a single study over a few decades. Because the derivative vector $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, this environmental and temporal limitation is built directly into the estimator; the same $\mathbf{G}$ would yield a different $V_A$ in another environment. To account for this mismatch, we write the required theoretical variance as
\begin{equation}
    \bar{V}_A = c\,\hat{V}_A, \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}_A$ is the pedigree estimate and $c$ is an unknown scaling factor.

Biologically, $c$ captures how representative the study period \edit{and the study population are of the evolutionary past of the population whose genealogy $\pi$ records. Because a pedigree covers a single locality over a few decades, whereas the lineages sampled for $\pi$ move among localities over the coalescent window, $c$ includes spatial as well as temporal departures of the pedigree estimate from the long-term variance.} Analytically, $c$ is the factor by which the empirical estimate must be multiplied to reproduce the observed data, allowing us to evaluate the exact discrepancy each model demands. Throughout, we assume a constant $N$ with an equal sex ratio and random pairing with respect to breeding value; Appendices~A and B provide general forms, and Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists all notation.

\subsection{The variance maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance}\label{sec:msdb}
At MSDB, the influx of deleterious mutations generates additive genetic variance in fitness at a rate $V_m = U_d\,\mathbb{E}[s^2]$ per generation, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ is the heterozygous effect on relative fitness. Each generation, selection removes a fraction $s$ of the standing variance $V_A$, while drift removes a fraction $1/(2N_e)$~\cite{burger1989,charlesworth2015}. For a single effect $s$, the diffusion solution for a semidominant allele~\cite{kimura1969} (Appendix~\ref{app:D_stationary}) gives the equilibrium variance
\begin{equation}
    V_A = U_d\,s\left[1 - \frac{4N_es}{e^{4N_es} - 1}\right]. \label{eq:VA_MSD}
\end{equation}
When selection is weak relative to drift ($N_es \ll 1$), MSDB reduces to the mutation--drift variance $2N_eU_ds^2$~\cite{lynch1986}. When selection is strong relative to drift, each deleterious allele is held near its deterministic mutation--selection balance frequency, yielding~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2012}
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d\,s \qquad (N_es \gg 1). \label{eq:strong}
\end{equation}
Notably, assuming selection is strong relative to drift ($N_es \gg 1$) does not violate the requirement of the $N_e$ reduction models that the absolute variance remain small ($V_A \ll 1$) and individual selective effects remain weak ($s \ll 1$). Provided the population is sufficiently large, both boundary conditions are seamlessly satisfied. Because the bracketed term in Equation~\eqref{eq:VA_MSD} never exceeds one, $U_d\,s$ represents the absolute maximum variance MSDB can maintain at any effective size. Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:strong} yields the deleterious effect required to sustain a given long-term variance,
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{\bar{V}_A}{U_d}, \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
which we evaluate at the pedigree estimate ($c = 1$).

Under a distribution of fitness effects (DFE), class $k$ receives a fraction $\varphi_k$ of new mutations and, provided $N_es_k \gg 1$, contributes $U_d\varphi_ks_k$ to the standing variance. Summed across all classes, the total maintained variance is $U_d\bar{s}$. Consequently, $s^*$ recovers the arithmetic mean $\bar{s}$ of the DFE regardless of its underlying shape, while nearly neutral mutations ($N_es_k \lesssim 1$) contribute less (Appendix~\ref{app:D_dfe}). We assume this strong-selection limit throughout, effectively reducing MSDB to mutation--selection balance. Because $N_es \ll 1$ implies $V_A = 2N_eU_ds^2 \ll U_d/(2N_e)$, a variance maintained primarily by weakly selected mutations would instead require $N_e \ll U_d/(2V_A)$---an impossibly small effective size of only a few individuals given the empirical values of $\hat{V}_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_inversion}; Results).

\subsection{Effective size at unlinked sites}\label{sec:unlinked}
We first consider a neutral site that assorts independently of all selected loci, adhering to the assumptions of \autoref{sec:quantity}, and assume the association between this site and its selected background is eroded solely by segregation. Let us track a neutral gene copy originating in an ancestor ($t = 0$) whose breeding value for log-fitness deviates from the population mean by $z - \bar{z}$. Because each subsequent carrier receives half of its breeding value from the parent transmitting the copy and half from a randomly drawn mate, the carrier's expected breeding value deviation in generation $t$ halves each generation to $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$. In the weak-selection limit, this yields an expected relative fitness of $1 + (z - \bar{z})/2^t$. Because the number of gene copies is multiplied by its carrier's relative fitness each generation, the ancestor's expected long-term contribution is
\begin{equation}
    R(z) \approx \prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\left[1 + \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2^t}\right] \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z}), \qquad Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = 2,
\end{equation}
yielding Robertson's cumulative transmission factor of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. Crucially, the pedigree estimate $\hat{V}_A$ captures only the variance of the initial $t = 0$ factor---the ancestor's own variance in relative fitness. All subsequent terms are theoretically extrapolated from Mendelian inheritance. By applying the exact same fitness mapping to the descendants' decaying breeding values, this product strictly assumes that the selective landscape remains constant across all generations spanned by the lineage. Intuitively, $Q = 2$ implies that an ancestor's descendants collectively add exactly as much to its long-term genetic footprint as the ancestor's own initial reproductive success.

Drift at a neutral site is governed by the variance in long-term contributions among ancestors~\cite{wray1990}. Relative to the Poisson baseline established in \autoref{sec:quantity}, the effective population size ratio is given by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R]^2}{\mathbb{E}[R^2]}, \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
a relation derived from the probability of pairwise lineage coalescence (Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}). In this study, every reduction in effective size at unlinked sites due to heritable fitness variance relies on this ratio; our models differ only in how the expected contribution $R$ is parameterized by the breeding values of an ancestor and its mates (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Assuming small $V_A$, the linear approximation $R \approx 1 + Q(z - \bar{z})$ yields a variance of $Q^2V_A$. This term represents the exact inflation of long-term reproductive variance driven by inherited fitness differences beyond the neutral Poisson expectation~\cite{robertson1961,nei1966,woolliams1993}; \cite[Equation~16]{santiago1995} (Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}). Consequently, for an unlinked site under random mating ($Q = 2$), Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} simplifies to $N_e/N = 1/(1 + 4V_A)$. 

Shifting from the ancestor-centered perspective to the gene-centered perspective, $(z - \bar{z})/2^t$ mathematically represents the association between the neutral copy and the selected background of its carrier in generation $t$. Formally, this is the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the neutral site and the selected loci, summed over those loci and weighted by their fitness effects. In this framework, $Q$ is simply this association integrated over the generations it persists~\cite{barton2011}. Because the decay of an ancestor's breeding value via segregation perfectly mirrors the decay of LD via independent assortment, the two perspectives coincide exactly at an unlinked site. However, only the gene-centered perspective seamlessly accounts for the additional processes that break this association: selection (below) and recombination (\autoref{sec:linkage}).

Selection further erodes an ancestor's advantage by removing carriers of deleterious alleles. To quantify this, we define the retention factor $Z$ as the fraction of a neutral site's association with its selected background that survives one generation of selection. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} treat this retention factor as a constant parameter across generations. Assuming mates have breeding values correlated by $r_{mf}$, their cumulative transmission factor is
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{2}{2 - Z(1 + r_{mf})}. \label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian breeding value composed of many loci of small effect under multiplicative fitness, $Z = 1$ exactly. This mathematical identity explains why the model of Barton and Etheridge (\autoref{sec:onemodel}) requires no free retention factor (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). Conversely, because the association of a deleterious allele is lost alongside the carriers that selection removes~\cite{nordborg1996}; \cite[Appendix]{charlesworth2012}, each effect class $k$ is retained under strong selection at
\begin{equation}
    Z_k = 1 - s_k, \label{eq:Z}
\end{equation}
independent of the magnitude of $V_A$. For an unlinked site under random mating, this yields $Q = 2/(1 + s_k) < 2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:D_purge}). Intuitively, while purging erodes the association by a fraction $s_k$ each generation, independent assortment at an unlinked site already halves the association per generation. For small $s_k$, recombination vastly outpaces purging, altering $Q$ only negligibly. Consequently, under MSDB, $Q = 2$ serves as a robust upper bound, and the specific evolutionary process maintaining $V_A$ affects the unlinked $N_e$ reduction exclusively through $Z$. We therefore compute this unlinked reduction assuming $Z = 1$, reserving the explicit retention factor $Z_k$ for the local reduction at linked sites (\autoref{sec:linkage}). Because this unlinked effect acts through the individual carrying the site rather than the specific haplotype on which it resides, every independently assorting neutral site in the genome experiences it equally. We refer to this shared baseline as the global effective size. Notably, under MSDB, this global reduction converges to the limit of background selection at unlinked loci (Appendix~\ref{app:D_bgs}).

This reduction relies on fixed additive effects, requiring that an allele's fitness impact remains independent of its own frequency. This condition excludes underdominance, stabilizing selection, and frequency-dependent selection. Within this additive regime, an ancestor's expected contribution stabilizes within roughly ten generations---and overwhelmingly within the first two---because the initial terms of the $Q$ series dominate~\cite{barton2011}. Consequently, the selective advantage needs to maintain its sign for only a few generations to significantly amplify drift. This condition is met under MSDB, during beneficial sweeps, and under fluctuating selection with positive intergenerational autocorrelation. Conversely, selection that reverses direction every generation rapidly truncates this advantage; the terms of the series alternate in sign, yielding a suppressed transmission factor of $Q = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(-\tfrac{1}{2})^t = 2/3$. However, applying Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} to quantify this drift requires two additional conditions: the retention factor must remain constant across generations~\cite{santiago1995}, and allele frequencies at the selected loci must change negligibly while LD persists~\cite{barton2000}. The infinitesimal model inherently satisfies this latter condition, as does MSDB at unlinked sites, where deleterious alleles remain near their equilibrium frequencies. In contrast, alleles sweeping between extreme frequencies violate this stability, generating more drift than the concurrent fitness variance alone would predict~\cite{barton2000}.

Crucially, these restrictive conditions apply only to the brief window over which a single ancestor's contribution decays, not to the macro-evolutionary timescale of coalescence. Neutral diversity accumulates drift over roughly $4N_e$ generations (\autoref{sec:quantity}), a timescale over which $V_A$ may fluctuate wildly. Because each generation contributes an inverse effective size of $N/N_e(t) = 1 + 4V_A(t)$, long-term diversity reflects the harmonic mean of $N_e$~\cite{wright1938}. Over $T$ generations,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\tilde{N}_e}{N} = \left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{N}{N_e(t)}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\bar{V}_A}, \qquad \bar{V}_A = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_A(t). \label{eq:harmonic}
\end{equation}
This linearity demonstrates that \edit{under the small-variance approximation} neutral diversity responds strictly to the arithmetic mean $\bar{V}_A$---the explicit target of Equation~\eqref{eq:c}---and, assuming a constant $Z$, is entirely insensitive to the temporal trajectory of $V_A$. \edit{Under the multiplicative model, fluctuations remove more diversity than a constant variance with the same mean (\autoref{sec:onemodel}).} If the census size $N(t)$ also fluctuates, this relationship holds with $N$ replaced by its harmonic mean provided $V_A(t)$ and $1/N(t)$ are uncorrelated. If they covary, however, the long-term variance required by Equation~\eqref{eq:c} becomes the mean of $V_A(t)$ weighted by $1/N(t)$, because generations with small population sizes disproportionately dictate both the harmonic mean and the drift induced by heritable fitness variance.

\subsection{Separate sexes, social monogamy and extra-pair paternity}\label{sec:mating}
The preceding derivations assume a hermaphrodite population. With separate sexes, Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995} showed that the selective variance must be averaged across the sexes rather than summed (Appendix~\ref{app:B_kappa}). Furthermore, the demographic determinants of a parent's family are connected to the mating system. Under random mating, each offspring's two parents are drawn independently, meaning a parent's family size depends primarily on its own breeding value. Under social monogamy, however, the nest operates as the unit of productivity, making a parent's family size dependent on its social partner's breeding value as well. In socially monogamous birds, extra-pair paternity reassigns a fraction $\alpha$ of a nest's offspring to outside sires without altering the total number of offspring the nest raises. Because Woolliams, Bijma and Villanueva~\cite{woolliams1999} formulated an individual's expected long-term contribution as a linear regression on multiple selective advantages, their framework naturally accommodates systems where family size depends on both partner's breeding value. Applying this approach to social monogamy with extra-pair paternity (Appendix~\ref{app:B_kappa}) yields
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\kappa(\alpha)V_A}. \label{unlinked_mating}
\end{equation}
Here, $V_A$ is the additive genetic variance common to both sexes--matching the empirical estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}--and $\kappa(\alpha)$ is a novel mating-system scalar. This scalar represents the variance in long-term contributions averaged over the sexes, expressed in units of its random-mating expectation, $4V_A$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_kappa}). Equivalently, $\kappa$ quantifies the factor by which the mating system amplifies the drift induced by a given $V_A$. 

Under random mating, $\kappa = 1$. Under social monogamy, we assume a male's extra-pair siring success is proportional to his breeding value, ensuring it weighs on his total breeding success as much as a female's breeding value weighs on hers--a necessary condition when applying a single variance to both sexes requires. This gives
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(\alpha) = 2 - \tfrac{3}{2}\alpha + \tfrac{5}{8}\alpha^2. \label{eq:kappaB}
\end{equation}
In the absence of extra-pair paternity (i.e., $\alpha = 0$), social partners share every offspring equally, and $\kappa = 2$ recovers Santiago and Caballero's result for pair-mated families. As extra-pair paternity increases, a male's reproductive success becomes increasing decoupled from his social partner's breeding value and more reliant on his own. Consequently, $\kappa$ decreases monotonically, reaching $9/8$ at $\alpha = 1$. If extra-pair siring success is instead assumed to be independent of male breeding value, the scalar $\kappa$ takes lower values; we explore this alternative assumption as a sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:B_limits}, Figure~\ref{fig:S_kappa}).

\subsection{Large variances and the multiplicative bound}\label{sec:onemodel}
Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} and its random-mating case---hereafter the small-variance approximation---hold only provided $V_A \ll 1$. This restriction arises because deriving the linear relationship $R(z) \approx 1+Q(z-\bar{z})$ requires approximating the multi-generational product of fitnesses as an additive sum, discarding higher-order compounding across generations. While the empirical pedigree estimates $\hat{V}_A$ generally fall within this small-variance regime, inverting Equation~\eqref{unlinked_mating} to ask what long-term variance $\bar{V}_A$ is required to explain the massive empirical diversity deficits ($N_{eC}/N \ll 1$) demands values of $\bar{V}_A \gg 1$, where the linear approximation breaks down and overestimates the required variance. 

Evaluating large variances therefore requires an explicit, non-linear model of fitness. The generalized linear mixed models used by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} already assume a multiplicative architecture, modeling expected breeding success as exponential in a Gaussian latent breeding value $z$ ($w \propto e^{z}$). Under a pure log link, the exact variance in relative fitness on the observed scale is $V_A = e^{\sigma_z^2}-1$, where $\sigma_z^2 = \mathrm{Var}(z)$ is the latent log-fitness variance~\cite[Equations~15--16]{devillemereuil2016}. For small variances, $V_A \approx \sigma_z^2$; for large variances, $V_A > \sigma_z^2$, meaning any lower bound derived for $\sigma_z^2$ serves as an even stricter lower bound for $V_A$. 

Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} solved this exact multiplicative model for parents drawn in proportion to $e^{z}$, with $z$ additive and Gaussian. Because a lineage's per-generation selective advantages sum exactly on the log scale without truncation, the long-term contribution $R$ is exponential rather than linear in $z$ ($R \propto e^{Qz}$). Consequently, $Q = 2$ enters in the exponent, and Equation~\eqref{eq:ratio} evaluates to $\exp[-\mathrm{Var}(\ln R)]$. Under random mating, heritable variance inflates the coalescence rate by a factor of $e^{4V_A}$~\cite{barton2011,weissman2012} (treating $\sigma_z^2 \approx V_A$; Appendix~\ref{app:A_R}):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = e^{-4V_A}. \label{eq:ne_mult}
\end{equation}
In the limit of small $V_A$, both Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} and the small-variance approximation converge to $1 - 4V_A$. As $V_A$ increases, however, exponential compounding in Equation~\eqref{eq:ne_mult} produces a vastly stronger reduction in $N_e$. Under strict monogamy, where pairs form at random and family productivity scales with $e^{z + z'}$, the exponent doubles to $e^{-8V_A}$~\cite{weissman2012}, providing the multiplicative counterpart to Santiago and Caballero's pair-mated families. Extending this multiplicative framework to incorporate separate sexes and extra-pair paternity bounds the effective size reduction between two exponentials (Appendix~\ref{app:B_mult}):
\begin{equation}
   e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A} \le \frac{N_e}{N} \le e^{-4\kappa(\alpha) V_A}, \label{eq:ne_mult_pair}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_\text{max}$ is the maximum value that $\kappa$ attains under a given mating system: $\kappa_\text{max}=2$ under social monogamy (corresponding to $\kappa(\alpha)$ at $\alpha = 0$) and $\kappa_\text{max}=1$ under random mating. Like $\kappa$, $\kappa_\text{max}$ measures the variance of long-term log-contributions in units of its random-mating baseline.

Crucially, this exponential reduction relies heavily on the unbounded upper tail of the Gaussian distribution for log-fitness: for instance, a 100-fold reduction in $N_e$ is driven predominantly by extreme ancestors lying more than four standard deviations above the mean (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}). In natural populations, ecological and physiological constraints---such as territorial saturation, clutch-size trade-offs, or the bounded probability of breeding in Bonnet et al.'s zero-inflation component---inevitably truncate this upper tail, weakening the resulting drift. We therefore treat $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}V_A}$ as the theoretical maximum reduction in neutral diversity that a stationary variance $V_A$ can generate, referring to it hereafter as the \emph{multiplicative bound}. Unlike the linear regime of Equation~\eqref{eq:harmonic}, however, the convexity of the exponential coalescence rate ($N/N_e(t) = e^{4\kappa V_A(t)}$) implies by Jensen's inequality that temporal fluctuations in $V_A(t)$ around a mean $\bar{V}_A$ remove even more diversity than a stationary variance of the same mean. \edit{The least variance that accounts for a deficit (\autoref{sec:conditions}) then constrains $(4\kappa_\text{max})^{-1}\ln$ of the time average of $e^{4\kappa_\text{max} V_A(t)}$, which exceeds $\bar{V}_A$ appreciably only if the variance reaches, in some generations, values well above its mean. Moreover, a finite population realizes the bound only while $8\kappa_\text{max}V_A \lesssim \ln N$. Above that variance, the ancestors that produce the reduction are rarer than one per generation (Appendix~\ref{app:A_tail}), so the bound describes a population of $N$ breeding adults only down to $N_e \approx \sqrt{N}$, below which coalescence comes in bursts from single exceptional lineages.}

\subsection{Local effective size at linked sites}\label{sec:linkage}
When a neutral site is physically linked to selected loci, recombination separates them less frequently than independent assortment separates unlinked sites. Consequently, unless purifying selection purges the deleterious allele first, these physical associations outlast the rapid per-generation halving of the global reduction ($Q = 2$), depressing a local effective size that varies across the genome. Under linkage, the unit of inheritance that preserves the association shifts from the individual to the chromosomal segment. 

While a pedigree measures fitness variance among individuals---which suffices to parameterize the global reduction---quantifying the local reduction requires two inputs defined at the chromosomal scale: the fraction of variance carried by each chromosome, and the persistence time of its linked associations. Standard models of background selection derive both inputs from MSDB~\cite{murphy2023,buffaloKern2024,marsh2026}. Here, by contrast, the empirical pedigree estimate supplies the total variance while the genome annotation determines each chromosome's share, $v_i = \hat{V}_A f_i$, where $f_i$ is the proportion of functional sequence residing on chromosome $i$. Because a pedigree cannot resolve the persistence of allelic associations, only this second input requires MSDB, entering through the retention factor $Z_k$ of each effect class (Equation~\eqref{eq:Z}) weighted by its equilibrium share of $V_A$, $\nu_k = \varphi_ks_k/\bar{s}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). Thus, unlike the global effective size, the local effective size depends explicitly on the mechanism maintaining $V_A$. Because $Z_k$ is independent of $V_A$ under strong selection, $\hat{V}_A$ dictates the magnitude of the local reduction whereas the DFE dictates its decay kernel. This hybrid parameterization predicts a stronger reduction than pure MSDB whenever the empirical variance exceeds mutational expectations ($s^* > \bar{s}$), while setting $\bar{V}_A = U_d\bar{s}$ directly recovers the classical MSDB prediction (\autoref{sec:conditions}). For clarity, the expressions below are written for a single effect class with retention factor $Z$.
% Murphy et al. 2023 (eLife) is in references.bib as murphy2023 (Crossref-checked with the bibliography, 2026-10-27).

Because pedigree linkage maps resolve whole-chromosome genetic lengths far more reliably than fine-scale recombination landscapes, we treat the chromosome as our focal unit. We place the neutral site and selected loci uniformly along chromosome $i$, of genetic length $M_i$ Morgans, and average the resulting reduction across all pairwise positions. Across a whole chromosome, pairs of sites can lie up to $M_i$ Morgans apart---frequently exceeding $0.5$ Morgans---where a linear mapping function ($r = \ell$) yields unrealistic recombination fractions above $\tfrac12$. We therefore employ Haldane's mapping function, $r(\ell) = \tfrac12(1 - e^{-2\ell})$, which properly saturates at $\tfrac12$ over large genetic distances.

Linkage disequilibrium between a neutral site and a selected locus separated by $\ell$ Morgans is retained across a single generation of recombination and selection in the proportion $\rho = (1 - r(\ell))Z = \frac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})$, representing the linked counterpart to the unlinked segregation factor of $\tfrac12$. The chromosomal segment's cumulative transmission factor---replacing $Q$ in the segment's long-term contribution---is therefore $1/(1-\rho)$, which recovers $Q = 2$ at the unlinked, neutral-retention limit ($r = \tfrac12, Z = 1$). Squaring this factor and averaging over all pairwise chromosomal positions yields (Appendix~\ref{app:C_kernel})
\begin{equation}
    \overline{Q^2}_i = \frac{2}{M_i^2}\int_0^{M_i} (M_i - \ell)\left(\frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{Z}{2}(1 + e^{-2\ell})}\right)^2 d\ell. \label{eq:haldane_avg}
\end{equation}
Substituting $Z = 1 - s$ reduces the term inside the integral to $1/[s + r(\ell)(1-s)]$, the familiar kernel from classical background selection theory~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,santiago1998,santiago2016,buffaloKern2024}. Whereas Buffalo and Kern~\cite{buffaloKern2024} evaluate this kernel locally along a high-resolution recombination map, our spatial average yields the expected chromosome-wide reduction when only total map length $M_i$ is known. While Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1998} and Buffalo and Kern~\cite[Supplementary Equations~32--33]{buffaloKern2024} derived similar chromosome-wide averages assuming a linear map for a central or end site, Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg} incorporates Haldane's mapping function and averages over the position of the neutral site as well. Appendix~C provides the closed-form solution to Equation~\eqref{eq:haldane_avg}, its asymptotic limits, and the consequences of clustering selected loci within low-recombination regions.

Under physical linkage, the ancestor-centered and gene-centered perspectives of \autoref{sec:unlinked} formally diverge. Because neither the homologous (\emph{trans}) haplotype nor a social partner's breeding value co-segregates with the focal gene copy, the individual ancestor's long-term contribution no longer governs drift at linked sites. Meiosis immediately breaks associations with the homologous chromosome, meaning a neutral allele remains exclusively linked in \emph{cis} to the selected alleles on its own haplotype~\cite{buffaloKern2024}, which carries half the chromosome's diploid variance ($v_h = v_i/2$). Similarly, only the carrier's own breeding value---not its mate's---acts in \emph{cis}. We denote the weight of this \emph{cis} component in the carrier's family size as $\kappa_\text{cis}$, and the weight of the carrier's own breeding value in the total mating-system scalar $\kappa = \kappa_\text{own} + \kappa_\text{partner}$ as $\kappa_\text{own}$; under the mating model of \autoref{sec:mating}, both equal $1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:B_split}). The remainder of the genome---comprising the homologous haplotype and all other chromosomes---assorts independently of the focal neutral site, generating the global reduction driven by the residual variance $\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}v_h$. Assuming selected loci are distributed independently along the genetic map so that their effects multiply~\cite{hudson1995,santiago2016}, and that fitness is multiplicative across loci (\autoref{sec:onemodel}), the local and global reductions combine additively in the exponent~\cite[Supplementary Equations~28--29]{buffaloKern2024}; \cite[cf.][]{santiago1998}:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i \approx \exp\!\Big\{-\Big[\underbrace{\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i\,v_h}_{\text{local factor (\textit{cis})}} + \underbrace{Q^2\left(\kappa V_A - \kappa_\text{own}\,v_h\right)}_{\text{global factor (rest of the genome)}}\Big]\Big\}, \label{total_red}
\end{equation}
where $Q = 2/(2 - Z)$ evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:Q} at the same retention factor as the local term, and $Q^2$ represents the asymptotic limit of $\overline{Q^2}_i$ as $M_i \to \infty$. Across a DFE, $\overline{Q^2}_i$ and $Q^2$ are replaced by their averages over the variance weights $\nu_k$. To evaluate the multiplicative bound, $\kappa_\text{max}$ replaces $\kappa$ (see Appendix~\ref{app:omega} for the corresponding small-variance approximation).

Because the \emph{cis} haplotype variance $v_h$ is explicitly subtracted from the global term and transferred to the local term, no variance is double-counted. In the limit of a freely recombining chromosome ($M_i \to \infty$), Equation~\eqref{total_red} collapses smoothly to the global reduction $e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}$. Hereafter, we define the pure effect of linked selection as the additional reduction generated by the local factor beyond this global baseline. Normalizing Equation~\eqref{total_red} by the unlinked reduction at the same retention factor isolates the chromosome-specific linked multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i \equiv \frac{(N_e/N)_i}{(N_e/N)_\text{unlinked}} = \exp\!\left[-v_h\left(\kappa_\text{cis}\,\overline{Q^2}_i - \kappa_\text{own}\,Q^2\right)\right], \label{linked_penalty}
\end{equation}
which satisfies $\Omega_i \le 1$ because $\overline{Q^2}_i \ge Q^2$ for every effect class and $\kappa_\text{cis} = \kappa_\text{own}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:omega}). Assuming neutral sites are distributed uniformly across the physical genome, the genome-wide effective size reduction is the average over all $n$ autosomes weighted by their physical lengths $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N_e}{N}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}} \left(\frac{N_e}{N}\right)_i = \overline{\Omega}\,e^{-Q^2\kappa V_A}, \qquad \overline{\Omega} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{L_i}{L_\text{total}}\, \Omega_i. \label{eq:genome_linked}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The two equilibrium tests as limits on the long-term variance}\label{sec:conditions}
Inverting the multiplicative bound yields the minimum long-term variance required to account for an observed deficit in neutral diversity. Let
\begin{equation}
    \delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{4N\mu} \approx \frac{N_{eC}}{N}
\end{equation}
denote the diversity ratio defined in \autoref{sec:quantity}\edit{, which we evaluate at its estimate $\hat{\delta} = \pi_\text{obs}/(4\mu N_c)$ (\autoref{sec:pi_methods})}. At long-term equilibrium, setting the multiplicative bound equal to $\delta$ gives the minimum required variance:
\begin{equation}
    V_{A,\delta} = \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}}. \label{eq:vamax_mult}
\end{equation}
Because the multiplicative bound maximizes the loss of diversity per unit of variance, a population with $\bar{V}_A < V_{A,\delta}$ cannot owe its diversity deficit solely to heritable fitness variance at unlinked sites. Notably, $V_{A,\delta}$ grows only logarithmically with the deficit, increasing by $\ln(10)/(4\kappa_\text{max}) \approx 0.58/\kappa_\text{max}$ for each additional tenfold loss of diversity. Explaining a 100-fold deficit under random mating, for instance, requires $\bar{V}_A \ge 1.15$, while a 10,000-fold deficit requires $\bar{V}_A \ge 2.3$. By contrast, inverting the small-variance approximation yields a valid threshold, $V_{A,\max}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:vamax}, Appendix~\ref{app:A_first}), only when $\bar{V}_A \le 1$—that is, for populations retaining at least $1/(1 + 4\kappa)$ of their neutral diversity expectation (roughly one-fifth under random mating and one-ninth under strict monogamy). When physical linkage is included, the variance required to explain $\delta$ is obtained by numerically solving for the root of Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} set equal to $\delta$, as the linked multiplier $\overline{\Omega}$ itself depends on $V_A$ (Methods). Because $\overline{\Omega} \le 1$, incorporating linkage reduces the required variance below the corresponding unlinked threshold at the same retention factor, $\ln(1/\delta)/(Q^2\kappa)$ (which equals $V_{A,\delta}$ at $Z = 1$ and $\kappa = \kappa_\text{max}$).

Expressed in terms of the scaling factor $c = \bar{V}_A/\hat{V}_A$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:c}), each equilibrium model imposes a one-sided threshold on $c$. Because MSDB can maintain at most $U_d\,\bar{s}$ (\autoref{sec:msdb}), a population passes the maintenance test only if
\begin{equation}
    c \le c_\text{MSD} \equiv \frac{U_d\,\bar{s}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
When $c\text{MSD} < 1$, this ratio represents the maximum fraction of the pedigree estimate that recurrent deleterious mutation can sustain. Conversely, heritable fitness variance accounts for the unlinked diversity deficit only if $\bar{V}_A \ge V_{A,\delta}$, meaning a population passes the diversity test only if
\begin{equation}
    c \ge c_\text{div} \equiv \frac{V_{A,\delta}}{\hat{V}_A}.
\end{equation}
When $c\text{div} > 1$, this ratio gives the minimum factor by which the long-term variance must exceed the contemporary pedigree estimate to explain the missing diversity. Because both thresholds represent equilibrium requirements, any required value of $c \ne 1$ implies a departure from stationarity. Crucially, a single long-term scaling factor $c$ can satisfy both tests simultaneously if and only if $c_\text{div} \le c_\text{MSD}$, which requires
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{4\kappa_\text{max}} \le U_d\,\bar{s}, \qquad \text{or equivalently,} \qquad \delta \ge e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}. \label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
Condition~\eqref{eq:compat} is entirely independent of the pedigree estimate $\hat{V}_A$. Even under the conservative multiplicative bound, fitness variance maintained at MSDB cannot reduce neutral diversity at unlinked sites below a fraction $e^{-4\kappa_\text{max}U_d\bar{s}}$ of its neutral expectation. At a typical vertebrate value of $U_d\bar{s} = 0.01$, for example, compatibility under random mating requires $\delta \ge 0.96$, i.e., at most a $4\%$ loss of diversity. Whether the two equilibrium models can be reconciled by any stationary variance is therefore predetermined by $U_d\bar{s}$, the mating system, and $\delta$ before any pedigree estimate is consulted; $\hat{V}_A$ merely determines which of the two tests a population passes or fails at $c = 1$. Note that when physical linkage is included, the numerical root of Equation~\eqref{eq:genome_linked} replaces $V_{A,\delta}$, and compatibility is evaluated numerically (Methods).

\subsection{Approximations biased toward passing both tests}\label{sec:approx}
Because our core analytical choices deliberately bias each test toward passing, the conclusion that a population fails either test--and that no single variance passes both (i.e. $c_\text{div} > 1 > c_\text{MSD}$)--is conservative. 

On the diversity side, our approximations maximize the reduction in effective size generated by a given $V_A$, thereby minimizing the required threshold $V_{A,\delta}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unbounded multiplicative fitness:} We apply the multiplicative bound ($w \propto e^z$), which relies on an unattenuated Gaussian upper tail of extreme ancestors rather than imposing physiological or ecological ceilings on family size (\autoref{sec:onemodel}).
    \item \textbf{Maximum mating-system amplification:} Under social monogamy, the bound uses $\kappa_\text{max} = 2$ (strict monogamy, $\alpha = 0$) in place of the smaller $\kappa(\alpha)$ produced by extra-pair paternity (\autoref{sec:mating}).
    \item \textbf{Complete retention at unlinked sites:} For the global reduction, we set $Z = 1$ ($Q = 2$) rather than accounting for the erosion of associations by purifying selection ($Q = 2/(1+s_k) < 2$; \autoref{sec:unlinked}).
    \item \textbf{Absence of crossover interference:} Along chromosomes, we use Haldane's mapping function rather than a map incorporating positive crossover interference, which elevates recombination rates at intermediate genetic distances and weakens linked selection.
    \item \textbf{Empirical scaling of linked variance:} In the local factor, we scale chromosomal variance by the total pedigree estimate $\hat{V}_A$ rather than restricting it to the smaller variance maintainable at MSDB ($U_d\bar{s}$; \autoref{sec:linkage}).
\end{itemize}

On the maintenance side, our approximation maximizes the variance that recurrent mutation can sustain, thereby maximizing $c_\text{MSD}$ and yielding the smallest possible required selection coefficient $s^*$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strong-selection limit:} We evaluate MSDB in the mutation--selection balance limit ($N_es \gg 1$, giving $V_A \approx U_d\bar{s}$), which represents the strict upper ceiling on the variance maintainable at any effective population size (\autoref{sec:msdb}).
\end{itemize}

\edit{Four} factors could potentially act in the opposite direction, and we detail each explicitly. First, if breeding values follow a strongly right-skewed rather than Gaussian distribution—concentrating fitness variance within a few extreme lineages—the retention factor $Z$ can exceed one, amplifying drift beyond the Gaussian model (Appendix~\ref{app:A_Z}). Second, while our baseline linked model distributes selected and neutral sites uniformly along each chromosome, clustering functional loci within low-recombination regions strengthens linked selection; we derive and evaluate this clustering effect in Appendix~C. \edit{Third, under the multiplicative bound a variance that fluctuates over the genealogy removes more diversity than a constant variance with the same mean (\autoref{sec:onemodel}), so a long-term mean below $V_{A,\delta}$ could account for a deficit if the variance reached, in some generations, values well above any pedigree estimate.} Finally, on the maintenance side, the required deleterious effect $s^* = \bar{V}_A/U_d$ scales inversely with the genome-wide deleterious mutation rate $U_d$. Because an underestimate of $U_d$ would inflate $s^*$, we evaluate all maintenance tests across two independent mutation-rate estimates and a broad range of functional target sizes derived from the genome annotations (Methods).
