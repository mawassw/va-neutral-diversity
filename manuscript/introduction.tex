The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, is the heritable part of the differences in reproductive success among individuals~\cite{crow1958,robertson1961}. In wild populations, the pedigree-based animal model has been applied to lifetime breeding success to estimate $V_A$ in total fitness rather than in a single component~\cite{kruuk2004,postma2014}. By the secondary theorem of selection~\cite{robertson1966,price1970}, a non-zero $V_A$ implies directional selection on the phenotypes closely related to fitness, without specifying which alleles selection acts on or whether the predicted change is realized, and wild populations often show less evolutionary change than predicted based on the classical models~\cite{merila2001,mawass2025contrast}. Recently, Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} meta-analyzed this estimate across 19 long-term monitored populations of birds and mammals and found that $V_A$ is often substantial, which they interpreted as a sign of ongoing adaptation. At the level of alleles, however, a variance this large raises two central questions: how much neutral diversity is lost, and what maintains it.

Across animal species, neutral diversity varies by only a narrow margin despite vastly larger variance in census size (Lewontin's paradox~\cite{lewontin1974}), implying that effective population sizes must lie orders of magnitude below census sizes in abundant taxa~\cite{leffler2012,buffalo2021,lewin2026}. Heritable variance in fitness is one cause of this deficit. While stochastic variation in family size inherently drives genetic drift~\cite{wright1938}, heritability compounds this effect across generations. When a favored ancestor passes its reproductive advantage to its offspring, it severely inflates the variance in long-term lineage contributions~\cite{robertson1961}. Drift at a neutral site is then set by how much ancestors' expected long-term contributions vary~\cite{wray1990}. Theories of drift under selection compute this contribution differently from the ancestor's heritable advantage, but all assume that selection pressures remain strictly constant over time. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995,santiago1998} modeled the advantage as persisting by a constant fraction each generation, showing that at long-term equilibrium, the resulting drift reduces the ratio of effective to census size to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium variance in relative fitness and $Q$ is Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, representing the ancestor's advantage summed across all generations in which it persists. Woolliams et al.~\cite{woolliams1993,woolliams1999} subsequently extended this framework to multiple fitness components, including correlated fitness between mates.

Whereas the preceding models track the long-term contribution of a focal ancestor, Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} recast this dynamic from the perspective of the gene. In this framework, the probability that a neutral gene survives is proportional to the reproductive value of the individual carrying it. Crucially, they showed that selection on the genetic background---even at unlinked loci---can drastically inflate the variance in this reproductive value. A neutral allele shares its carrier's fitness advantage only as long as it remains associated with the selected alleles that carrier transmitted. Viewed from this gene-centric perspective, Robertson's $Q$ is precisely this association summed over the generations before it breaks down. This association is formally linkage disequilibrium (LD), a term that applies to unlinked sites as well as to linked ones~\cite{barton2000}. Because recombination halves this LD each generation at an unlinked site, $Q$---as the sum of this decaying association over time---converges to a geometric sum of 2~\cite{santiago1998,charlesworth2012}. Here, the gene and ancestor perspectives align, as the halving of LD matches the per-generation halving of an ancestor's expected contribution due to segregation. Consequently, every unlinked site experiences the carrier's heritable fitness variance equally, resulting in a uniform, genome-wide reduction in $N_e$ as defined by Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} provides a robust approximation for any fitness landscape provided $V_A$ is small. In this weak-selection limit, any continuous fitness function can be linearized, making the expected long-term contribution strictly proportional to the breeding value. However, if the effects of individual loci on fitness are multiplicative---as is often assumed in quantitative genetic models of fitness---these advantages are additive on a log scale, making the expected contribution an exponential function of the breeding value. Under this exponential scaling, the variance in long-term contributions reduces $N_e$ by a factor of $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating, and $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy~\cite{weissman2012}. 

At linked sites, however, the reduction in $N_e$ shifts from a global baseline to local topography. Because LD scales with genetic distance, a neutral site's reduction in $N_e$ is governed by the specific selective landscape of its chromosome neighborhood. This localized reduction is also vastly stronger. Closely linked sites recombine rarely, allowing their association to outlast the rapid decay of unlinked loci. Consequently, if selection is slow to purge the variation, LD persists over many generations, driving the cumulative association $Q$ well above 2. Recent empirical studies have leveraged this local topography---primarily modeling it as linked background selection---to infer the variance in fitness or the strength of purifying selection directly from patterns in sequence data or temporal allele frequency shifts~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020,murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. However, predicting diversity at this resolution strictly requires high-density recombination maps. For most wild vertebrates, pedigree linkage maps resolve whole-chromosome lengths far better than fine-scale recombination rate variation, fundamentally limiting the application of these local models.

Because a linked association lasts only until the selected allele is purged, the severity of the reduction in $N_e$ depends on what maintains the fitness variance $V_A$~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. Mutation--selection--drift balance (MSDB) is the classical account of this maintenance~\cite{haldane1937,crow1970}. When selection is strong relative to drift, the variance maintained approximates to
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ the mean of the distribution of fitness effects (DFE)~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2015}. Yet, empirical measurements consistently violate this baseline. Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} found that genetic variance in \textit{Drosophila} fitness components exceeds MSDB expectations by more than an order of magnitude, pointing to balancing selection or other uncharacterized forces~\cite{johnson2005,charlesworth2022}. Similarly, Connallon and Czuppon~\cite{connallon2026} demonstrated that vertebrate mutation rates are far too low to account for the massive $V_A$ estimated in wild populations by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}. Because both $U_d$ and $\bar{s}$ can now be estimated independently~\cite{eyrewalker2007,huber2017,bergeron2023,lin2026}, the exact fraction of empirical $V_A$ that mutation can maintain must be evaluated population by population. 

This excess empirical variance also fundamentally reduces $N_e$ at unlinked sites, yet models of the diversity deficit have overwhelmingly concentrated on linked effects. When $V_A$ is driven strictly by MSDB, this genome-wide reduction recovers the classic limit of background selection at unlinked sites~\cite{charlesworth2012}. Historically, this unlinked reduction was assumed to be negligible compared to linked effects because unlinked associations are short-lived~\cite{charlesworth2012,matheson2024}. However, Matheson and Masel~\cite{matheson2024} recently showed that in human-like genomes, the unlinked reduction actually overtakes the linked effect once $U_d>1$. Crucially, estimating this genome-wide unlinked reduction via Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} (where $Q=2$) bypasses the limitations of the linked models because it requires neither the MSDB assumption ($U_d \bar{s}$) nor a high-resolution genomic map. It requires only the empirical $V_A$ measured from a pedigree and its persistence time. While Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} applied this logic to the mean vertebrate estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}---predicting a 16 to 55\% reduction in unlinked neutral diversity---no study has yet estimated how much of the diversity deficit this map-free, unlinked reduction accounts for on a population-by-population basis, nor how that share compares with the local reduction at linked sites.

Both models rely on the additive variance in relative fitness---precisely the metric estimated by Bonnet et al.---meaning the empirical estimates and theoretical parameters are directly comparable. However, a contemporary pedigree estimate is fundamentally not the variance either model demands. Pedigrees capture only a few decades of specific cohorts and environments, yet heritability in wild populations fluctuates substantially with changing environmental conditions~\cite{charmantier2005,wilson2006,mawass2022}. Neutral diversity, by contrast, reflects an effective size averaged over the roughly $4N_e$ generations of a coalescence. Predicting a reduction in diversity therefore requires the fitness variance to be averaged across this deep evolutionary timescale. Because MSDB describes a variance held constant at equilibrium, evaluating a population against MSDB effectively tests the very assumption of stationarity that the diversity model requires. Ultimately, whether a population conforms to either framework depends on how its transiently measured variance relates to its long-term counterpart. By applying both tests to the same populations, we can determine whether a single stationary variance can satisfy both models, or whether episodic processes are required to account for the measured variance, the missing diversity, or both.

Here we test the 19 pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} against both equilibrium models, applying the maintenance test to 18 populations and the diversity test to 9. We first generalize the reduction at unlinked sites by allowing extra-pair paternity, which places socially monogamous populations on a continuum between strict monogamy and random mating~\cite{santiago1995,weissman2012,woolliams1999}. We then incorporate linked sites using only the map length of each chromosome. Finally, we ask how far the true long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate to pass each test. Because a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, any deviation from the pedigree estimate narrows one discrepancy only to widen the other. We find that the measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the missing neutral diversity, and too large to be maintained at MSDB. Under multiplicative effects, heritable variance in fitness roughly halves the effective size of the median population at unlinked sites. In stark contrast, observed diversity lies hundreds to tens of thousands of times below its neutral expectation. Bridging this deficit would require a long-term variance above one---larger than any empirical estimate---and linkage fails to resolve this gap in any population. Because the long-term variances required by the two tests differ by more than a hundredfold in every population with diversity data, no single rescaling of the pedigree estimates passes both tests as the long-term variance.
