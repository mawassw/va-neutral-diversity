The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, is the heritable component of variation in reproductive success among individuals~\cite{crow1958,robertson1961}. In wild populations, the pedigree-based animal model has been applied to lifetime breeding success to estimate $V_A$ in total fitness rather than in isolated life-history components~\cite{kruuk2004,postma2014}. By Fisher's fundamental theorem and the secondary theorem of selection~\cite{robertson1966,price1970}, a non-zero $V_A$ implies an ongoing increase in mean genetic fitness and directional selection on traits genetically correlated with fitness, without specifying which alleles selection acts on or whether those changes are realized---and indeed, wild populations frequently exhibit less evolutionary change than classical models predict~\cite{merila2001,mawass2025contrast}. Recently, Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} meta-analyzed pedigree estimates across 19 long-term monitored populations of birds and mammals and found that $V_A$ is often substantial, which they interpreted as evidence of pervasive ongoing adaptation. At the allelic level, however, a variance of this magnitude raises two fundamental population-genetic questions: how much neutral diversity does it eliminate, and what evolutionary mechanism maintains it?

The effective population size ($N_e$) implied by a population's neutral diversity and mutation rate is typically far smaller than its census size ($N_c$), often by several orders of magnitude~\cite{buffalo2021,lewin2026}. This deficit grows with abundance, so that across animal species diversity varies over a much narrower range than census size (Lewontin's paradox~\cite{lewontin1974,leffler2012}). Heritable variance in fitness is one of the processes that lower $N_e$ below the census size, and the theory of drift under selection predicts its genome-wide effect from the variance, whatever loci carry it~\cite{robertson1961,santiago1995}. While stochastic variation in family size inherently generates genetic drift~\cite{wright1938}, heritability compounds this variation across generations: when an ancestor transmits its reproductive advantage to its offspring, the variance in long-term genetic contributions across lineages is inflated~\cite{robertson1961}. Drift at a neutral site is governed directly by this variance in ancestors' expected long-term contributions~\cite{wray1990}. Although theories of drift under selection compute this long-term contribution under different assumptions, they all require selection pressures to remain constant across the brief span of generations over which an ancestor's advantage decays. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995,santiago1998} modeled this advantage as decaying by a constant fraction each generation, showing that at long-term equilibrium, the resulting drift reduces the ratio of effective size to the number of breeding adults, $N$, to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium variance in relative fitness and $Q$ is Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, representing an ancestor's selective advantage summed across all generations in which it persists. Woolliams et al.~\cite{woolliams1993,woolliams1999} subsequently generalized this framework to accommodate multiple fitness components, including correlated reproductive success between mates.

Whereas these ancestor-centered models track the descendants of a focal individual, Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} recast the same process from the perspective of a tracked gene copy. In their framework, the probability that a neutral allele leaves descendants is governed by the reproductive value of the individuals carrying it, and selection on the genetic background---even at unlinked loci---can substantially inflate the variance of this reproductive value. A neutral allele shares its carrier's fitness advantage only while it remains statistically associated with the selected alleles transmitted by that carrier. From this gene-centered perspective, Robertson's $Q$ is precisely this association---formally, linkage disequilibrium (LD), whether between linked or unlinked sites~\cite{barton2000}---summed over the generations before it decays. At an unlinked site, independent assortment halves this LD each generation, causing the cumulative association $Q$ to converge to a geometric sum of $2$~\cite{santiago1998,charlesworth2012}. Here, the gene-centered and ancestor-centered perspectives coincide, as the per-generation halving of LD mirrors the Mendelian halving of an ancestor's expected breeding value. Consequently, every unlinked neutral site experiences its carrier's heritable fitness variance equally, producing a uniform, genome-wide reduction in $N_e$ governed by Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. This linear expression provides a robust approximation for any smooth fitness landscape provided $V_A$ is small, so that the expected long-term contribution remains linear in the breeding value. When individual loci act multiplicatively on fitness, however, selective advantages sum on a logarithmic scale, making the expected long-term contribution exponential in the breeding value. Under this multiplicative architecture, heritable variance reduces unlinked $N_e$ by a factor of $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating and $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy~\cite{weissman2012}. 

At physically linked sites, this reduction in $N_e$ shifts from a uniform global baseline to a heterogeneous local topography. Recombination erodes the LD between two sites in proportion to the recombination fraction between them, whereas purging removes it in proportion to the selected allele's effect on fitness. Consequently, selected sites close enough that recombination separates them from a neutral site more slowly than purging removes them act on it together, as an effectively non-recombining block, and the neutral site's effective size depends on the selection in its chromosomal neighborhood~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. This local reduction can be much stronger than the unlinked baseline. Independent assortment halves an unlinked association each generation, so it lasts about two generations, whereas a tightly linked association lasts until a rare crossover breaks it or selection removes the allele, which for a slowly purged allele can take many generations. Because the neutral site shares the allele's effect on fitness for that whole time, the variance the allele adds to the site's long-term contribution grows with the square of the association's duration. Recent empirical studies have leveraged this local topography---primarily within the framework of background selection---to infer fitness variance or the strength of purifying selection directly from genomic diversity landscapes or temporal allele-frequency covariances~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020,murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}.

Because a linked association persists only until the selected allele is eliminated or fixed, the severity of the local reduction in $N_e$ depends directly on the evolutionary mechanism maintaining $V_A$~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. Mutation--selection--drift balance (MSDB) is the classical null model for this maintenance~\cite{haldane1937,crow1970}. When purifying selection is strong relative to genetic drift, the standing variance maintained at MSDB is approximately
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ is the arithmetic mean of the distribution of deleterious fitness effects (DFE)~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2015}. Empirical estimates of fitness variance, however, routinely exceed this mutational baseline. Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} demonstrated that standing genetic variance in \textit{Drosophila} fitness components exceeds MSDB expectations by more than an order of magnitude, pointing toward balancing selection, fluctuating selection, or other non-equilibrium forces~\cite{johnson2005,charlesworth2022}. Similarly, Connallon and Czuppon~\cite{connallon2026} argued qualitatively that vertebrate deleterious mutation rates are too low to sustain the large $V_A$ estimates reported by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, though without directly testing those population estimates against empirical parameterizations of $U_d\bar{s}$. Because both $U_d$ and $\bar{s}$ can now be constrained from comparative and population-genomic data~\cite{eyrewalker2007,huber2017,bergeron2023,lin2026}, the exact fraction of empirical $V_A$ that recurrent mutation can sustain can be tested quantitatively in each population. 

If wild populations truly harbor fitness variance well above MSDB expectations, that excess variance must also depress $N_e$ at unlinked sites across the genome---yet studies of the diversity deficit have overwhelmingly focused on linked effects. When $V_A$ is maintained strictly by MSDB, Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} recovers the classical unlinked limit of background selection~\cite{charlesworth2012}. Historically, this unlinked effect was considered negligible compared to linked background selection because unlinked associations decay within a few generations~\cite{charlesworth2012,matheson2024}. Matheson and Masel~\cite{matheson2024}, however, recently demonstrated that in genomes with vertebrate-like recombination maps, the unlinked reduction overtakes the linked effect whenever $U_d > 1$. Crucially, evaluating this global unlinked reduction via Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} (with $Q = 2$) bypasses the main obstacles of local linked models: it requires neither the assumption that $V_A$ is maintained at MSDB nor a fine-scale recombination map, relying solely on the pedigree estimate of $V_A$ and the mating system. Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} applied this unlinked approximation to the cross-species mean estimate of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, predicting a $16\%$ to $55\%$ reduction in neutral diversity. A reduction of that size is far smaller than the deficits of Lewontin's paradox, so heritable variance in fitness is not expected to explain them. What has not been measured is the size of its contribution in each population, with that population's own estimate, mating system and linkage map, or how much of the variance recurrent mutation maintains.

Both theoretical frameworks---drift under heritable fitness variance and maintenance at MSDB---are parameterized by the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, the exact quantity estimated by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}. Yet a contemporary pedigree estimate is not the long-term parameter either equilibrium model requires. Pedigree studies span only a few decades of specific cohorts and environmental regimes, across which heritability can fluctuate substantially~\cite{charmantier2005,wilson2006,mawass2022}. Neutral genetic diversity, by contrast, reflects an effective population size integrated over the roughly $4N_e$ generations required for lineages to coalesce, requiring then the long-term historical average of $V_A$. Conversely, because MSDB describes a stationary variance at equilibrium, testing a population against MSDB directly evaluates the stationarity assumption on which equilibrium diversity predictions rely. Whether a population satisfies either model therefore hinges on how its contemporary pedigree estimate relates to its long-term historical variance. Evaluating the same populations with both equilibrium tests simultaneously reveals whether any single stationary variance can reconcile the two models, or whether non-equilibrium dynamics are required to explain the contemporary fitness variance, the diversity deficit, or both.

Here we test the 19 pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} against both equilibrium models, applying the maintenance test to 18 populations and the diversity test to 9. We ask how far the long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate for recurrent mutation to maintain it, and how far for it to account for the missing diversity. Because a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, any deviation from the pedigree estimate narrows one discrepancy only to widen the other. We find that each equilibrium accounts for a small share of what it is meant to explain. Under multiplicative effects, heritable variance in fitness roughly halves the effective size of the median population at unlinked sites, whereas observed diversity lies hundreds to tens of thousands of times below its neutral expectation. On a logarithmic scale, the measured variance therefore accounts for a median of 7\% of each deficit. Explaining the deficits would require a long-term variance above one, larger than any empirical estimate, and linkage does not resolve the shortfall in any population. At measured mutation rates, with the annotated functional sequence as the deleterious target and mean deleterious effects assigned from related species, recurrent mutation maintains about a twentieth of the measured variance in the median population. Because the long-term variances that the two equilibria require differ by more than a hundredfold in every population with diversity data, no single long-term variance is both small enough for mutation to maintain and large enough to account for the missing diversity.
That gap is set by the variance mutation maintains, the mating system and the observed deficit, so it does not depend on the pedigree estimates. These results also incorporate extra-pair paternity, which places socially monogamous populations between strict monogamy and random mating~\cite{santiago1995,weissman2012,woolliams1999}, and the effect of linkage, using only the map length of each chromosome.
