The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, is the heritable part of the differences in reproductive success among individuals~\cite{crow1958,robertson1961}. In wild populations it can now be measured directly using the pedigree-based animal model, which separates additive genetic variance from the similarity that relatives share through their environment~\cite{kruuk2004}, and when applied to lifetime breeding success it estimates $V_A$ in total fitness rather than in one of its components~\cite{postma2014}. The same quantity can also be estimated without measuring fitness on individuals, from the expected change in allele frequency due to selection across independent replicate populations and the linkage disequilibrium of their base population~\cite{geetaarun2026}. By the secondary theorem of selection~\cite{robertson1966,price1970}, a non-zero $V_A$ implies directional selection on the phenotypes that make up fitness, without specifying which alleles selection moves or whether the predicted change is realized, and wild populations often show less evolutionary change than predicted~\cite{merila2001, mawass2025contrast}. Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} meta-analyzed this estimate across 19 long-term monitored populations of birds and mammals and found that $V_A$ is often substantial, which they interpreted as a sign of ongoing adaptation in phenotypes that share genetic variance with fitness. At the level of alleles, a variance this large raises two questions: how much neutral diversity it removes, and what maintains it.

Across animal species, the effective size implied by neutral diversity, $\pi = 4N_e\mu$, lies orders of magnitude below the census size~\cite{leffler2012,buffalo2021,lewin2026}, the observation behind Lewontin's paradox~\cite{lewontin1974}. Heritable variance in fitness is one cause of this deficit. Any variance in family size increases drift~\cite{wright1938}, and inheritance prolongs its effects: a favored ancestor passes its advantage to its offspring and, halved by segregation, to theirs, so its descendants remain over-represented for several generations~\cite{robertson1961}. Drift at a neutral site is then set by how much ancestors' expected long-term contributions vary~\cite{wray1990}. Theories of drift under selection differ in how they compute this contribution from the ancestor's heritable advantage, and all compute it with selection acting in the same way in each of the generations it spans. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995,santiago1998} let the advantage persist by a constant fraction each generation and showed that at long-term equilibrium this drift reduces the effective size $N_e$, relative to the number of breeding adults $N$, to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium variance and $Q$ is Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, the ancestor's advantage counted over every generation in which it persists. Woolliams et al.~\cite{woolliams1993,woolliams1999} wrote the expected contribution as a linear regression on several selective advantages at once, which accommodates separate sexes and advantages shared with mates.

From the gene's perspective, Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} showed that the probability that a gene copy survives is proportional to the reproductive value of the individual that carries it, and that selection on unlinked loci in its background can greatly increase the variance of that value. A neutral allele shares its carrier's contribution only while it stays associated with the selected alleles that carrier transmitted, and in this perspective $Q$ is this association summed over the generations it lasts. The association is linkage disequilibrium (LD), a term that applies to unlinked sites as well as to linked ones~\cite{barton2000}. At an unlinked site LD is halved each generation, so $Q = 2$~\cite{santiago1998,charlesworth2012}; the ancestor and gene perspectives then coincide, and because every site in the genome shares the carrier's breeding value, the reduction in $N_e$ given by Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} is the same at every unlinked site and so sets a global effective size; only at unlinked sites does the carrier's long-term contribution set drift exactly. Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} holds for any model of fitness while $V_A$ is small, because a small advantage makes the expected contribution linear in the breeding value. If the effects of loci on fitness multiply, as the animal-model estimate itself assumes, the advantages of successive generations add on the log scale, so the expected contribution is exponential in the breeding value. The same variance then reduces $N_e$ by the factor $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating and $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy, and because a new allele's chance of fixation is a function of $N_e$, Weissman and Barton~\cite{weissman2012} found that this factor also multiplies the fixation probability of a new mutation. When $V_A$ is maintained by purifying selection against a steady influx of deleterious mutations, at mutation--selection--drift balance (MSDB), the same factor is the limit of background selection at unlinked sites~\cite{charlesworth2012}.

At linked sites the reduction in $N_e$ is local, because a neutral site's association with a selected allele on the same chromosome depends on the genetic distance between them, so the reduction experienced by each site depends on the selected variation in its own chromosomal neighborhood and varies along the genome. It is also stronger, because the closer the two sites lie in genetic distance, the less often they recombine, so their LD outlasts the roughly two generations an unlinked association lasts and, if selection removes variation slowly, it may persist for many generations, making $Q > 2$. At MSDB this local reduction is linked background selection~\cite{charlesworth1993,hudson1995}. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago2016} predicted $N_e$ jointly with the rate at which deleterious mutations fix, expressing the reduction through the additive variance in fitness that linked deleterious mutations generate. Buffalo and Kern~\cite{buffaloKern2024} built background selection from the additive variance in fitness contributed by each chromosomal segment and fitted it to neutral diversity along the human genome to estimate that variance, while Murphy et al.~\cite{murphy2023} fitted the classical model to the same pattern to infer the strength of purifying selection on coding and non-coding elements. Buffalo and Coop~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020} estimated linked selection from temporal change in allele frequencies in \textit{Drosophila}, and across species, Buffalo~\cite{buffalo2021} found that background selection and recurrent hitchhiking at \textit{Drosophila} parameters, or parameters tenfold stronger, still predict far more diversity than is observed in vertebrate species like those studied by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}. In the empirical studies the strength of selection, or the variance in fitness it maintains, is inferred from patterns in sequence data rather than measured in a population~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020,murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}, and predicting diversity along the genome at this resolution requires high-resolution recombination maps, whereas the pedigree linkage maps of most wild vertebrates resolve the length of each chromosome far better than how recombination varies along it.

A linked association lasts as long as the selected allele, so how long it persists depends on how fast that allele is purged, and in turn on what maintains $V_A$~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. MSDB is the classical account of the evolutionary forces involved in this maintenance~\cite{haldane1937,crow1970}. When selection is strong relative to drift, the variance maintained approximates to
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ the mean heterozygous effect of new mutations on relative fitness, the mean of the distribution of fitness effects (DFE)~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2015}. Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} compared this model against the genetic variance in fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} and found more than mutation could maintain, which he attributed to balancing selection~\cite[see also][]{johnson2005}, a conclusion that mutation-accumulation lines also support for two of three fitness components~\cite{sharp2018}. Estimates for whole third chromosomes of a laboratory \textit{Drosophila} population exceed the variance that mutation is expected to maintain by more than an order of magnitude, from which Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} concluded that other forms of selection must contribute, of a nature still unclear. For wild vertebrates, Connallon and Czuppon~\cite{connallon2026}, modeling how balancing selection can maintain such variance, noted that deleterious mutation rates appear too low to account for the estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, an order-of-magnitude comparison with generic parameters. Both inputs of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_mukai} are now measured in vertebrates: $U_d$ from mutation rates estimated by pedigree sequencing~\cite{bergeron2023} and annotated genomes, and $\bar{s}$ from estimates of the DFE~\cite{eyrewalker2007,huber2017,lin2026}. The share of the measured variance that mutation can maintain, and so how much those other forms of selection must supply, can therefore be estimated population by population.

The variance that MSDB maintains also reduces $N_e$ at unlinked sites, yet models that attribute part of the diversity deficit to selection have concentrated on linked sites, although coalescent effective sizes estimated from diversity are genome-wide. At MSDB the unlinked reduction in $N_e$ has been taken to be much smaller than the linked one, because an unlinked association is short-lived, and models of background selection usually omit it~\cite{charlesworth2012,matheson2024}. Matheson and Masel~\cite{matheson2024} found instead, in simulations of a human-like genome, that the unlinked reduction exceeds the linked one once $U_d$ is above one. In both views its size follows from $U_d\bar{s}$, the variance that deleterious mutation would maintain. Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} does not depend on that assumption, because at $Q = 2$ the unlinked reduction requires only $V_A$ and how long the variance is sustained, with no model of what maintains it and no genomic map, so a variance measured in a pedigree is enough to predict it. Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} applied Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} to the mean of the estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, which would reduce neutral diversity at unlinked sites by 16 to 55\%, but no study has estimated, population by population, how much of the missing diversity the unlinked reduction accounts for and how that share compares with the reduction at linked sites.

Both models of maintenance and of the reduction in neutral diversity involve the same quantity, the additive variance in relative fitness, and Bonnet et al.'s estimates are expressed in it, so neither test requires a change of units. They are not however the variance either model requires. A pedigree estimate describes the few decades of cohorts and environments sampled, and heritability in wild populations changes with environmental conditions~\cite{charmantier2005,wilson2006,mawass2022}. Neutral diversity instead reflects effective size averaged over the roughly $4N_e$ generations of a coalescence, so the reduction in diversity depends on the variance averaged over those generations, and MSDB describes a variance held constant at equilibrium, so the stationarity that the diversity test assumes is what the maintenance test checks. Whether a population passes each test therefore depends on how the measured variance relates to its long-term counterpart, and testing both in the same population shows whether a single long-term variance can pass both, or whether other processes are required to account for the measured variance, the missing diversity, or both.

Here we put the 19 pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} to both tests. We generalize the reduction at unlinked sites by allowing extra-pair paternity, which places socially monogamous populations on a continuum between strict monogamy and random mating~\cite{santiago1995,weissman2012,woolliams1999}, add linked sites using only the map length of each chromosome, and ask how far the long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate to pass each test. Because a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, any error in the estimate narrows one discrepancy and widens the other. We find that the measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing, and too large to be maintained at MSDB. Recurrent mutation at the DFEs assigned to these populations maintains about a twentieth of the measured variance in the median population. If the effects of loci on fitness multiply, as the animal model assumes, heritable variance in fitness roughly halves effective size at unlinked sites in the median population, whereas observed diversity lies hundreds to tens of thousands of times below its neutral expectation, and linkage does not resolve this gap in any population. The long-term variances that the two tests require differ by more than a hundredfold in every population with diversity data, so no single multiple of the pedigree estimates passes both tests.
