\section{Introduction}
% Introduction redraft, 2026-10-16. Organised around the two classical questions about heritable

Heritable variation in fitness has two consequences that can be studied apart. It changes neutral diversity, because a parent of high breeding value passes more than its share of copies of every allele it carries, neutral ones included, for as long as the advantage stays with them (Robertson 1961). It also has to be supplied: selection removes part of it every generation, and something must put back what is removed. Both consequences are governed by one quantity, the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, on the scale where mean fitness is one. What $V_A$ does to neutral diversity and what maintains it are therefore two questions about the same number, and both have been answered quantitatively, in humans and in \textit{Drosophila}.

The effect on diversity depends on how long a selective advantage stays attached to a neutral allele. For a neutral site assorting independently of every selected locus, Mendelian segregation halves the inherited breeding value each generation, and Santiago and Caballero (1995, 1998) summed that series into a closed form:
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A},
\end{equation}
where $Q = 2$ is the cumulative transmission factor and $V_A$ is their mean-standardized variance $C^2$. The expression is exact when $V_A$ is the long-run equilibrium variance; a variance that changes over the coalescent timescale enters through its time average. Because $N/N_e = 1 + 4V_A$ is linear, the unlinked term alone removes only a modest share of diversity at any $V_A$ well below one. Linkage keeps the association longer, and summing it along the genetic map gives the exponential reduction of background selection (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996; Santiago and Caballero 2016). Buffalo and Kern (2024) turned this quantitative-genetic view of linked selection into an inference: fitted to human diversity along the genome, it returns the equilibrium fitness variance, and the distribution of fitness effects, that would have produced the observed pattern. In \textit{Drosophila}, Buffalo and Coop (2019, 2020) measured the same coupling as genome-wide covariance in allele-frequency change through time. These studies run from diversity to the fitness variance it implies. Diversity far below its neutral expectation is the rule across species (Leffler et al. 2012; Buffalo 2021; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), and the unlinked term, the one part that needs no map, has largely been left out of the accounting (Matheson and Masel 2025).

The maintenance question runs the other way: not what a given $V_A$ does, but whether mutation can supply it. At mutation--selection--drift balance, new deleterious mutations bring variance in at rate $V_m = U_d s^2$ and selection removes it at rate $s$, so when selection dominates drift ($2N_e s \gg 1$) the standing variance approaches $V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Crow 1970; Charlesworth 2015), where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ the mean heterozygous effect. Charlesworth (2015) set this expectation against the variance in fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} and found more of it than mutation can maintain, leaving balancing selection to account for a substantial share (Johnson and Barton 2005; Sharp and Agrawal 2018). Both parameters are now measured in vertebrates: $U_d$ from mutation rates estimated by pedigree sequencing (Bergeron et al. 2023), and $\bar{s}$ from site-frequency inference of the distribution of fitness effects (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017). Neither comes from neutral diversity.

Wild vertebrates are where $V_A$ in total fitness has actually been measured, yet neither question has been put to those measurements. The pedigree animal model separates additive genetic variance from the shared environments and maternal effects of relatives in long-term monitoring records (Henderson 1975; Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010). Applied to lifetime breeding success, a composite lifetime measure of fitness, it estimates $V_A$ directly, rather than through a fitness component or a model of diversity (Postma 2014). Bonnet et al. (2022) fitted it to 19 populations of 15 species, from Soay sheep on St Kilda to great tits in Wytham Woods, and back-transformed each estimate to the observed scale of relative fitness, $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, with $\mathbf{d}$ the average derivative of mean relative fitness with respect to the model's link-scale predictors. That is the currency of both models: Santiago and Caballero's $C^2$ and the standing variance of mutation--selection--drift balance. Nothing has to be rescaled. The posterior modes run from 0.003 to 0.50, with a median of 0.10.

What separates the estimate from both models is time. A pedigree estimate is a snapshot: it integrates a few cohorts, and Bonnet et al.'s $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed population state, so the same $\mathbf{G}$ in a different environment yields a different $V_A(w)$. That is what the estimator is designed to measure, and it is not what either model takes as input. Diversity integrates fitness variance over the $\sim 4N_e$ generations of a pairwise coalescence; because $N/N_e$ is linear in $V_A$ and $\pi$ reflects the harmonic-mean $N_e$ over that window, diversity responds to the time-averaged variance $\bar{V}_A$. Mutation--selection--drift balance is a statement about the stationary variance. Both comparisons therefore turn on one scaling, $c = \bar{V}_A/\hat{V}_A$, the factor by which the long-run variance differs from the pedigree snapshot $\hat{V}_A$. Diversity asks how large $c$ must be for $V_A$ to explain the missing diversity, and mutation asks how small it must be for $V_A$ to be maintained. The stationarity the diversity calculation assumes is exactly what the maintenance calculation tests. Because the two requirements move in opposite directions, any common bias in the estimate, including environmental covariance among relatives absorbed by the animal model, narrows one discrepancy only by widening the other.

Here we put the 19 pedigree estimates through both comparisons. On the diversity side, the unlinked baseline is extended to separate sexes and social monogamy through a mating-system scalar $\kappa$, which keeps the calculation consistent with a single $V_A$ estimated across both sexes (Santiago and Caballero 1995; Woolliams et al. 1999), and linked interference is integrated along empirical recombination maps. Inverting the baseline gives $V_{A,\max}(\delta)$, the largest variance compatible with a given diversity deficit $\delta$, as a curve against which any population with a diversity estimate can be read. On the maintenance side, inverting $V_A \approx U_d\bar{s}$ gives the mean heterozygous effect $s^* = V_A/U_d$ the estimates would require. The measured variance is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing, and too large to be maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance. Over the measured range the unlinked term reduces neutral diversity 1.7-fold at the median and about 4.5-fold at the maximum, against deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold where diversity and census data exist; maintaining it would need $s^*$ near 0.16, where vertebrate distributions of fitness effects put the mean at $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$. Maintenance allows the long-run variance to be at most about 7\% of the snapshot, while the diversity deficit needs it to exceed the snapshot by more than the formula's domain allows. No single $c$ satisfies both, so the gap lies between the two models, and the pedigree estimates sit inside it.
