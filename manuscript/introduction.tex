Heritable variation in fitness has two consequences that can be studied apart. It reduces neutral diversity, because a parent of high breeding value passes on more than its share of copies of every allele it carries, neutral ones included, for as long as the advantage stays attached to them (Robertson 1961). It also has to be resupplied, because selection removes part of it every generation. Both consequences are governed by one quantity, the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, on the scale where mean fitness is one, and the two are not independent: how linked selection shapes the variation a population retains depends on what maintains its variance in fitness (Li and Berg 2026). Each consequence has a quantitative theory, and each theory has been confronted with data. That has happened in humans and \textit{Drosophila}, with a fitness variance either inferred from sequence data or measured on components of fitness.

The effect on diversity depends on how long a selective advantage stays attached to a neutral allele. At a neutral site assorting independently of every selected locus, Mendelian segregation halves the inherited breeding value each generation, and Santiago and Caballero (1995, 1998) summed that series to obtain, for a population at equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A},
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of breeding adults, $Q = 2$ is the cumulative transmission factor, and $V_A$ is their mean-standardized variance $C^2$, the long-run equilibrium additive variance in relative fitness. Linkage keeps the association longer, and summing it along the genetic map gives the exponential reduction of background selection (Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996; Santiago and Caballero 2016). Buffalo and Kern (2024) turned this quantitative-genetic view of linked selection into an inference: fitted to the landscape of human diversity, it returns the equilibrium fitness variance, and the distribution of fitness effects, that would have produced it. Buffalo and Coop (2019, 2020) read the same signal from the other end, as covariance in allele-frequency change across generations, which in experimentally evolving \textit{Drosophila} populations measures the share of change due to linked selection on fitness variance. Both approaches run from diversity, or its change, to the fitness variance it implies. The comparative pattern they leave open is large. Neutral diversity across species falls orders of magnitude short of its census expectation (Leffler et al. 2012; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), linked selection at the strongest published parameters does not account for the shortfall (Buffalo 2021), and the unlinked term, the one part that needs no genetic map, has largely been left out of the accounting (Matheson and Masel 2025).

Maintenance is the opposite question: not what a given $V_A$ does, but whether mutation can supply it. New deleterious mutations bring additive variance in at rate $V_m = U_d s^2$, and when selection dominates drift ($2N_e s \gg 1$) the standing variance at mutation--selection--drift balance approaches $V_A \approx U_d\bar{s}$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Crow 1970; Charlesworth 2015), where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ the mean heterozygous effect. Charlesworth (2015) set this expectation against the variance in fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} and found more than mutation can maintain, with balancing selection a substantial contributor (see also Johnson and Barton 2005). Sharp and Agrawal (2018) made the test experimental. Mutation-accumulation lines predicted the variance that mutation--selection balance should sustain, and the prediction matched standing variance for larval viability but fell short for male mating success and female fecundity. Both inputs to the expectation are now measured in vertebrates: $U_d$ from mutation rates estimated by pedigree sequencing (Bergeron et al. 2023), and $\bar{s}$ from site-frequency inference of the distribution of fitness effects (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2026). Neither comes from neutral diversity.

Wild vertebrates are where $V_A$ in total fitness has been measured. The pedigree animal model separates additive genetic variance from the shared environments and maternal effects of relatives in long-term monitoring records (Henderson 1975; Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010). Applied to lifetime breeding success, a composite lifetime measure of fitness, it estimates $V_A$ directly rather than through a fitness component or a model of diversity (Postma 2014). Bonnet et al. (2022) fitted it to 19 populations of birds and mammals, from Soay sheep on St Kilda to great tits in Wytham Woods, and found the variance often substantial. Each estimate was back-transformed to the observed scale of relative fitness, $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{d}$ holds the average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to the model's link-scale predictors. That is the currency of both theories above: Santiago and Caballero's $C^2$ and the standing variance of mutation--selection--drift balance. Yet the two questions have been put to these numbers only in passing. Connallon and Czuppon (2026) observe that deleterious mutation rates do not appear high enough to account for such variances, and show how drift and fluctuating selection let balancing selection supply more of it. The shortfall itself, however, has been argued qualitatively from \textit{Drosophila}-like parameters rather than computed from each population's mutation rate and genome. The diversity these variances should have removed has not been computed population by population at all.

What the comparison has to bridge is time. A pedigree estimate is a snapshot: it integrates the few cohorts a monitoring study spans, and the $\mathbf{d}$ of Bonnet et al. is evaluated at the observed state of the population, so the same $\mathbf{G}$ in another environment yields another $V_A(w)$. That is what the estimator is for, since it predicts the rate of adaptation in the generations measured (Bonnet et al. 2022), and heritability in wild populations is known to shift with environmental conditions, at least for morphological traits (Charmantier and Garant 2005; Wilson et al. 2006). Both theories instead take the long-run variance as input. Neutral diversity, $\pi = 4N_e\mu$, is set over the roughly $4N_e$ generations of a pairwise coalescence, over which fluctuations in effective size enter through their harmonic mean (Wright 1938), and mutation--selection--drift balance describes the stationary variance (Lynch and Hill 1986). Reading a snapshot into either theory therefore assumes a scaling between the estimate and the long-run variance. Nothing requires the two theories to agree on what that scaling must be.

Here we carry the pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022) through both theories and ask how the snapshot must be scaled to satisfy each. On the effect side, we extend the unlinked baseline to separate sexes and to social monogamy with extra-pair paternity, so that a single $V_A$ estimated across both sexes enters consistently (building on Santiago and Caballero 1995 and Woolliams et al. 1999). We then add linked interference integrated along empirical recombination maps, and invert the result into the largest variance compatible with a given diversity deficit. On the maintenance side, we use measured mutation rates and coding target sizes to obtain the mean heterozygous selection coefficient that each population's $V_A$ would require. The measured variance is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing, and too large to be maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance. Over the measured range the unlinked term reduces neutral diversity 1.7-fold at the median and about 4.5-fold at the maximum, against deficits of 230- to 38,000-fold where diversity and census data exist. Maintaining that variance would need a mean heterozygous effect near 0.16, where vertebrate distributions of fitness effects place it at $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$. Maintenance therefore allows the long-run variance to be at most about 7\% of the snapshot, while the diversity deficit needs it to exceed the snapshot by more than the theory's domain allows. Because any common bias in the estimates narrows one discrepancy only by widening the other, no single scaling satisfies both, and the pedigree estimates sit in the gap between the two theories.
