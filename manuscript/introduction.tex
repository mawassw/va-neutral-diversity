\section{Introduction}

The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, is the heritable part of the differences in reproductive success among individuals (Crow 1958; Robertson 1961). In wild populations it can now be measured directly. The pedigree-based animal model separates additive genetic variance from the similarity that relatives share through their environment (Kruuk 2004), and applied to lifetime breeding success it estimates $V_A$ in total fitness rather than in one of its components (Postma 2014). Bonnet et al. (2022) made this estimate for 19 long-term monitored populations of birds and mammals and found substantial $V_A$ in most of them, which they read as a sign of ongoing adaptation. Classical theory asks two further things of a variance this large: what it does to neutral diversity, and what maintains it.

Neutral diversity is far lower than population sizes predict. Diversity measures effective size, $\pi = 4N_e\mu$, and across animal species the effective size it implies lies orders of magnitude below the census size (Leffler et al. 2012; Buffalo 2021; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), the observation behind Lewontin's paradox (Lewontin 1974). Heritable variance in fitness is one cause. Any variance in family size increases drift (Wright 1938), and inheritance makes it last: a favoured ancestor passes its advantage to its offspring and, halved by segregation, to theirs, so its descendants remain over-represented for several generations (Robertson 1961). Santiago and Caballero (1995, 1998) showed that at long-term equilibrium this reduces effective size, relative to the number of breeding adults $N$, to
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium variance and $Q$, Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, is the association between a neutral site and its selected background summed over the generations in which it persists. At a site unlinked to every selected locus, segregation halves the association each generation, and $Q = 2$ unless selection purges the selected alleles faster (Santiago and Caballero 1998; Charlesworth 2012). Every site in the genome shares this reduction, which sets a global effective size. It is a rate of drift, not the diversity deficit itself; the question is how much of the deficit it can explain. Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} holds while $V_A$ is small. For larger variances, in the multiplicative model, where fitness is exponential in an additive breeding value, the reduction is $N_e/N = e^{-4V_A}$ without a pair bond and $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy (Barton and Etheridge 2011; Weissman and Barton 2012). The animal model relates breeding value to expected breeding success in the same way. At $Q = 2$ the global reduction needs nothing but $V_A$: no model of how the variance is maintained and no map of the genome. Yet it has largely been left out of accounts of the diversity deficit (Matheson and Masel 2025).

At linked sites the reduction is stronger, and it needs more than $V_A$. An association with a selected locus on the same chromosome is broken only as often as the two sites recombine, so it persists for many generations and depresses a local effective size that varies along the genome. Under mutation--selection balance this local reduction is background selection (Charlesworth et al. 1993; Hudson and Kaplan 1995). Santiago and Caballero (2016) and Buffalo and Kern (2024) expressed it through the additive variance in fitness, and Buffalo and Kern fitted it to diversity along the human genome to estimate that variance. Buffalo and Coop (2019, 2020) estimated linked selection from temporal change in allele frequencies in \textit{Drosophila}, and across species, linked selection at the strongest published parameters does not account for the deficit (Buffalo 2021). In each of these studies $V_A$ is inferred from sequence data, not measured. Their position-by-position predictions also need recombination maps resolved within chromosomes, whereas most wild vertebrates have pedigree linkage maps, which give the length of each chromosome far better than the variation along it. How long a linked association persists depends, moreover, on how long the selected alleles do, and so on what maintains $V_A$ (Li and Berg 2026).

The classical account of how $V_A$ persists is a balance between recurrent deleterious mutation and the selection that removes it (Haldane 1937; Crow 1970). Mutation adds additive variance in fitness at rate $U_d s^2$, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous selection coefficient. When selection is strong relative to drift ($2N_e s \gg 1$), the variance maintained is
\begin{equation}
V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{s}$ is the mean of the distribution of fitness effects (DFE) (Mukai et al. 1972; Charlesworth 2015). Charlesworth (2015) set this against the genetic variance in fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} and found more than mutation could maintain, pointing to balancing selection instead (see also Johnson and Barton 2005). Mutation-accumulation lines gave the same answer for male mating success and female fecundity, though not for larval viability (Sharp and Agrawal 2018). For wild vertebrates, Connallon and Czuppon (2026), modelling how balancing selection can maintain such variance, noted that deleterious mutation rates appear too low to account for the estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022). That comparison was qualitative. Both inputs of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_mukai} are now measured in vertebrates: $U_d$ from mutation rates estimated by pedigree sequencing (Bergeron et al. 2023), and $\bar{s}$ from estimates of the DFE (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2026).

To the best of our knowledge, pedigree estimates of $V_A$ have not been set against either expectation: neither the reduction in diversity they imply nor the mutational input needed to maintain them. Bonnet et al.'s estimates are additive variance in relative fitness, the quantity both theories use, so the comparison needs no change of units. It does need a statement about time. A pedigree estimate describes the cohorts and environments sampled, and heritability in wild populations changes with environmental conditions (Charmantier and Garant 2005; Wilson et al. 2006; Mawass et al. 2022). Both expectations concern the long-term variance instead. Neutral diversity reflects effective size over the roughly $4N_e$ generations of a coalescence, and mutation--selection balance describes a variance that holds constant over time. Setting a pedigree estimate against either is therefore a statement about how a few decades of variance in fitness relate to its long-term value.

Here we put the 19 pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022) to both tests, using the multiplicative model for the estimate and the prediction alike. The global reduction comes from Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}, extended to separate sexes and social monogamy so that one $V_A$ estimated across both sexes applies to every mating system (Santiago and Caballero 1995; Woolliams et al. 1999), and from the multiplicative bound, the largest reduction a given $V_A$ can produce, extended to extra-pair paternity. Averaging the local reduction over position along each chromosome, which needs only its map length, adds linked sites. Measured mutation rates give the mean selection coefficient needed to maintain each estimate. We then ask how far the long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate to pass each test. The diversity test assumes a variance sustained over the coalescent window, which is what the maintenance test asks of mutation. And a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, so any error in the estimate, whatever its source, narrows one discrepancy and widens the other.

The measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing, and too large to be maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance. The global effective size is at least 0.22 of the number of breeding adults (median 0.60), and even the multiplicative bound falls short of the observed deficits, of 230- to 38,000-fold, by factors of 80 to 31,000. Explaining them would take $V_A$ of 1.1 to 1.7, against measured values of at most one half. Linkage lowers that to about 1.0 in mammals and 0.3--0.4 in birds, still above every population's credible interval and only under the purifying selection that this variance is too large for. Maintenance would require a mean selection coefficient a median 18 times the vertebrate DFE means. The long-term variances the two tests require differ by a factor of about 390 on average, by more than 100 in every population, and by 80 to 440 even with linkage. No single rescaling of the pedigree estimates satisfies both.
