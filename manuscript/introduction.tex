The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, measures the heritable component of variation in fitness and is central to theories of adaptation, of the maintenance of genetic variation, and of effective population size (Crow 1958; Robertson 1961; Charlesworth 2015). In wild populations, $V_A$ is estimated with the pedigree animal model, which separates additive genetic variance from the environmental and maternal similarity of relatives (Henderson 1975; Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010) and, applied to lifetime breeding success, estimates $V_A$ directly rather than through a fitness component (Postma 2014). Bonnet et al. (2022) applied it to 19 populations of birds and mammals, including Soay sheep on St Kilda and great tits in Wytham Woods, and reported substantial $V_A$ in many of them, expressed on the observed scale of relative fitness as $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the latent-scale additive genetic covariance matrix and $\mathbf{d}$ the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to the latent predictors.

Standing $V_A$ requires a model of how it is maintained across generations. The classical model is a balance between recurrent deleterious mutation and purifying selection. New mutations contribute additive variance at rate $V_m = U_d s^2$, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous selection coefficient, and when selection is strong relative to drift ($2N_e s \gg 1$) the equilibrium variance is $V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Crow 1970; Charlesworth 2015). Charlesworth (2015) compared this expectation with the genetic variance in fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} and concluded that mutation--selection balance accounts for only part of it, with balancing selection contributing substantially (see also Johnson and Barton 2005). Sharp and Agrawal (2018) tested it experimentally with mutation-accumulation lines and found it sufficient for larval viability but not for male mating success or female fecundity. Connallon and Czuppon (2026) showed that drift and fluctuating selection allow balancing selection to maintain substantial $V_A$, noting that deleterious mutation rates appear too low to account for the wild-vertebrate estimates. That comparison was qualitative, although both inputs are now measured in vertebrates: $U_d$ from mutation rates estimated by pedigree sequencing (Bergeron et al. 2023), and $\bar{s}$ from the distribution of fitness effects (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2026).

Selection acting on $V_A$ also reduces the effective size of a population. Individuals with high breeding values for fitness leave more offspring, and because their advantage is inherited, selection increases the variance in long-term genetic contributions and with it the rate of drift at neutral sites (Robertson 1961). For a neutral site unlinked to the selected loci, segregation halves the transmitted breeding value each generation, and Santiago and Caballero (1995, 1998) showed that at long-term equilibrium
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of breeding adults, $Q$ is the cumulative transmission factor summed over generations, 2 when only Mendelian segregation breaks the association, and $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium additive variance in relative fitness (their $C^2$). With $Q = 2$ this genome-wide reduction depends only on Mendelian segregation, and so holds whatever process maintains $V_A$; when the selected alleles are themselves purged within a few generations, $Q$ is smaller (Santiago and Caballero 1998; Charlesworth 2012). It sets a global effective size: every neutral site shares it, whatever its position on the recombination map, because it arises from variance in reproductive success among individuals rather than from the haplotype on which the site sits. It is first order in selection. When fitness is multiplicative in a Gaussian breeding value for log-fitness, the reproductive value of an ancestor varies across ancestors as $e^{4V_A}$ and the probability of coalescence scales with its square (Barton and Etheridge 2011), so the reduction grows exponentially with $V_A$ rather than hyperbolically, and the two forms agree while $V_A$ is small. Because neutral nucleotide diversity is expected to scale with effective size, $\pi = 4N_e\mu$, Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} predicts the reduction in diversity caused by selection at unlinked loci. Across species, neutral diversity is orders of magnitude lower than expected from census size (Leffler et al. 2012; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), yet the genome-wide reduction has largely been omitted when accounting for this deficit (Matheson and Masel 2025). The animal models that estimate $V_A$ for fitness are fitted on exactly such a log scale, and their observed- and latent-scale variances are related by known transformations (de Villemereuil et al. 2016), but the reduction in effective size has not been derived on the scale on which pedigree estimates of $V_A$ are made.

At linked sites the reduction is stronger, and its form depends on the process that maintains $V_A$ (Li and Berg 2026). Associations between a neutral site and the selected loci on its own chromosome are broken only at the rate of recombination between them, so they persist for many generations and depress a local effective size that varies along the genome with recombination rate and the density of selected sites. Under mutation--selection balance this is background selection, the loss of neutral variation linked to deleterious mutations that purifying selection removes (Charlesworth et al. 1993; Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996). Background selection also acts on unlinked sites, where each locus has a negligible effect but their sum over the genome reduces diversity uniformly (Santiago and Caballero 1998; Charlesworth 2012); under mutation--selection balance this unlinked component is the genome-wide reduction of Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. Both are commonly grouped as linked selection, but they differ in what they require: the global effective size requires only $V_A$, whereas the local one also requires the process that maintains it, because how long a linked association lasts depends on how long the selected background persists. Maps of background selection along the genome sum the contributions of linked sites and absorb the uniform component into the reference level of diversity (McVicker et al. 2009). Santiago and Caballero (2016) and Buffalo and Kern (2024) expressed background selection in terms of the additive variance in fitness along the genome; Buffalo and Kern (2024) fitted this model to human diversity to estimate the equilibrium fitness variance and the distribution of fitness effects, and Buffalo and Coop (2019, 2020) estimated the contribution of linked selection from temporal covariance in allele frequencies in evolving \textit{Drosophila} populations. Across species, linked selection at the strongest published parameters does not account for the deficit in diversity (Buffalo 2021). In each case $V_A$ is inferred from genomic data rather than measured, and in the background-selection models it is the variance that mutation--selection balance would maintain.

To the best of our knowledge, pedigree estimates of $V_A$ have not been evaluated against either expectation: neither the reduction in diversity they imply nor the mutational input required to maintain them. The estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022) are on the scale both theories use, so the comparison needs no change of units, but it does need a statement about timescale. A pedigree estimate refers to the cohorts and environments sampled, since $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, and heritability in wild populations varies with environmental conditions (Charmantier and Garant 2005; Wilson et al. 2006). Both expectations refer instead to the long-term $V_A$: neutral diversity reflects effective size over the roughly $4N_e$ generations of a pairwise coalescence, with fluctuations entering as a harmonic mean (Wright 1938), and mutation--selection balance describes the stationary variance (Lynch and Hill 1986).

In this work, we evaluate the pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022) against both expectations, using one model of fitness for the estimate and the prediction: the multiplicative model on which the animal-model estimate rests. We keep the global and local effective sizes separate. We compute the global reduction implied by each estimate, which assumes nothing about how $V_A$ is maintained, at first order, extending Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} to separate sexes and social monogamy so that a single $V_A$ estimated across both sexes enters consistently (Santiago and Caballero 1995; Woolliams et al. 1999), and to all orders under the multiplicative model, where it is an upper bound on what a given $V_A$ can do. Inverting the latter gives the smallest $V_A$ that could account for a given deficit in diversity. We then partition the genome-wide effective size into this global component and a local component at linked sites, computed along empirical recombination maps using the background-selection kernel at the measured $V_A$ rather than at the variance that balance would maintain, obtain from measured mutation rates the mean selection coefficient required to maintain each estimate, and ask how far the long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate to satisfy each expectation. We find that the measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing, and too large to be maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance. At first order the global reduction is at most a few-fold. Even the multiplicative upper bound falls short of deficits of two to four orders of magnitude by factors of roughly a hundred to tens of thousands. Explaining them would require $V_A$ above one in every population, against measured values of at most one half. The local reduction adds little in mammals; in birds it can approach the deficit, but only for a long-term variance six to more than a hundred times the pedigree estimate and only under the purifying selection that this same variance is too large for. Maintenance would require a mean selection coefficient one to two orders of magnitude above vertebrate estimates. The two expectations therefore need long-term variances that differ by more than two orders of magnitude, so no single rescaling of the pedigree estimates satisfies both.
