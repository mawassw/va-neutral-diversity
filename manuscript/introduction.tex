The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, is the heritable component of variation in reproductive success among individuals~\cite{crow1958,robertson1961}. In wild populations, the pedigree-based animal model has been applied to lifetime breeding success to estimate $V_A$ in total fitness rather than in isolated life-history components~\cite{kruuk2004,postma2014}. By Fisher's fundamental theorem and the secondary theorem of selection~\cite{robertson1966,price1970}, a non-zero $V_A$ implies an ongoing increase in mean genetic fitness and directional selection on traits genetically correlated with fitness, without specifying which alleles selection acts upon or whether those changes are realized---and indeed, wild populations frequently exhibit less evolutionary change than classical models predict~\cite{merila2001,mawass2025contrast}. Recently, Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} meta-analyzed pedigree estimates across 19 long-term monitored populations of birds and mammals and found that $V_A$ is often substantial, which they interpreted as evidence of pervasive ongoing adaptation. At the allelic level, however, a variance of this magnitude raises two fundamental population-genetic questions: how much neutral diversity does it eliminate, and what evolutionary mechanism maintains it?

Across animal species, neutral genetic diversity varies within a remarkably narrow range despite spanning orders-of-magnitude differences in census population size (Lewontin's paradox~\cite{lewontin1974}), implying that effective population sizes ($N_e$) must lie far below census sizes (\claude{$N_c$}) in abundant taxa~\cite{leffler2012,buffalo2021,lewin2026}. Heritable variance in fitness is a primary candidate for this deficit. While stochastic variation in family size inherently generates genetic drift~\cite{wright1938}, heritability compounds this variation across generations: when an ancestor transmits its reproductive advantage to its offspring, the variance in long-term genetic contributions across lineages is inflated~\cite{robertson1961}. Drift at a neutral site is governed directly by this variance in ancestors' expected long-term contributions~\cite{wray1990}. Although theories of drift under selection compute this long-term contribution under different assumptions, all require selection pressures to remain constant across the brief span of generations over which an ancestor's advantage decays. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995,santiago1998} modeled this advantage as decaying by a constant fraction each generation, showing that at long-term equilibrium, the resulting drift reduces the ratio of effective size to \claude{the number of breeding adults, $N$,} to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium variance in relative fitness and $Q$ is Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, representing an ancestor's selective advantage summed across all generations in which it persists. Woolliams et al.~\cite{woolliams1993,woolliams1999} subsequently generalized this framework to accommodate multiple fitness components, including correlated reproductive success between mates.

Whereas these ancestor-centered models track the descendants of a focal individual, Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} recast the same process from the perspective of a tracked gene copy. In their framework, the probability that a neutral allele leaves descendants is governed by the reproductive value of the individuals carrying it, and selection on the genetic background---even at unlinked loci---can substantially inflate the variance of this reproductive value. A neutral allele shares its carrier's fitness advantage only while it remains statistically associated with the selected alleles transmitted by that carrier. From this gene-centered perspective, Robertson's $Q$ is precisely this association---formally, linkage disequilibrium (LD), whether between linked or unlinked sites~\cite{barton2000}---summed over the generations before it decays. At an unlinked site, independent assortment halves this LD each generation, causing the cumulative association $Q$ to converge to a geometric sum of $2$~\cite{santiago1998,charlesworth2012}. Here, the gene-centered and ancestor-centered perspectives coincide, as the per-generation halving of LD mirrors the Mendelian halving of an ancestor's expected breeding value. Consequently, every unlinked neutral site experiences its carrier's heritable fitness variance equally, producing a uniform, genome-wide reduction in $N_e$ governed by Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}. This linear expression provides a robust approximation for any smooth fitness landscape provided $V_A$ is small, so that the expected long-term contribution remains linear in the breeding value. When individual loci act multiplicatively on fitness, however, selective advantages sum on a logarithmic scale, making the expected long-term contribution exponential in the breeding value. Under this multiplicative architecture, heritable variance reduces unlinked $N_e$ by a factor of $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating and $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy~\cite{weissman2012}. 

At physically linked sites, this reduction in $N_e$ shifts from a uniform global baseline to a heterogeneous local topography. Because LD decays at a rate set by the recombination fraction, a neutral site's effective size is governed by the selective landscape of its chromosomal neighborhood. This localized reduction can be vastly stronger than the unlinked baseline: tightly linked sites recombine rarely, allowing their associations to outlast the rapid halving of unlinked loci and driving the cumulative association well above $2$ whenever purifying selection is slow to purge the selected variants. Recent empirical studies have leveraged this local topography---primarily within the framework of background selection---to infer fitness variance or the strength of purifying selection directly from genomic diversity landscapes or temporal allele-frequency covariances~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020,murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}. Predicting diversity at this local resolution, however, requires fine-scale recombination maps. For most wild vertebrates, pedigree linkage maps resolve whole-chromosome genetic lengths far more reliably than fine-scale recombination rate variation, limiting the direct application of high-resolution local models.

Because a linked association persists only until the selected allele is eliminated or fixed, the severity of the local reduction in $N_e$ depends directly on the evolutionary mechanism maintaining $V_A$~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996,li2026}. Mutation--selection--drift balance (MSDB) is the classical null model for this maintenance~\cite{haldane1937,crow1970}. When purifying selection is strong relative to genetic drift, the standing variance maintained at MSDB is approximately
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ is the arithmetic mean of the distribution of deleterious fitness effects (DFE)~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2015}. Empirical estimates of fitness variance, however, routinely exceed this mutational baseline. Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} demonstrated that standing genetic variance in \textit{Drosophila} fitness components exceeds MSDB expectations by more than an order of magnitude, pointing toward balancing selection, fluctuating selection, or other non-equilibrium forces~\cite{johnson2005,charlesworth2022}. Similarly, Connallon and Czuppon~\cite{connallon2026} argued qualitatively that vertebrate deleterious mutation rates are too low to sustain the large $V_A$ estimates reported by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, though without directly testing those population estimates against empirical parameterizations of $U_d\bar{s}$. Because both $U_d$ and $\bar{s}$ can now be constrained from comparative and population-genomic data~\cite{eyrewalker2007,huber2017,bergeron2023,lin2026}, the exact fraction of empirical $V_A$ that recurrent mutation can sustain can be tested quantitatively in each population. 

If wild populations truly harbor fitness variance well above MSDB expectations, that excess variance must also depress $N_e$ at unlinked sites across the genome---yet studies of the diversity deficit have overwhelmingly focused on linked effects. When $V_A$ is maintained strictly by MSDB, Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} recovers the classical unlinked limit of background selection~\cite{charlesworth2012}. Historically, this unlinked effect was considered negligible compared to linked background selection because unlinked associations decay within a few generations~\cite{charlesworth2012,matheson2024}. Matheson and Masel~\cite{matheson2024}, however, recently demonstrated that in genomes with vertebrate-like recombination maps, the unlinked reduction overtakes the linked effect whenever $U_d > 1$. Crucially, evaluating this global unlinked reduction via Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} ($Q = 2$) bypasses the main obstacles of local linked models: it requires neither the assumption that $V_A$ is maintained at MSDB ($U_d \bar{s}$) nor a fine-scale recombination map, relying solely on the pedigree estimate of $V_A$ and the mating system. While Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} applied this unlinked approximation to the cross-species mean estimate of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}---predicting a $16\%$ to $55\%$ reduction in neutral diversity---no study has yet evaluated how much of the diversity deficit this unlinked reduction explains in individual wild populations, nor how it compares alongside chromosome-scale linked selection.

Both theoretical frameworks---drift under heritable fitness variance and maintenance at MSDB---are parameterized by the additive genetic variance in relative fitness, the exact quantity estimated by Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}. Yet a contemporary pedigree estimate is not the long-term parameter either equilibrium model requires. Pedigree studies span only a few decades of specific cohorts and environmental regimes, across which heritability can fluctuate substantially~\cite{charmantier2005,wilson2006,mawass2022}. Neutral genetic diversity, by contrast, reflects an effective population size integrated over the roughly $4N_e$ generations required for lineages to coalesce, demanding the long-term historical average of $V_A$. Conversely, because MSDB describes a stationary variance at equilibrium, testing a population against MSDB directly evaluates the stationarity assumption upon which equilibrium diversity predictions rely. Whether a population satisfies either model therefore hinges on how its contemporary pedigree estimate relates to its long-term historical variance. Evaluating the same populations with both equilibrium tests simultaneously reveals whether any single stationary variance can reconcile the two models, or whether non-equilibrium dynamics are required to explain the contemporary fitness variance, the diversity deficit, or both.

Here we test the 19 pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} against both equilibrium models, applying the maintenance test to 18 populations and the diversity test to 9. We first generalize the reduction at unlinked sites by allowing extra-pair paternity, which places socially monogamous populations on a continuum between strict monogamy and random mating~\cite{santiago1995,weissman2012,woolliams1999}. We then incorporate linked sites using only the map length of each chromosome. Finally, we ask how far the true long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate to pass each test. Because a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, any deviation from the pedigree estimate narrows one discrepancy only to widen the other. We find that the measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the missing neutral diversity, and too large to be maintained at MSDB. Under multiplicative effects, heritable variance in fitness roughly halves the effective size of the median population at unlinked sites. In contrast, observed diversity lies hundreds to tens of thousands of times below its neutral expectation. Bridging this deficit would require a long-term variance above one---larger than any empirical estimate---and linkage fails to resolve this gap in any population. Because the long-term variances required by the two tests differ by more than a hundredfold in every population with diversity data, no single rescaling of the pedigree estimates passes both tests as the long-term variance.
