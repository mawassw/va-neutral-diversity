The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, is the heritable component of variation in reproductive success~\cite{crow1958,robertson1961}, and pedigree-based animal models fitted to lifetime breeding success now estimate it for total fitness in wild populations~\cite{kruuk2004,postma2014}. By Fisher's fundamental theorem and the secondary theorem of selection~\cite{robertson1966,price1970}, a non-zero $V_A$ implies an ongoing increase in mean genetic fitness and directional selection on traits genetically correlated with fitness, without specifying which alleles respond. Yet wild populations often change less than these theorems predict~\cite{merila2001,mawass2025contrast}. Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} meta-analyzed pedigree estimates from 19 long-term studies of birds and mammals and found that $V_A$ is often substantial, which they interpreted as evidence of contemporary adaptive evolution. Such a variance, however, is measured over a few generations, whereas its two population-genetic consequences are long-term. Heritable variance in fitness reduces neutral diversity, and selection depletes that variance unless some process maintains it. How much of these populations' missing neutral diversity does the measured variance account for, and how much of that variance can recurrent mutation maintain?

The effective population size ($N_e$) implied by a population's neutral genetic diversity and mutation rate is smaller than its census size ($N_c$), often by several orders of magnitude~\cite{buffalo2021,lewin2026}. This deficit grows with abundance, so that across animal species genetic diversity varies over a much narrower range than census size (Lewontin's paradox~\cite{lewontin1974,leffler2012}). Heritable variance in fitness is one of the reasons $N_e$ is below the census size, and theory predicts the reduction in $N_e$ from that variance alone, whatever the genetic architecture~\cite{robertson1961,santiago1995}. Random variation in family size causes drift on its own~\cite{wright1938}. Heritable variance in fitness adds to that drift because an ancestor's advantage passes to its descendants, which increases the variance among ancestors in long-term genetic contribution~\cite{robertson1961}, the quantity that sets drift at a neutral site~\cite{wray1990}. Theories of drift under selection differ in how they compute the long-term contribution, but all assume that selection remains constant over the few generations in which an ancestor's advantage decays. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995,santiago1998} modeled the advantage as decaying by a constant fraction each generation; assuming Poisson non-heritable variance in family size, the equilibrium ratio of effective size to the number of breeding adults, $N$, at an unlinked locus is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium additive genetic variance in relative fitness and $Q$, Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, sums over generations the fraction of an ancestor's selective advantage that persists in its descendants. Woolliams et al.~\cite{woolliams1993,woolliams1999} generalized this framework to multiple fitness components, including correlated reproductive success of mates.

Whereas these ancestor-centered models follow the descendants of an individual, Barton and Etheridge~\cite{barton2011} followed a gene copy, which shares its carrier's advantage only while it stays associated with the selected alleles the carrier transmits. That association is the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between a neutral site and the selected sites, which arises whether or not the sites are linked~\cite{barton2000}. From this gene-centered perspective, Robertson's $Q$ is this LD summed over the generations before it decays. At an unlinked site, independent assortment halves the LD each generation, so $Q = 2$~\cite{santiago1998,charlesworth2012}. The two perspectives coincide there because LD and the share of an ancestor's breeding value carried by its descendants are both halved each generation. Consequently, $N_e$ is reduced by the same amount at every unlinked neutral site, and Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} gives that reduction provided $V_A$ is small. When loci act multiplicatively on fitness, the long-term contribution becomes exponential in the breeding value, and heritable variance in fitness multiplies $N_e$ at unlinked sites by $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating and by $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy~\cite{weissman2012}. At a linked site, by contrast, the association lasts until a rare crossover breaks it or selection removes the allele, so the reduction varies along the genome and, where that removal is slow, can be much stronger than the unlinked reduction~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996}. Studies of this variation, mostly under models of background selection, have inferred heritable variance in fitness or the strength of purifying selection from diversity along human chromosomes or from temporal allele-frequency covariances~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020,murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}.

Because a linked association can last as long as the selected allele segregates, the reduction in $N_e$ at linked sites depends on the mechanism that maintains $V_A$. Mutation--selection--drift balance (MSDB) is the classical null model for this maintenance~\cite{haldane1937,crow1970}. When purifying selection is strong relative to drift, MSDB maintains an additive genetic variance in fitness of approximately
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ is the arithmetic mean of the distribution of deleterious fitness effects (DFE)~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2015}. Measured variance in fitness, however, is often larger. Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} showed that standing genetic variance in \textit{Drosophila} fitness components exceeds the MSDB expectation by more than an order of magnitude, which implies a contribution from balancing selection, fluctuating selection, or departures from equilibrium~\cite{johnson2005,charlesworth2022}. Similarly, Connallon and Czuppon~\cite{connallon2026} argued qualitatively that vertebrate deleterious mutation rates are too low to sustain the estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, without testing those estimates against measured $U_d\bar{s}$. Because $U_d$ and $\bar{s}$ can now be estimated from comparative and population-genomic data~\cite{eyrewalker2007,huber2017,bergeron2023,lin2026}, the share of each population's $V_A$ that recurrent mutation maintains can be measured.

Whatever maintains it, heritable variance in fitness reduces $N_e$ at unlinked sites, yet studies of the diversity deficit have focused on linked effects. At MSDB, this unlinked reduction is the free-recombination limit of background selection, long considered negligible because unlinked associations decay within a few generations~\cite{charlesworth2012}. However, Matheson and Masel~\cite{matheson2024} showed that with vertebrate-like recombination maps, the unlinked reduction is larger than the linked one whenever $U_d$ exceeds 1. The unlinked reduction can also be predicted from a pedigree estimate of $V_A$ and the mating system, without a fine-scale recombination map and without assuming that $V_A$ is maintained at MSDB. Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} applied this prediction to the cross-species mean estimate of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} and obtained a $16\%$ to $55\%$ reduction in neutral diversity. A reduction of that size is far smaller than the deficits of Lewontin's paradox, so heritable variance in fitness is not expected to explain them. What has not been estimated is the size of the reduction in each population, given its own estimate of $V_A$, mating system, and linkage map.

Both the diversity prediction and MSDB concern the long-term additive genetic variance in relative fitness, a parameter that a contemporary pedigree estimate~\cite{bonnet2022} does not directly measure. Pedigree studies span a few decades of particular cohorts and environments, over which additive genetic variance and heritability can fluctuate~\cite{charmantier2005,wilson2006,mawass2022}. Neutral diversity, by contrast, reflects an effective size averaged over the roughly $2N_e$ generations that pairs of lineages take to coalesce, and so depends on a $V_A$ sustained over that span. Because MSDB describes a stationary variance, testing a population against MSDB also tests the stationarity on which equilibrium diversity predictions rely. By applying both tests to the same populations, we therefore ask whether a single stationary variance can satisfy both models, or whether departures from equilibrium are required to explain the heritable variance in fitness, the diversity deficit, or both.

Here we test the pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} against both equilibrium models, 18 of the 19 for maintenance at MSDB and the 9 with diversity and census data for neutral diversity, allowing for the mating system, including extra-pair paternity, and for linkage along chromosomes. Under multiplicative fitness effects, the predicted unlinked reduction roughly halves the effective size in the median population. Among the 9 populations with diversity data, it accounts for a median of 7\% of the deficit on a logarithmic scale. Explaining the observed deficits, which range from 230- to 38,000-fold, would require a long-term $V_A > 1$, larger than any empirical estimate, and including linkage does not resolve the shortfall in any population. Recurrent deleterious mutation maintains only about 5\% of the measured $V_A$ in the median population. Because the long-term variances required by the two equilibria differ by more than a hundredfold in every population with diversity data, no single stationary variance satisfies both. That gap depends on each population's mutational input, mating system, and observed deficit, and not on its pedigree estimate.
