The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A(w)$, is the heritable component of variation in reproductive success~\cite{crow1958,robertson1961}, and pedigree-based animal models fitted to lifetime breeding success now estimate it for total fitness in wild populations~\cite{kruuk2004,postma2014}. By Fisher's fundamental theorem and the secondary theorem of selection~\cite{robertson1966,price1970}, a non-zero $V_A$ implies an ongoing increase in mean genetic fitness and directional selection on traits genetically correlated with fitness, without specifying which alleles respond—though wild populations often change less than these theorems predict~\cite{merila2001,mawass2025contrast}. Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} meta-analyzed pedigree estimates from 19 long-term studies of birds and mammals and found that $V_A$ is often substantial, which they interpreted as evidence of contemporary adaptive evolution. Such a variance, however, is measured over a few generations, whereas its two population-genetic consequences are long-term. Heritable variance in fitness reduces neutral diversity, and selection depletes it unless some process maintains it. How much of the diversity these populations lack does the measured variance account for, and how much of the variance can recurrent mutation maintain?

The effective population size ($N_e$) implied by a population's neutral genetic diversity and mutation rate is typically far smaller than its census size ($N_c$), often by several orders of magnitude~\cite{buffalo2021,lewin2026}. This deficit grows with abundance, so that across animal species genetic diversity varies over a much narrower range than census size (Lewontin's paradox~\cite{lewontin1974,leffler2012}). Heritable variance in fitness is one of the processes that lower $N_e$ below the census size, and the theory of drift under selection predicts its genome-wide effect from the variance, whatever loci carry it~\cite{robertson1961,santiago1995}. Random variation in family size alone causes drift~\cite{wright1938}. Heritability compounds it, because an ancestor that transmits its reproductive advantage to its offspring inflates the variance in long-term genetic contributions among lineages~\cite{robertson1961}, and this variance is what sets drift experienced at a neutral site~\cite{wray1990}. Standard theories of drift under heritable fitness variation compute the long-term genetic contribution under different assumptions, but all assume that selection remains constant over the few generations in which an ancestor's advantage decays. Santiago and Caballero~\cite{santiago1995,santiago1998} modeled the advantage as decaying by a constant fraction each generation; assuming Poisson non-heritable variance in family size, the equilibrium ratio of effective size to the number of breeding adults, $N$, at an unlinked locus is 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium additive genetic variance in relative fitness and $Q$, Robertson's cumulative transmission factor, sums the fractional persistence of an ancestor's selective advantage across generations. Woolliams et al.~\cite{woolliams1993,woolliams1999} generalized this framework to multiple fitness components, including correlated reproductive success of mates. 

\claude{Whereas these ancestor-centered models follow the descendants of an individual, Barton and Etheridge}~\cite{barton2011}\claude{ followed a gene copy, which shares its carrier's advantage only while it stays associated with the selected alleles the carrier transmits. From this gene-centered perspective, Robertson's $Q$ is that association, the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between neutral and selected sites, linked or unlinked}~\cite{barton2000}\claude{, summed over the generations before it decays. At an unlinked site, independent assortment halves the LD each generation, so $Q = 2$}~\cite{santiago1998,charlesworth2012}\claude{. The two perspectives coincide there, because the halving of LD mirrors the Mendelian halving of an ancestor's expected breeding value, so every unlinked neutral site experiences the same genome-wide reduction in $N_e$, given by Equation}~\eqref{eq:intro_sc}\claude{ while $V_A$ is small. When loci act multiplicatively on fitness, the long-term contribution becomes exponential in the breeding value, and heritable variance reduces unlinked $N_e$ by a factor of $e^{-4V_A}$ under random mating and $e^{-8V_A}$ under strict monogamy}~\cite{weissman2012}\claude{. At a linked site, the association lasts instead until a rare crossover breaks it or selection removes the allele, so the reduction varies along the genome and can be much stronger than the unlinked one where purging is slow}~\cite{hudson1995,nordborg1996}\claude{. Studies of this variation, mostly within the framework of background selection, have inferred fitness variance or the strength of purifying selection from diversity along human chromosomes or from temporal allele-frequency covariances}~\cite{buffalo2019,buffalo2020,murphy2023,buffaloKern2024}\claude{.}

Because a linked association lasts only until the selected allele is lost or fixed, the \claude{reduction in $N_e$ at linked sites} depends on the mechanism that maintains $V_A$. Mutation--selection--drift balance (MSDB) is the classical null model for this maintenance~\cite{haldane1937,crow1970}. When purifying selection is strong relative to drift, MSDB maintains an additive genetic variance in fitness of approximately
\begin{equation}
    V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}, \label{eq:intro_mukai}
\end{equation}
where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $\bar{s}$ is the arithmetic mean of the distribution of deleterious fitness effects (DFE)~\cite{mukai1972,charlesworth2015}. Estimates of fitness variance, however, often exceed this baseline. Charlesworth~\cite{charlesworth2015} showed that standing genetic variance in \textit{Drosophila} fitness components exceeds the MSDB expectation by more than an order of magnitude, pointing toward balancing selection, fluctuating selection, or departures from equilibrium~\cite{johnson2005,charlesworth2022}. Similarly, Connallon and Czuppon~\cite{connallon2026} argued qualitatively that vertebrate deleterious mutation rates are too low to sustain the estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, without explicitly testing those estimates against measures of $U_d\bar{s}$. Because $U_d$ and $\bar{s}$ can now be estimated from comparative and population-genomic data~\cite{eyrewalker2007,huber2017,bergeron2023,lin2026}, the share of each population's $V_A$ that recurrent mutation maintains can be measured.

Fitness variance above the MSDB expectation must also reduce $N_e$ at unlinked sites, yet studies of the diversity deficit have focused on linked effects. At MSDB, this unlinked reduction is the free-recombination limit of background selection, long considered negligible because unlinked associations decay within a few generations~\cite{charlesworth2012}. However, Matheson and Masel~\cite{matheson2024} showed that with vertebrate-like recombination maps, the unlinked reduction exceeds the linked one whenever the genome-wide deleterious mutation rate $U_d > 1$. Moreover, this unlinked baseline requires neither the assumption that $V_A$ is maintained at MSDB nor a fine-scale recombination map—only a pedigree estimate of $V_A$ and the mating system. Charlesworth and Jensen~\cite{charlesworth2022} applied this unlinked approximation to the cross-species mean estimate of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022}, predicting a $16\%$ to $55\%$ reduction in neutral diversity. A reduction of that size is far smaller than the deficits of Lewontin's paradox, so heritable variance in fitness is not expected to explain them. What remains to be measured is the size of its contribution within each population, with that population's own estimate, mating system and linkage map, or how much of the variance recurrent mutation maintains.

Both equilibria require the long-term additive genetic variance in relative fitness, a parameter that a contemporary pedigree estimate~\cite{bonnet2022} does not directly measure.  Pedigree studies span a few decades of particular cohorts and environments, over which additive genetic variance and heritability can fluctuate substantially~\cite{charmantier2005,wilson2006,mawass2022}. Neutral diversity, by contrast, reflects an effective size averaged over the roughly $2N_e$ generations that pairs of lineages take to coalesce, and so depends $V_A$ sustained over evolutionary timescales. Because MSDB describes a stationary variance, testing a population against MSDB also tests the stationarity on which equilibrium diversity predictions rely. Applying both tests to the same populations therefore asks whether a single stationary variance can satisfy both models, or whether departures from equilibrium are required to explain the fitness variance, the diversity deficit, or both.

Here we test the 19 pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al.~\cite{bonnet2022} against both equilibrium models, applying the maintenance test to 18 populations and the diversity test to 9, and allow for extra-pair paternity, which places socially monogamous populations between strict monogamy and random mating~\cite{santiago1995,weissman2012,woolliams1999}, and for linkage, using only the map length of each chromosome. We ask how far the long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate for recurrent mutation to maintain it, and how far for it to account for the missing diversity. Because a larger long-term variance removes more diversity but is harder to maintain, any deviation from the pedigree estimate narrows one discrepancy only to widen the other. We find that each equilibrium accounts for a small share of what it is meant to explain. Under multiplicative effects, heritable fitness variance roughly halves the unlinked effective size of the median population, whereas observed diversity falls two to four orders of magnitude below its neutral expectation. Because independent reductions in $N_e$ multiply, this unlinked factor accounts for a median of 7\% of the log-diversity deficit.  Explaining the deficits would require a long-term variance above one, larger than any empirical estimate, and linkage does not resolve the shortfall in any population. At measured mutation rates, with the annotated functional sequence as the deleterious target and mean deleterious effects assigned from related species, recurrent mutation maintains about a twentieth of the measured additive fitness variance in the median population. Because the long-term variances that the two equilibria require differ by more than a hundredfold in every population with diversity data, no single long-term variance is both small enough for mutation to maintain and large enough to account for the missing diversity, a gap set by the variance mutation maintains, the mating system and the observed deficit rather than by the pedigree estimates.
