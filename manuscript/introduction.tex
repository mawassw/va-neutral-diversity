The additive genetic variance in relative fitness, $V_A$, measures the heritable component of variation in fitness and is central to theories of adaptation, of the maintenance of genetic variation, and of effective population size (Crow 1958; Robertson 1961; Charlesworth 2015). In wild populations, $V_A$ is estimated with the pedigree animal model, which separates additive genetic variance from the environmental and maternal similarity of relatives (Henderson 1975; Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010) and, applied to lifetime breeding success, estimates $V_A$ directly rather than through a fitness component (Postma 2014). Bonnet et al. (2022) applied it to 19 populations of birds and mammals, including Soay sheep on St Kilda and great tits in Wytham Woods, and reported substantial $V_A$ in many of them, expressed on the observed scale of relative fitness as $V_A(w) = \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{d}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ is the latent-scale additive genetic covariance matrix and $\mathbf{d}$ the vector of average derivatives of mean relative fitness with respect to the latent predictors.

Standing $V_A$ requires a model of how it is maintained across generations. The classical model is a balance between recurrent deleterious mutation and purifying selection. New mutations contribute additive variance at rate $V_m = U_d s^2$, where $U_d$ is the diploid genome-wide deleterious mutation rate and $s$ the heterozygous selection coefficient, and when selection is strong relative to drift ($2N_e s \gg 1$) the equilibrium variance is $V_A \approx U_d \bar{s}$ (Mukai et al. 1972; Crow 1970; Charlesworth 2015). Charlesworth (2015) compared this expectation with the genetic variance in fitness components of \textit{Drosophila} and concluded that mutation--selection balance accounts for only part of it, with balancing selection contributing substantially (see also Johnson and Barton 2005). Sharp and Agrawal (2018) tested it experimentally with mutation-accumulation lines and found it sufficient for larval viability but not for male mating success or female fecundity. Connallon and Czuppon (2026) showed that drift and fluctuating selection allow balancing selection to maintain substantial $V_A$, noting that deleterious mutation rates appear too low to account for the wild-vertebrate estimates. That comparison was qualitative, although both inputs are now measured in vertebrates: $U_d$ from mutation rates estimated by pedigree sequencing (Bergeron et al. 2023), and $\bar{s}$ from the distribution of fitness effects (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Huber et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2026).

Second, heritable variance in fitness reduces neutral diversity. Diversity scales with effective size, $\pi = 4N_e\mu$, and across species it is orders of magnitude lower than census size predicts (Leffler et al. 2012; Lewin and Eyre-Walker 2026), the observation behind Lewontin's paradox (Lewontin 1974). Any variance in family size increases the rate of drift (Wright 1938). When part of that variance is additive, an individual's advantage passes to its offspring and, halved by segregation, to theirs in turn, so the descendants of a favored ancestor remain over-represented for several generations, and drift at neutral sites exceeds what the same variance in family size would cause if it were not inherited (Robertson 1961). Santiago and Caballero (1995, 1998) showed that at long-term equilibrium
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_e}{N} = \frac{1}{1 + Q^2 V_A}, \label{eq:intro_sc}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of breeding adults, $V_A$ is the long-term equilibrium additive variance in relative fitness (their $C^2$ in the absence of a pair bond), and $Q$, the cumulative transmission factor of Robertson (1961), is the association between a neutral site and its selected background summed over the generations in which it persists. Recombination and the loss of the selected alleles both erode the association, so $Q$ depends on how tightly the neutral site is linked to the selected loci and on how long those alleles persist. At a site unlinked to every selected locus, segregation halves the association each generation, and $Q = 2$ unless the selected alleles are purged faster than that (Santiago and Caballero 1998; Charlesworth 2012). This unlinked reduction is shared by every neutral site in the genome and so sets a global effective size. At $Q = 2$ it requires nothing but $V_A$: no model of how the variance is maintained and no map of the genome, so a pedigree estimate of $V_A$ is all that is needed to predict it. Yet the global reduction has largely been left out of accounts of the diversity deficit (Matheson and Masel 2025), and maps of background selection absorb it into their reference level of diversity (McVicker et al. 2009).

Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} is a leading-order approximation. It treats the advantage of each lineage as small, and so holds while $V_A \ll 1$, for any map from breeding value to fitness. A deficit of orders of magnitude lies outside that range, so asking what variance could produce one extrapolates the equation rather than applying it. Barton and Etheridge (2011) treated the problem at every order for one map: fitness proportional to $e^{z}$, with $z$ an additive, Gaussian breeding value for log-fitness, in a population whose parents are chosen independently, without separate sexes or pair bonds. The expected long-term contribution of an ancestor is then exponential in its breeding value and varies across ancestors as $e^{4V_A}$, and the probability of coalescence goes as its square, so their result implies $N_e/N = e^{-4V_A}$. The two forms agree while $V_A$ is small and diverge as it grows, the exponential giving the larger reduction. It does so by drawing on ancestors in the far upper tail of log-fitness, a tail that any limit on fitness, such as territory or clutch size, would cut off. The first-order form has the complementary strength: it has been extended to separate sexes, mating systems, linkage and purging (Santiago and Caballero 1995, 1998; Woolliams et al. 1999). Pedigree estimates sit on the scale of the exponential form. The animal models are fitted with a log link, and the variance they report is the variance of $z$ (de Villemereuil et al. 2016), so one model of fitness can serve for both the estimate and the prediction. At every order, however, that model has been solved only without pair bonds, whereas many of the populations in which $V_A$ has been measured are socially monogamous.

At linked sites the reduction is stronger, and it depends on more than $V_A$. Associations with selected loci on the same chromosome are broken only as often as the two sites recombine, so they persist for many generations and depress a local effective size that varies along the genome. How long they persist depends on how long the selected alleles do, and so on the process that maintains $V_A$ (Li and Berg 2026). Under mutation--selection balance the local reduction is background selection, the loss of neutral variation linked to deleterious mutations that purifying selection removes (Charlesworth et al. 1993; Hudson and Kaplan 1995; Nordborg et al. 1996). Santiago and Caballero (2016) and Buffalo and Kern (2024) expressed background selection in terms of the additive variance in fitness. Buffalo and Kern evaluated it at each position of the human genome from every selected segment's recombination distance to it, using fine-scale recombination maps and annotations of functional sequence, and fitted this map of expected diversity to observed diversity to estimate the equilibrium fitness variance and the distribution of fitness effects. Buffalo and Coop (2019, 2020) estimated the contribution of linked selection from temporal covariance in allele frequencies in evolving \textit{Drosophila} populations, and across species, linked selection at the strongest published parameters does not account for the deficit in diversity (Buffalo 2021). In each case $V_A$ is inferred from genomic data rather than measured, and in background-selection models it is the variance that mutation--selection balance would maintain. A prediction made position by position also needs recombination maps resolved within chromosomes. These exist for humans and \textit{Drosophila}, but for most wild vertebrates the available maps are pedigree linkage maps, sometimes from a related species, which measure the map length of each chromosome far better than the variation in recombination rate along it.

To the best of our knowledge, pedigree estimates of $V_A$ have not been evaluated against either expectation: neither the reduction in diversity they imply nor the mutational input required to maintain them. The estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022) are on the scale both theories use, so the comparison needs no change of units, but it does need a statement about timescale. A pedigree estimate refers to the cohorts and environments sampled, since $\mathbf{d}$ is evaluated at the observed state of the population, and heritability in wild populations varies with environmental conditions (Charmantier and Garant 2005; Wilson et al. 2006). Both expectations refer instead to the long-term $V_A$: neutral diversity reflects effective size over the roughly $4N_e$ generations of a pairwise coalescence, with fluctuations entering as a harmonic mean (Wright 1938), and mutation--selection balance describes the stationary variance (Lynch and Hill 1986).

In this work, we evaluate the pedigree estimates of Bonnet et al. (2022) against both expectations, using one model of fitness for the estimate and the prediction: the multiplicative model on which the animal-model estimate rests. We keep the global and local effective sizes separate. We compute the global reduction implied by each estimate, which assumes nothing about how $V_A$ is maintained, at first order, extending Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_sc} to separate sexes and social monogamy so that a single $V_A$ estimated across both sexes enters consistently (Santiago and Caballero 1995; Woolliams et al. 1999), and to all orders under the multiplicative model, with and without a pair bond, where it is an upper bound on what a given $V_A$ can do. Inverting the latter gives the smallest $V_A$ that could account for a given deficit in diversity. We then partition the genome-wide effective size into this global component and a local component at linked sites, computed from the map length of each chromosome by averaging the background-selection kernel over the positions of neutral sites and selected loci along it, at the measured $V_A$ rather than at the variance that balance would maintain, obtain from measured mutation rates the mean selection coefficient required to maintain each estimate, and ask how far the long-term variance would have to differ from the pedigree estimate to satisfy each expectation. We find that the measured $V_A$ is too small to explain the neutral diversity these populations are missing, and too large to be maintained at mutation--selection--drift balance. At first order the global reduction is at most a few-fold. Even the multiplicative upper bound falls short of deficits of two to four orders of magnitude by factors of roughly a hundred to tens of thousands. Explaining them would require $V_A$ above one in every population, against measured values of at most one half. The local reduction adds little in mammals; in birds it can approach the deficit, but only for a long-term variance six to more than a hundred times the pedigree estimate and only under the purifying selection that this same variance is too large for. Maintenance would require a mean selection coefficient one to two orders of magnitude above vertebrate estimates. The two expectations therefore need long-term variances that differ by more than two orders of magnitude, so no single rescaling of the pedigree estimates satisfies both.
